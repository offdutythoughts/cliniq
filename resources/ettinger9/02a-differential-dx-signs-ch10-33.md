### Ch 10 — "Ain't Doin' Right" / Ill Thrift

**Definition:** Ill thrift is a vague, poorly defined decrease in vitality — a non-specific change in activity level, mental arousal, or behavior. Not a discrete sign; the hallmark is its non-specific nature.

**Key initial considerations:**
- Any organ system disorder can cause ill thrift
- Wide variability in severity of underlying cause (trivial to life-threatening)
- First step: review signalment and medical history

**Signalment clues:**
- Young animals: congenital malformations, parasitoses, trauma, foreign bodies/substances
- Mature dogs/cats: degenerative diseases, neoplasia
- Large-breed dogs: higher prevalence osteosarcoma, splenic/cardiac neoplasia
- Small-breed dogs: myxomatous mitral valve disease, chronic pancreatitis
- Chondrodystrophic breeds: intervertebral disc disease
- Sex: pyometra, prostatitis (sex-specific); immune-mediated polyarthritis (higher in females)

**Serious vs. nonserious triage:**

*Serious case* — ANY of:
- Hypovolemia/hemodynamic instability (poor perfusion, weak/absent pulse, pathological arrhythmia, dehydration)
- Neurologic compromise (depressed mentation, locomotor deficits, generalized weakness)
- Respiratory difficulty (dyspnea, inappropriate tachypnea)
- Persistent pain (mentation change, subdued behavior, guarding)

*Nonserious case* — normal or insignificantly abnormal physical exam findings for all body systems.

**Diagnostic approach:**

*Nonserious cases — first tier:*
- Packed cell volume and total solids
- Blood glucose
- BUN estimation (e.g., Azostix)
- Urine specific gravity
- Urine dipstick profile
- (Often feasible from same samples): CBC, serum biochemistry, complete UA
- USG >1.035 indicates concentrating capacity; <1.035 warrants further investigation
- Adult cats: consider total thyroxine; retroviral testing for all cats
- Heartworm serology in endemic areas
- Thoracic radiographs and abdominal ultrasound if structural lesion suspected

*Serious cases — second tier:*
- As above, plus investigation of abnormalities found on first-tier tests

**Normal water intake thresholds:**
- Dogs: <100 mL/kg/24 hr (usually <80 mL/kg/24 hr)
- Cats: <200 mL/cat/24 hr or <50 mL/kg/day

**"Ill thrift not a sign of disease" — differential:**
- Client overinterpretation secondary to anxiety, environment change, loss of another pet
- Normal aging-associated activity decrease
- Normal interindividual variation

### Ch 11 — Distinguishing Behavioral Disorders From Medical Disorders

**Oral repetitive behaviors with GI etiology (key findings):**
- Excessive surface-licking: 14/19 dogs (74%) had GI disorders (eosinophilic/lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, delayed gastric emptying, IBS, chronic pancreatitis, giardiasis)
- Fly-biting: 7/7 dogs had underlying GI disease (gastric/duodenal eosinophilic or lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, delayed gastric emptying, gastroesophageal reflux); resolved in 5/6 with GI treatment
- Pica: may be secondary to IBD — GI biopsies should always be taken when operating for GI foreign body removal in "repeat offenders"
- Flank sucking (Dobermans), checking behaviors (Miniature Schnauzers): include GI disease in DDx
- Psychogenic alopecia in cats: 16/21 (76%) had underlying medical cause

**Behavioral disorder clues — age of onset:**
- Young animals (6–18 months) typical for behavioral disorders
- Sudden appearance of new behaviors in middle-aged or older animals: suspect underlying medical condition
- Dogs: impulse-control aggression at 18–24 months; cats: intercat aggression 2–4 years

**Subtle GI signs to ask about specifically:**
- Flatulence, borborygmi, eructation, neck extension/stretching, lip licking, tongue flicking, repetitive swallowing, drooling

### Ch 12 — Dermatologic Manifestations of Systemic Disease

**Approach:** Skin is the largest, most visible organ; dermatologic signs often guide diagnosis of systemic disease. Diascopy (glass slide pressure) differentiates blanching erythema from non-blanching petechiae/ecchymoses.

**SKIN COLOR CHANGES**

**Erythema (redness):**
- Vasodilation or erythrocyte extravasation (petechiae/ecchymoses)
- Darier's sign (rubbing elicits urticarial wheal): consistent with mast cell tumor, systemic mastocytosis, urticaria pigmentosa
- Cutaneous flushing (episodic): pheochromocytoma (ch. 298)
- Localized/multifocal: allergies, ectoparasites, infections, immune-mediated disease, adverse drug reactions (erythema multiforme), neoplasia (mast cell tumors, cutaneous lymphoma), trauma
- Petechiae/ecchymoses: vasculitis, thrombocytopenia, immune-mediated disease, ehrlichiosis, Rocky Mountain spotted fever

**Pallor/hypopigmentation:**
- Severe anemia or inadequate perfusion (hypovolemia, cardiac failure)
- Vitiligo; cutaneous lupus; uveodermatological disease; hypoadrenocorticism; cutaneous lymphoma
- Chédiak-Higashi syndrome: blue smoke Persian cats — abnormal granulocytes/platelets, recurrent infections/hemorrhage
- Canine cyclic hematopoiesis: gray coat + light nose in Collies — autosomal recessive, bone marrow stem cell dysfunction

**Yellow skin:** Jaundice (bilirubin accumulation — liver disease or hemolysis); carotenoid pigment accumulation

**Black/blue lesions:** Hyperpigmentation (post-inflammatory); pigmented viral plaques (papillomavirus → may progress to SCC); melanomas; cutaneous hemangiomas; apocrine gland cysts/tumors; cyanosis (reduced oxygenation)

**ALOPECIA — systemic causes:**

- **Hypothyroidism:** Hair loss begins nose bridge, ear pinnae, tail, elbows; thyroid hormones necessary for anagen phase initiation
- **Hyperadrenocorticism:** Thin dull coat → thin skin → hyperpigmentation, bruises, phlebectasias, striae, poor wound healing, secondary infections, calcinosis cutis
- **Hyperestrogenism (female dogs):** Polycystic ovaries or ovarian neoplasia → bilaterally symmetric alopecia, enlarged vulva/nipples, abnormal estrous cycles
- **Hyperestrogenism (male dogs):** Sertoli cell tumor (especially cryptorchid) → alopecia, nipple enlargement, pendulous prepuce, ventral hyperpigmentation, attraction of other males, atrophy of non-neoplastic testicle; linear preputial dermatosis (erythematous/hyperpigmented linear band between preputial orifice and scrotum)
- **Iatrogenic hyperestrogenism:** Estrogen-containing compounds for urinary incontinence; contact with human hormone creams/patches
- **Pituitary dwarfism (GSD, Karelian Bear Dog):** Autosomal recessive, LHX3 mutation in GSD; bilaterally symmetric alopecia, proportionate dwarfism, delayed permanent tooth eruption
- **Feline paraneoplastic alopecia:** Older cat; rapid extensive alopecia beginning periocularly → ventral neck, abdomen, legs; hairs epilate easily; shiny non-fragile skin; ± dry scaly footpads; ± secondary Malassezia → excessive grooming; caused by pancreatic adenocarcinoma (most common), hepatic carcinoma, biliary cholangiocarcinoma
- **Dermatomyositis:** Collies, Shetland Sheepdogs; autosomal dominant incomplete penetrance in Collies; chromosome 35 linked in Shelties; crusting/scaling/alopecia on face/lower extremities/tail → atrophic skin; ± dysphagia, gait abnormalities, megaesophagus
- **Epitheliotropic (cutaneous) T-cell lymphoma:** Erythroderma, alopecia, variable pruritus, scales, crusts, plaques, nodules, leukoderma, leukotrichia, mucocutaneous ulcers, depigmented/ulcerated/hyperkeratotic footpads; rare Sezary syndrome
- **Feline thymoma-associated exfoliative dermatitis:** Erythema, crusting, scaling, alopecia ± secondary Malassezia/pruritus; ± cough, dyspnea, anorexia, weight loss

**THIN SKIN / SKIN FRAGILITY:**
- **Cutaneous asthenia (Ehlers-Danlos):** Hereditary; defective connective tissue formation; skin hyperextensibility and fragility; ± joint hyperlaxity, hernias, periodontal disease, subcutaneous hematomas; procollagen peptidase defect in one cat
- **Feline skin fragility:** Most often hyperadrenocorticism (adrenal tumor or iatrogenic glucocorticoids/progestins); rarely FIP, hepatic lipidosis, hepatic neoplasia

**THICK SKIN:**
- **Mucinosis:** Breed-associated in Chinese Shar-Pei; occasionally hypothyroid dogs
- **Acromegaly:** Thickened myxedematous skin, excessive skin folds, hypertrichosis, thick hard claws; pituitary neoplasia or hyperprogesteronism

**SCALES AND/OR CRUSTS:**
- **Canine distemper:** "Hard pad disease" — hyperkeratosis of footpads and planum nasale; superficial pustules (impetigo) in acute phase
- **Dermatomyositis:** (see above)
- **Epitheliotropic T-cell lymphoma:** (see above)
- **Thymoma-associated exfoliative dermatitis:** (see above)
- **Leishmaniasis:** >80% of dogs have skin lesions; silvery white scales on head/pinnae/limbs; nasodigital hyperkeratosis; periocular alopecia; onychogryposis; paronychia; nasal depigmentation; nodular dermatitis; systemic: lethargy, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, muscle wasting, cachexia, fever, epistaxis, lameness, ± renal failure/PU/PD
- **Lethal acral dermatitis/acrodermatitis (Bull Terriers):** Autosomal recessive, MKLN1 splicing defect chromosome 14; lighter skin pigmentation, difficulty chewing/swallowing, splaying digits, footpad crusting/cracking, auricular/mucocutaneous ulcers → onychodystrophy, paronychia, interdigital pyoderma, diarrhea, respiratory infections; most die from secondary infections before 7 months
- **Lupus erythematosus — DLE:** Face, planum nasale, dorsal nose; ± epistaxis, secondary infections
- **Lupus erythematosus — SLE:** Skin lesions in 40–50% of cases; mild scaling/alopecia of face/dorsum to severe ulcerations; ± fever, malaise, anemia, weight loss, lymphadenopathy, lameness, depression/behavioral changes
- **Exfoliative cutaneous lupus erythematosus (ECLE — German Shorthaired Pointers):** Familial; excessive scaling/crusting muzzle, pinnae, dorsum → severe lameness, renal disease; UNC93B1 missense variant; most euthanized before 4 years
- **Paraneoplastic pemphigus:** Thymic lymphoma or splenic sarcoma; scaling/crusting, vesiculobullous and ulcerative lesions, face and clawfolds
- **Superficial necrolytic dermatitis (SND/hepatocutaneous):** Ulceration/crusting of mucocutaneous junctions, face, footpads, pressure points; hypoaminoacidemia → keratinocyte degeneration; most affected dogs have liver disease; rarely glucagonoma; ± non-regenerative anemia, mild hyperglycemia, elevated liver enzymes; feline form: pancreatic tumors or chronic liver disease
- **Zinc-responsive dermatitis:** Erythema, alopecia, scales, crusts on face, mucocutaneous junctions, feet, footpads; Alaskan Malamutes and Siberian Huskies (decreased intestinal zinc absorption); Pharaoh Hounds (severe form)

**PLAQUES AND/OR NODULES:**
- **Calcinosis cutis:** Granular plaques most commonly on dorsum or inguinal region; metastatic calcification (renal failure, systemic blastomycosis, paecilomycosis) or dystrophic (hyperadrenocorticism, inflammatory diseases); calcinosis circumscripta over pressure points in young large-breed dogs
- **Eosinophilic plaques (cats):** Underlying allergies (parasitic, dietary, environmental)
- **Feline viral diseases:** FHV-1 → cutaneous/oral ulcers ± severe pruritus; feline calicivirus → oral vesicles/ulcers; feline poxvirus → ulcerating cutaneous nodules ± systemic signs (fever, anorexia, depression, diarrhea, pneumonia); FeLV → chronic/recurrent gingivitis, pyoderma, poor wound healing, seborrhea, exfoliative dermatitis, cutaneous horns, secondary infections (bacteria, Demodex, dermatophytes)
- **Hyperpigmented viral plaques:** Papillomavirus infection on ventral abdomen/axilla; may progress to squamous cell carcinoma
- **Infectious/pyogranulomatous nodules:** Blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, aspergillosis, lagenidiosis, mycobacterial infections
- **Nodular dermatofibromas (German Shepherds):** Autosomal dominant FLCN mutation; multiple cutaneous nodules, cysts + bilateral renal cystadenocarcinomas; females also have uterine leiomyomas/leiomyosarcomas
- **Sterile nodular panniculitis — pancreatic panniculitis variant:** Sterile subcutaneous inflammatory nodules, may ulcerate/drain purulent or oily exudate; associated with pancreatitis and pancreatic tumors; DDx: foreign bodies, immune-mediated reactions, infections, vitamin E deficiency (pansteatitis in cats on high red tuna/cod liver oil), serum alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency, injection reactions, burns, trauma
- **Xanthomas:** Yellow-white papules/nodules/plaques on head, limbs, feet, bony prominences; hyperlipidemia from hereditary hyperlipoproteinemia, diabetes mellitus, hyperadrenocorticism, progestational compounds, high-fat diet

### Ch 13 — Ophthalmic Manifestations of Systemic Disease

**Examination approach:** Slit lamp biomicroscope for anterior segment; Finoff transilluminator for focal bright light; indirect ophthalmoscopy (panretinal 2.2, 20D or 28D lens) for large retinal area; Welch Allyn Panoptic for combined ease/coverage.

**Tapetal fundus lesion algorithm:**
- Hyperreflective → inactive lesion
- Not hyperreflective (active) → color unchanged from surrounding tapetum → clear fluid (early systemic hypertension, secondary retinal edema) OR color changed → cells present (hemorrhage, white blood cells, neoplastic cells)

**EYELIDS AND CONJUNCTIVA:**
- Blepharitis in 102 dogs: allergic disease 52%, infectious/parasitic 21.5%, autoimmune 17.7%, neoplastic 4.9%
- Conjunctivitis in cats (hyperemia, chemosis, blepharospasm, epiphora): FHV-1 (most common, often self-limiting, recrudescent infections eyes only); Chlamydophila felis, Mycoplasma felis (less common); disseminated histoplasmosis (conjunctival/nictitating membrane involvement in cats)
- FHV-1 antiviral doses: topical 0.5% cidofovir BID, vidarabine/trifluridine/idoxuridine QID; oral famciclovir — recommended 90 mg/kg BID (pharmacokinetic study); efficacy shown at 90 mg/kg TID (placebo-controlled study)
- Conjunctivitis in dogs: most often secondary to KCS or systemic allergies; canine herpesvirus rare
- Conjunctival/nictitating membrane involvement: disseminated histoplasmosis in cats

**Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca (KCS):**
- Dogs: immune-mediated destruction most common; other causes: DM, neurogenic, iatrogenic (gland of nictitating membrane excision, radiation), distemper, chronic blepharoconjunctivitis, sulfonamides, etodolac (211 cases, 1992–2002), oral antihistamines
- Dogs in ICU: significant decrease in tear production → risk of corneal ulceration → recommend lubricating artificial tear ointment several times daily
- Cats: KCS rare; transient decrease in FHV-1 infection (may become permanent); blepharospasm prominent (not mucoid discharge); normal STT can be low secondary to stress; treat with viscous artificial tear solution 2–3 times daily

**CORNEA — systemic disease associations (Table 13.1):**

**IRIS (anterior uveitis):**
- Clinical signs: aqueous flare, decreased IOP, painful miosis, conjunctival/scleral hyperemia, ± vision loss
- **Cats — most common causes:**
  - FeLV, FIV, FIP (feline coronavirus), Toxoplasma gondii, systemic fungal diseases, neoplasia
  - Bartonella henselae role still debated
- **Dogs — causes include:**
  - Ocular (trauma, lens-induced uveitis) and systemic (bacterial, fungal, rickettsial, protozoal, parasitic, viral, immune-mediated, neoplastic)
  - Uveodermatologic disease: immune-mediated destruction of pigment in eye and skin → uveitis + leukoderma/leukotrichia
- **Lipemic uveitis:** Accumulation of lipoproteins in anterior chamber; Miniature Schnauzers and Yorkshire Terriers overrepresented; systemic disease in 72% (DM, HAC, hypothyroidism most common); requires both hyperlipidemia AND uveitis
- **Pigmentary uveitis (Golden Retrievers):** Inherited; NOT associated with systemic disease; iris hyperpigmentation, fibrinous material, pigmentation on anterior lens capsule → secondary cataract, glaucoma, blindness
- **Uveitis treatment:** Topical 1% prednisolone acetate, 0.1% dexamethasone, or 0.05% difluprednate; topical 1% atropine for painful miosis; topical NSAIDs (0.03% flurbiprofen, 0.1% diclofenac, 0.5% ketorolac) if corneal ulcer present; systemic corticosteroids for severe/choroidal involvement; monitor IOP

**Aqueocentesis:** Not diagnostic for non-neoplastic uveitis; useful for lymphoma diagnosis in dogs and cats.

**LENS:**
- **Diabetic cataracts (dogs):** Second most common cause (after hereditary); onset very sudden — days to weeks; aldose reductase converts glucose → sorbitol accumulation → water → fiber damage; intumescent cataract; spontaneous lens capsule rupture reported; shorter surgical window than genetic cataracts
- **Hypocalcemia:** Focal cataract formation
- **Cats:** Most common cause of cataract = chronic anterior uveitis

**CHOROID AND RETINA:**
- Causes similar to anterior uveitis; clinical signs include retinal edema, vascular tortuosity/vasculitis, intraretinal/preretinal/vitreal hemorrhage, retinal detachment
- Punctate retinal hemorrhages (119 eyes, 83 dogs): systemic disease in 60%; DM, multiple myeloma, systemic hypertension most common
- **Topical medications not effective for chorioretinitis** — systemic treatment required

**SUDDEN ACQUIRED RETINAL DEGENERATION (SARD):**
- Common cause of irreversible blindness in dogs
- Demographics: most commonly middle-to-older aged female dogs; small breeds predisposed
- Systemic signs preceding vision loss: PU/PD, polyphagia, weight gain; elevated liver enzymes, stress leukogram (mimics Cushing's)
- Diagnosis: ERG shows extinguished retinal function; chromatic PLR — absent/severely diminished with bright RED light, normal with BLUE light; ophthalmoscopy normal in acute phase
- Histopathology: photoreceptor outer segment acute degeneration with relative preservation of rest of retina
- Etiology: poorly understood; immune-mediated suggested

**ORBITAL DISEASE:**
- Orbital abscess (dogs): dental disease, foreign body penetration, rarely abscess elsewhere
- Masticatory myositis: immune-mediated; bilateral painful orbital disease; diagnose by type 2M fiber-specific autoantibodies against masticatory muscle myosin heavy and light chains
- Extraocular polymyositis: young Golden Retrievers; bilateral exophthalmos; all extraocular muscles enlarged on CT/MRI; normal retropulsion; 360° scleral visibility
- Orbital neoplasia: usually malignant in both dogs and cats; primary or secondary from nasal cavity extension or metastatic

**SYSTEMIC HYPERTENSION — ocular manifestations:**
- Hyphema, iris aneurysms, vitreal hemorrhage, retinal hemorrhage, retinal vascular tortuosity, retinal edema, retinal detachment, vision loss → mydriatic pupil
- 88 cats with hypertensive chorioretinopathy: 61% blind in both eyes at presentation; of these, 58% regained some vision with treatment
- Similar signs in hyperviscosity syndrome (e.g., multiple myeloma)
- Treatment: manage hypertension; topical corticosteroids 2–4 times daily if hyphema present; monitor IOP (glaucoma risk)

**Hyphema — systemic causes:**
- Systemic hypertension; any bleeding disorder
- Ophthalmic causes: trauma, chronic uveitis, retinal detachment, intraocular neoplasia
- Post-sterilization hyphema: rare in cats

**METASTATIC NEOPLASIA:**
- Uvea is highly vascular; acts as filter for neoplastic cells
- Most common: lymphoma in iris of dogs and cats
- Cats: focal pink raised iris lesion; aqueocentesis → cytospin for lymphoma diagnosis
- Dogs: manifests as anterior uveitis ± focal nodules
- Treatment: systemic chemotherapy; topical corticosteroids if systemic disease not adequately controlled

**Detailed disease descriptions:**

**Hypoxia from systemic disease:** Autoregulation fails when mean arterial blood pressure <50 mm Hg. Global ischemia causes bilateral watershed zone infarcts. Hyperviscosity (polycythemia, hypercholesterolemia/hyperlipidemia, immune-mediated disease, sepsis, coagulopathies, hyperglobulinemia) can cause ischemic infarcts. Acute vision loss in cats after cerebrovascular accident may be due to mouth gag occluding maxillary arteries. Recovery is slow, taking weeks to months.

**Systemic hypertension:** Autoregulation fails at rapidly developing hypertension; neurologic signs occur at lower blood pressure (170 mm Hg) than when hypertension develops slowly. Signs: seizures, ataxia, stupor, blindness. Postmortem: cerebral edema, cerebellar herniation, arteriolar hyalinosis, hyperplastic arteriosclerosis, ischemia, necrosis. Hyaline arteriopathy in cats → thrombosis → cervical myelopathy or encephalopathy.

**Hepatic encephalopathy (HE):** Caused by portosystemic shunt (PSS), microvascular dysplasia, idiopathic noncirrhotic portal hypertension, or other liver failure. Neurologic signs in 95% of dogs with PSS (waxing-waning, worse after meals). Range: mild (inability to learn, behavioral changes) to severe (head pressing, blindness, mentation changes, seizures). Hyperammonemia >120 mcmol/L documented in young Irish Wolfhounds without PSS. CSF: elevated ammonia, glutamine, quinolinic acid, tryptophan. MRI: hyperintense non–contrast-enhancing lesions in lentiform nuclei, widened sulci. Phenobarbital should be used with caution (protein-bound, liver-metabolized). Benzodiazepines may not prevent postsurgical "withdrawal" seizures. Congenital PSS before 2 years → better prognosis.

**Renal encephalopathy:** Toxic substances not excreted → encephalopathic signs similar to HE. PTH excess and subsequent hypercalcemia may play a role.

**Hypothyroidism (CNS):** Acute or chronic progressive CENTRAL VESTIBULAR signs may be sole sign. Mechanisms: atherosclerosis with hypercholesterol/hypertriglyceride-induced microthrombi, segmental demyelination, brain metabolic pathway dysfunction. BAER or EEG abnormalities possible. Treatment with thyroxine usually resolves signs. Myxedema coma (rare): brain-edema-induced mentation changes, hypothermia without shivering, nonpitting skin edema, bradycardia, hyponatremia, hypoventilatory hypoxia; clinical improvement usually within 24 hours but mortality high.

**Congenital hypothyroidism:** Disproportionate dwarfism, abnormal hair coat, lethargy, stiff/stilted gait, abnormal mentation; hypomyelination in corpus callosum, corona radiata, pons, pyramids, lateral funiculi.

**Hyperthyroidism (CNS in cats):** Hyperactivity, change in sleep/wake cycle, aggression, or obtundation; signs resolve with treatment.

**Pituitary-dependent hyperadrenocorticism (PDH):** Direct compression from pituitary macroadenoma in ~15–30% of dogs with PDH. Early signs with macroadenoma: inappetence, mild obtundation, disorientation, pacing. Severe signs (>1.5 cm diameter): obtundation, circling, tetraparesis, ataxia, seizures. Blindness rare. Infarcts possible from hyperlipidemia.

**Hypoglycemia:** Brain requires ~100 g/day glucose. Causes: poor nutrition, insulinoma, liver failure, hypoadrenocorticism, nonislet cell tumors (insulin-like growth factors), large metabolically active tumors (leiomyosarcoma), severe polycythemia, sepsis, paraneoplastic syndrome, exogenous insulin overdose. Signs: obtundation, weakness, disorientation, tremors, partial or generalized seizures, blindness, coma — wax and wane with episodes. No correlation between severity of signs and degree of hypoglycemia. Permanent brain damage possible.

**Thiamine deficiency:** Deficient in cats/dogs fed meat preserved with sulfur dioxide, thiaminase-containing fish, or food low in thiamine from processing. Histopathology: polioencephalomalacia with bilateral symmetric sponginess, necrosis, hemorrhage in medial vestibular nuclei, caudal colliculi, cerebellar nodulus, subcortical grey matter. MRI: hyperintense T2/FLAIR, gadolinium-enhancing. Treatment: resolution of signs after weeks to months.

**Irradiated diets (cats):** Leukoencephalomyelopathy; ataxia after several months; Wallerian degeneration in brain/spinal cord. Recovery on balanced diet or cobalamine (vitamin B12).

**Hypercalcemia:** Rarely causes seizures via poorly understood mechanisms.

**Hypocalcemia:** Lactating pets (eclampsia), CKD, primary hypoparathyroidism → increased membrane excitability → generalized tetany and seizures. May be associated with coagulopathies.

**Hyponatremia (severe):** Cerebral edema, life-threatening diffuse encephalopathy. Overcorrection → CNS signs from cerebral edema, central pontine myelinolysis, loss of oligodendroglia 2 to several days after treatment; MRI shows bilateral symmetrical T2-hyperintense areas in central thalamic nuclei. Correction must be gradual.

**Hypernatremia:** Osmotic movement of water out of brain cells → reduced brain volume, rupture of cerebral vessels, focal hemorrhage. Severity related to RATE of development rather than magnitude. Chronic: brain adapts by producing idiogenic osmoles; rapid correction → water into cells → cerebral edema.

**Neoplasia (CNS):** Direct invasion/metastasis or secondary to ischemic/hemorrhagic infarcts. Paraneoplastic syndromes: hypoglycemia (insulinoma), hypercalcemia (lymphoma, thymoma, apocrine adenocarcinoma). Diagnosis: MRI, CSF analysis, identifying primary neoplasm.

**Hyperthermia:** Origin of neurologic dysfunction not from direct temperature effects but from secondary changes — hepatocellular degeneration, DIC, respiratory alkalosis, reduced mean arterial pressure.

**PNS DISEASES:**

**Aortic thromboembolism (dogs):** Secondary to protein-losing nephropathy, HAC, neoplasia, DIC, sepsis, atherosclerosis, immune-mediated hemolytic anemia. Signs: chronic exercise intolerance, pelvic limb weakness, or acute symmetric/asymmetric pelvic limb ataxia/paresis/plegia — ischemic myopathy, neuropathy, or myelopathy. Cats: cardiomyopathy, hyperthyroidism, neoplasia → peracute signs (tachypnea, hypothermia, paraparesis/plegia).

**Hypothyroidism (PNS):** T4 → mitochondrial respiratory activity → ATP production. Deficiency: impaired Na+/K+ pump → axonal degeneration and demyelination. Signs: generalized weakness and focal signs — laryngeal paralysis, facial paralysis, megaesophagus, or vestibular syndrome. Electrodiagnostic and biopsy abnormalities: demyelination and axonal loss. Signs may resolve after months of treatment.

**Hyperthyroidism (cats PNS):** Neuromuscular weakness, ventroflexion of neck, plantigrade stance, exercise intolerance; reversible with treatment.

**Hyperadrenocorticism (PNS):** Fibrotic myopathy or polyneuropathy (rare). Fibrotic myopathy: stiff stilted gait, generalized muscle atrophy, difficulty flexing limbs; EMG and nerve/muscle biopsy show type II myofiber atrophy and fibrosis; stiffness rarely improves with treatment. Steroid myopathy: also from exogenous glucocorticoids.

**Hypoadrenocorticism (PNS):** Lethargy, weakness, tremors, collapse; painful muscle cramps extremely rare. Secondary hypoglycemia occasionally contributes. Full clinical recovery with treatment.

**Diabetes mellitus (DM — cats):** Sensorimotor polyneuropathy (late complication); most prominent in pelvic limbs — plantigrade stance, difficulty jumping, postural reaction deficits, decreased tendon reflexes, muscle atrophy. In ~10% of diabetic cats. Histopathology: demyelination, splitting/ballooning of myelin sheath, axonal injury, microvascular pathology. Permanent deficits common.

**Chronic kidney disease (PNS):** Renal secondary hyperparathyroidism → peripheral neuropathy and myopathy; excess PTH also partially affects motor nerve conduction velocities. PTH-mediated uremic myopathy may be reversible with calcitriol.

**Hypokalemia (PNS):** Reduced intake, excess renal/GI losses, rarely hyperaldosteronism or adrenal-dependent HAC. Genetic potassium-wasting nephropathy in Burmese cats. Alters resting muscle cell membrane potential → generalized muscular weakness in dogs/cats; ventroflexion of neck in cats.

**Paraneoplastic syndromes:** Remote effect of cancer on CNS and PNS. Signs can develop months to years before tumor detected. Thymoma: common cause of myasthenia gravis in cats and humans, less common in dogs. Focal (megaesophagus) or generalized; may improve with mass removal.

### Ch 15 — Intoxication Versus Acute Nontoxicologic Illness: Differentiating the Two
*(Author: Mark Edward Hitt)*

**Core principle:** Toxicosis belongs on the DDx from the start of any case. The clinician must avoid premature closure by repeatedly reassessing whether toxicosis has been excluded.

**Epidemiology:** Toxicoses are LESS common than the breadth of all diseases — they may fall to the bottom of DDx lists unless consciously considered.

#### Toxidromes — Key Clinical Tool
A toxidrome is a clustered set of clinical findings that identifies a probable class of toxicant. Presence of multiple signs within the same toxidrome reinforces the likelihood of toxicosis; a single sign without others makes toxicosis less likely.

| Toxidrome | Mechanism | Causative Agents (examples) | Clinical Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anticholinergic | Antagonism of CNS and peripheral cholinergic receptors | Atropine, Atropa belladonna, Datura spp. | CNS stimulation/disorientation/hyperexcitability, tachycardia, mydriasis, hyperthermia |
| Anticoagulant | Antagonism of coagulation cascade or platelet number/function | Anticoagulant rodenticides (brodifacoum, diphacinone), rivaroxaban, apixaban, warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel | Bleeding from mucous membranes, GI tract, urinary tract, wounds; petechiae; pulmonary/CNS/peritoneal/pleural hemorrhage |
| SAS (Solvents/Anesthetics/Sedatives) | Direct CNS effects | Gasoline, xylene, methylene chloride, Freon, nitrous oxide, isoflurane, benzodiazepines, barbiturates | Ataxia followed by depression, sedation, coma |
| Cholinergic | Cholinesterase antagonism → excess acetylcholine | Organophosphate insecticides, bethanechol, pyridostigmine, pilocarpine, nicotine, some mushrooms | "SLUDGE": salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastric emesis; mydriasis, tremors, seizures |
| Convulsant | GABA antagonism or glutamate agonism | Strychnine, hydrazine | Seizures are hallmark; often sudden and severe; generally temporally clustered |
| Irritant/Corrosive | Mucosal injury (inhalation, ingestion) or chemical burn (topical) | Smoke inhalation, potpourri ingestion (cats), household acids/alkalis | Inhalation: dyspnea, upper or lower airway; Ingestion: dysphagia, oral mucosal burns; Topical: skin erythema/sloughing at contact |
| Knockdown/Asphyxiants | Disrupted cellular O2 delivery | Cyanide, carbon monoxide | Cherry-red mucous membranes and venous blood, severe arrhythmia, mydriasis, seizures (cyanide); peracute presentation |
| Opioid | CNS depression (mu receptors) | Hydromorphone, fentanyl, illicit drugs | Depressed/disoriented/poorly responsive mentation; miosis; rapid reversal with naloxone |
| Stress-response/Sympathomimetic | Excess sympathetic ANS stimulation | Caffeine, nicotine, pseudoephedrine, cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine, methylphenidate, epinephrine | Anxiety, agitation exceptional for the individual in context |

#### Diagnostic Clues for Toxicosis
**History suggestive:** Missing/chewed pill containers; lost/dropped human medications; discovery of recreational drugs; exposure to raisins, grapes, xylitol; pica or known chewing behavior; improperly stored food; spoiled foods/carcasses; mushroom growth in accessible areas; blue-green algae access; home renovations (lead paint); indoor/outdoor plants; pesticide/herbicide exposure areas
- Multiple animals in same environment with similar signs → toxicologic cause more likely
- ASPCA Animal Poison Control: 888-426-4435

**Key timing rule:** Incompatible timing RULES OUT a toxin. Example: a dog with epistaxis with rodenticide exposure only in the preceding 12 hours CANNOT have that rodenticide as the cause.

**MDR-1 (ABCB1) mutation:** Affects pharmacogenetics; certain breeds have altered drug sensitivity.

#### Clinical Decision-Making
- **Type I decisions:** Heuristic/"gut instinct" — appropriate for emergencies but risk cognitive biases
- **Type II decisions:** Deliberate, sequential, algorithm-based — used when more time or thorough analysis needed
- DAMN-IT mnemonic: Degenerative, Anomalous/Anomalous, Mental/Mechanical/Metabolic, Nutritional, Neoplastic, Inflammatory/Infectious/Ischemic/Immune-mediated/Inherited/Idiopathic, Traumatic, Toxic

#### When Toxicosis Is Suspected (Management Priorities)
1. ABCs/stabilization first
2. Decontamination + enhanced elimination
3. Cytoprotection of organ systems
4. Collect samples pre-treatment (serum, whole blood, stomach contents, hair) for later toxicant identification
5. Sequential reassessment — ongoing since "finished asking questions" may never be reached

### Ch 16 — Fever
*(Authors: Séverine Tasker, Ian K. Ramsey)*

#### Definitions and Key Thresholds
- **Fever (pyrexia):** Increased body temperature with raised thermoregulatory set point in anterior hypothalamus, secondary to release of pyrogens. Animal does NOT show behavioral cooling responses.
- **Hyperthermia:** Increased body temperature WITHOUT change in thermoregulatory set point. Animal WILL show panting, vasodilation, cold-seeking behavior.
- **Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO):** Temperature >39.2°C (102.5°F) of at least 3 weeks' duration; no obvious cause after ≥3 vet visits and/or 3 days hospitalization including CBC, biochemistry, urinalysis; additionally often defined as fever persisting after 5–10 day course of antibacterials.
- **Normal rectal temperature:** 38.0°C–39.2°C (100.5°F–102.5°F); healthy dogs and cats can reach up to 39.7°C (103.5°F) in consulting room
- **Exercise-induced hyperthermia:** Labrador Retrievers can reach 42.2°C (108°F); most dogs can reach 41.1°C (106°F) after exercise
- **Life-threatening temperature:** >41.1°C (106°F) → neurological damage, DIC, metabolic abnormalities; more likely in hyperthermic patients; active cooling required immediately
- **Fever rarely exceeds:** 41.1°C (106°F) due to endogenous anti-pyretic mechanisms

#### Temperature Measurement
- **Gold standard (invasive):** Esophageal/pulmonary artery thermistors (not for conscious patients)
- **Rectal:** Most accurate; preferred method; good agreement with core in dogs
- **Tympanic membrane (dogs):** Second choice when rectal not possible in dogs; better tolerated, better agreement with rectal than axillary
- **Axillary (cats):** Second choice when rectal not possible in cats; rectal temperature ≈ axillary + 0.9°C (1.6°F) in cats
- **Critical difference >0.5°C (0.9°F):** Clinically unacceptable in both species
- **Nose palpation:** NOT reliable. Sensitivity 29.4%, specificity 79.5% for detecting rectal hyperthermia in dogs

#### Pathogenesis
Exogenous pyrogens → cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) → arachidonic acid cascade → ↑prostaglandin E2 → hypothalamic set point raised. Fever rarely kills bacteria; it improves host immune response.

#### Hyperthermia Causes (non-pyrogenic)
Exposure to increased environmental temperature; exercise (especially hot/humid, overweight dogs, respiratory impairment); seizures; hypermetabolic disorders (hyperthyroidism, hypocalcemia); certain drugs (opioids, ketamine and phenobarbital in cats; SSRIs in dogs and cats); intoxications (cocaine in dogs); malignant hyperthermia; stress (including veterinary clinic)

**Step 2: Acute (<5 days) fever with mild signs + no localizing signs**
- Monitor; may resolve spontaneously

**Step 3: Chronic (≥5 days) fever or severe signs — Minimum Database (Phase 1):**
- CBC (including blood smear)
- Serum biochemistry profile
- Urinalysis

- ± Thoracic radiographs and abdominal radiographs
- In cats: also abdominal ultrasound and alpha-1 glycoprotein (FIP support)
- 5-day antibacterial trial: clavulanate-potentiated amoxicillin (first line); doxycycline in tick-endemic areas; GS-441524 for FIP

**Phase 2 (if diagnosis still not apparent after Phase 1 + antibacterial trial):**
- Urine culture
- Specific serology/PCR (based on history and region)
- Abdominal ultrasound
- Radiographs of vertebrae, limbs
- Echocardiography

**Phase 3 (if still undiagnosed):**
- Bronchoscopy + BAL
- Arthrocentesis (ch. 87)
- CSF analysis (ch. 109)
- FNA of lymph nodes (even if normal)
- Blood and fecal cultures
- Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy
- Tissue biopsies
- MRI (brain, spine) before CSF tap
- CT (thorax, head)

**If all above performed → designate as FUO**
Note: ~22% of 217 referred dogs remained undiagnosed; ~15.1% of 106 referred cats undiagnosed

#### Key Diagnostic Clues by Signalment (Figures 16.3)
**Cats:** Infectious causes most common (especially bite abscess); FIP important in young cats; FIV/FeLV testing essential
**Dogs:** SRMA common in young dogs; breed-associated immunodeficiencies (Irish Setter LAD, Border Collie cyclic neutropenia, hypocobalaminemia Border Collies); neoplasia in older dogs (Bernese Mountain Dogs → histiocytic disease); sex-associated (pyometra in females)

#### Treatment of Fever
- **Antibacterials:** For known or strongly suspected bacterial infection; clavulanate-potentiated amoxicillin first choice; doxycycline in tick-endemic areas; avoid fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, later cephalosporins as first-line empiric choices
- **NSAIDs (anti-pyretics):** Reduce thromboxane/prostaglandins via COX inhibition; also analgesic. Reserve for severe or prolonged fever with significant clinical signs. Risks: renal/hepatic impairment, GI ulceration, blood dyscrasias — increased with repeated use
- **Steroids:** Only once immune-mediated diagnosis established. Exceptions: co-administer with antibacterials when immune-mediated + possible infectious cause cannot be differentiated (e.g., some tick-borne + secondary immune-mediated in cats)
- **Active cooling:** ONLY if temperature >41.1°C (106°F). Fans, cool water. Obese patients and those with upper respiratory compromise are higher risk
- **Fluid therapy:** Fever increases insensible water losses; monitor hydration carefully; IV fluids may be required
- **Mild acute fever of unknown cause:** May be better left untreated to allow development of diagnostic localizing signs

### Ch 17 — Anorexia
*(Author: María Dolores Pérez Alenza)*

#### Definitions
- **Adequate food intake:** Voluntary consumption enough to maintain or reach ideal body weight
- **Hyporexia:** Consumption of inadequate calories to maintain or reach ideal body weight
- **Anorexia:** Complete absence of voluntary food intake
- **Dysrexia:** Abnormal patterns of food intake (cyclic or unpredictable)

**Resting Energy Requirements (RER):**
- For pets 3–25 kg: RER (kcal/day) = (30 × body weight in kg) + 70
- For pets of any weight: RER (kcal/day) = 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75

**Key initial step:** ALWAYS first rule out INABILITY to eat (vs. reduced appetite). Causes of inability to eat: oral foreign body, dental disease, nasal or retrobulbar conditions, masticatory myositis, physical barriers (Elizabethan collar)

#### Complete DDx for Reduced Food Intake (Box 17.1)

**Environmental Stressors**
- Changes in diet, owners/caretakers, daily routine
- Presence of new cats, dogs, or humans
- Unnatural day/light cycles, ambient temperature, noisy surroundings
- Unfamiliar environment
- Lack of environmental resources (especially cats: places to hide and play)

**Dietary-Related Causes**
- Poor quality, spoiled, nutritionally unbalanced diet
- Inadequate taste, smell, format (canned/dry/semi-moist), kibble shape/size
- Feeding location and timing, type of bowl, presence of other animals or people

**Medications**
- Chemotherapy
- Opioids
- NSAIDs
- Antibiotics
- Antifungals

**Pain**
- Osteoarthritis
- Inflammatory pain not related to OA: chronic periodontal disease, feline lower urinary tract disease, inflammatory bowel disease, otitis
- Cancer pain
- Maladaptive chronic pain: CNS lesions, chronic intervertebral disk disease
- Postsurgical conditions

**Oropharyngeal/Cranial Disorders**
- Oral and periodontal diseases (inflammatory, foreign bodies)
- Nasal diseases (fungal, neoplastic)
- Ocular diseases
- Skull and mandibular lesions (mandibular fractures, osteomyelitis, TMJ disease)
- Myositis
- Trigeminal, facial neuritis

**Gastrointestinal (Including Hepatic and Pancreatic) Diseases**
- GI acute and chronic disorders
- Acute and chronic pancreatitis
- Hepatobiliary diseases

**Respiratory Distress**
- Pleural effusion
- Diaphragmatic hernia
- Airway diseases
- Pneumonia, bronchitis, asthma

**Cognitive and Neurological Dysfunction**
- Aging cognitive dysfunction
- Central neurological disorders

**Systemic Diseases**
- Acute and chronic kidney disease
- Infectious diseases
- Immune-mediated diseases
- Neoplasms
- Endocrine diseases (diabetic ketoacidosis, hypoadrenocorticism)
- Fever of unknown origin

#### Diagnostic Approach (Figure 17.1)
1. History (environmental, dietary, medications, other signs)
2. Physical exam (general, oral/nasal, ocular, rectal, ± neurologic/orthopedic)
3. Blood pressure: Hypotensive SBP <90 mmHg → ACTH stim test; Pre-hypertensive SBP ≥140 mmHg → SDMA, UPC
4. Clinical pathology: CBC, biochemistry, urinalysis
5. Diagnostic imaging: Radiographs, US, CT, MRI
6. Additional: Serology (FeLV-FIV, Toxoplasma, Leishmania, Ehrlichia), hormonal tests, fecal exam, endoscopy, FNB/biopsy

#### Management
- Address underlying cause
- Avoid environmental stressors; eliminate food aversions
- Diet palatability: warm food, change texture, add flavors/aromas, increase moisture
- Appetite stimulants:
  - **Mirtazapine:** Cats: 1.88 mg PO q24–48h (CKD cats); FDA-approved transdermal Miratraz 2 mg/cat; increases weight and reduces vomiting. Dogs: also effective
  - **Capromorelin (ENTYCE):** FDA-approved in dogs, 3 mg/kg PO; ghrelin receptor agonist; increases appetite, food consumption and weight; caution in cats (insulin suppression → glucose intolerance for up to 30 days)
  - **Cyproheptadine:** Serotonin antagonist
  - **Gabapentin:** Appetite-stimulating effect shown in cats post-ovariectomy
  - **Benzodiazepines (diazepam), anabolic steroids, low-dose propofol:** Less commonly used
- Assisted feeding: When consuming <RER for >3–5 days; timing depends on BCS, MCS, and underlying disease. Some conditions require immediate intervention (e.g., feline hepatic lipidosis). Requires placement of feeding tubes

### Ch 18 — Weight Loss
*(Author: Thomas Schermerhorn)*

#### Definitions and Thresholds
- **Clinically relevant weight loss (humans):** ≥5% over 6–12 months → should warrant investigation
- **Benchmark for dogs and cats:** ~5% body weight loss is a reasonable threshold
- **Cachexia vs. starvation:** Distinguished by two features: (1) inflammation is consistent in cachexia (not starvation); (2) ubiquitin-proteasome system activation + elevated resting energy expenditure in cachexia
- **Cachexia:** Marked activation of inflammatory cascade (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), muscle/adipose tissue loss, possible insulin-resistant state. Diagnosis: marked weight loss, severe muscle loss, decreased appetite in setting of chronic inflammatory response or cancer

#### Mechanisms of Weight Loss
1. Decreased caloric intake
2. Increased metabolic demand
3. Accelerated energy loss
4. Inability to utilize ingested calories
5. Inappetence

#### Causes of Weight Loss by Category (Box 18.1)

**Dietary History — Inadequate:**
- Starvation
- Underfeeding
- Poor quality food

**Dietary History — Adequate:**
- Environmental/housing factors: competition for food, limited access
- Oral and dental disease
- Impaired use of nutrients: specific nutrient deficiency, maldigestion, malabsorption, diabetes mellitus
- Protein-losing disease: nephropathy, gastroenteropathy

**GI and Endocrine Causes:**
- Oral/dental disease, megaesophagus, chronic gastroenteritis, IBD, pancreatitis, dysmotility (nausea/inappetence/protein loss/maldigestion/malabsorption)
- Hyperthyroidism (↑metabolic rate)
- Diabetes mellitus (inability to utilize nutrients; ↑appetite)
- Hypoadrenocorticism (↓appetite)
- Hyperparathyroidism (weight loss with ↓appetite)

**Cardiac, Respiratory, Kidney, Infectious, Inflammatory, Neurologic:**
- Cardiac cachexia: CHF from valvular disease or cardiomyopathy (dogs)
- CKD: uremia → nausea, gastric ulceration, vomiting → ↓appetite
- Chronic bacterial/fungal infection with systemic inflammation
- Immune-mediated/connective tissue disorders
- Neurologic disorders: impair autonomic function, cause dysmotility, or impair mastication/swallowing

**Miscellaneous (typically associated with cachexia):**
- Cardiac disease
- Elevated metabolism (hyperthyroidism, chronic fever)
- End-stage renal disease
- Neoplasia
- Chronic infection
- Chronic inflammation of any cause (immunologic disease)

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 18.1)
1. Verify weight loss (compare to recorded weights; >5% over <12 months)
2. Dietary history:
   - Inadequate → suspect starvation/underfeeding
   - Adequate → comprehensive diagnostic workup:
     - Neoplasia, GI disorders, endocrine disorders, renal disorders, cardiovascular disorders, infection, respiratory disorders, neurologic disorders, inflammatory disorders
3. Evidence of chronic inflammation or neoplasia → consider cachexia
4. No diagnosis found → periodic follow-up and serial monitoring (up to 30% of human cases never get diagnosis; wait-and-see strategy acceptable)

#### Laboratory Testing
- Non-specific; reflects primary pathology
- Cachexia: variable serum protein (acute phase response may elevate fibrinogen; ↓albumin)
- Cortisol and insulin may be elevated
- CBC, biochemistry, urinalysis as starting point
- T4 in cats; TLI, cobalamin, folate if malabsorption suspected

#### Treatment
- Primary: resolve underlying disease
- Hypercaloric feeding for cachexia (but alone insufficient — doesn't reverse muscle protein catabolism)
- Pharmacologic options: fish oils, appetite stimulants, anabolic agents, anti-inflammatory drugs, cytokine inhibitors, specific ubiquitin pathway inhibitors (investigational)

### Ch 19 — Polyphagia
*(Author: Nadja Sieber-Ruckstuhl)*

#### Definition
Polyphagia = excessive consumption of food. May be physiologic (increased energy requirements) or a sign of systemic disease.

**Key initial step:** Determine whether body weight (BW) is increasing, stable, or decreasing — this guides the differential diagnosis significantly.

#### Complete DDx for Polyphagia (Table 19.1)

**Primary (CNS-based)**
- CNS lesion: infection, neoplasia, trauma
- Psychogenic: stress, new palatable food, food competition

**Secondary — Medications**
- Glucocorticoids
- Anticonvulsants
- Antihistamines
- Benzodiazepines
- Cyproheptadine, mirtazapine
- Progestins

**Secondary — Physiologically Increased Metabolism**
- Growth
- Pregnancy
- Lactation
- Increased exercise
- Cold temperature

**Secondary — Pathologically Increased Metabolism**
- Hyperthyroidism (weight LOSS despite ↑food intake)
- Acromegaly

**Secondary — Decreased Energy Supply (due to LOSS)**
- Diabetes mellitus

**Secondary — Decreased Energy Supply (due to Malassimilation)**
- Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI/EPI)
- Infiltrative bowel disease
- Lymphangiectasis
- Parasites

**Secondary — Decreased Energy Supply (due to Decreased Intake/Cellular)**
- Megaesophagus
- Poor quality food
- Hypoglycemia

**Secondary — Decreased Energy Supply (due to Unknown Mechanisms)**
- Cushing's syndrome/hyperadrenocorticism
- Sudden acquired retinal degeneration syndrome (SARDS)
- Portosystemic shunt/hepatoencephalopathy

#### Body Weight Trend as Diagnostic Guide
- **Weight GAIN:** Primary/psychogenic polyphagia, drug-induced, or physiologically increased metabolism → caloric intake exceeds expenditure
- **Weight STABLE or LOSS despite polyphagia:** Pathologically increased metabolism (hyperthyroidism) OR nutrient loss/malabsorption (DM, EPI, parasites, IBD)

#### Physical Exam Findings by Disease
- **CNS lesion:** Ataxia, proprioceptive deficits, blindness, PU/PD, circling
- **DM:** Weight loss + PU/PD; cats: plantigrade stance
- **Cushing's syndrome:** PU/PD, exercise intolerance, hair/skin changes, panting, pot-belly
- **Hyperthyroidism (cats):** Weight loss, PU/PD, hyperactivity, personality changes, palpable cervical mass
- **Acromegaly:** Disproportionate growth of head, paws, belly, skin
- **EPI:** Large-volume, malodorous, soft stools
- **Infiltrative bowel disease/lymphangiectasis/parasites:** GI signs (diarrhea, melena)
- **Megaesophagus:** History of regurgitation
- **PSS/hepatic encephalopathy:** Neurologic abnormalities, ascites, retarded growth
- **SARDS:** Blind dogs resembling Cushing's syndrome

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 19.1)
1. History + clinical exam
2. Weigh the pet; assess BW changes
3. Rule out: pregnancy, psychogenic/environmental factors, dietary inadequacy
4. Minimum database: CBC, chemistry profile (with TT4 in cats), urinalysis
5. If young with unknown deworming history + polyphagia + weight loss + otherwise healthy: fecal exam and deworming trial
6. If abnormal → directed diagnostics:
   - Weight gain: neurologic exam, MRI/CSF/blood glucose; IGF-1, LDDS test
   - Hypoglycemia: abdominal US, glucose-insulin pair, dual-phase CT → insulinoma
   - Weight loss: TLI, cobalamin, fecal, vit B12 → EPI/malabsorption; basal T4 (cats) → hyperthyroidism; imaging → DM, megaesophagus, hepatic disease

### Ch 20 — Weight Gain
*(Author: Alexander J. German)*

#### Causes of Weight Gain
**Two main mechanisms:** (1) accumulation of fluid (rapid — days to weeks) or (2) accumulation of body tissue — adipose + lean (slow — weeks to months)

**Obesity (overwhelmingly most common cause):**
- Defined: >30% above ideal weight; "overweight" = >10% above ideal
- BCS 6–7 = overweight (10–20% above ideal); BCS 8–9 = obese (30–40% above ideal); some animals "beyond scale" (beyond BCS 9)
- Risk factors: genetics, dietary factors, owner factors, comorbidities, neutering, certain breeds (Labrador, Golden Retriever, brachycephalics, Beagles), middle age
- Concurrent drug therapy causing weight gain: corticosteroids, anticonvulsants (phenobarbital, potassium bromide), progestins

**Endocrinopathies:**
- Hypothyroidism (dogs): ↓metabolism, no change in caloric intake
- Hyperadrenocorticism (dogs and cats): ↑food intake + fat redistribution + organomegaly
- Insulinoma (dogs): ↑food intake
- Acromegaly (cats): ↑food intake + fat redistribution + organomegaly

**Fluid Accumulation (rapid weight gain):**
- Right-sided congestive heart failure
- Hepatic disease
- Infectious, inflammatory, or protein-losing conditions

**Abdominal Masses:** Large tumors, most likely intra-abdominal

**Musculoskeletal disease:** Immobility → decreased energy expenditure → weight gain

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 20.1)
1. Determine rate of weight gain:
   - Rapid (fluid): assess cardiac, hepatic, infectious, inflammatory, protein-losing causes; imaging (thoracic radiograph, US, echo), ± abdominal imaging
   - Slow (tissue): assess for obesity, endocrinopathy, or abdominal mass
2. Check for polyphagia, polydipsia, and other compatible signs
3. Check for hair and skin changes → suspect endocrinopathy
4. Laboratory: CBC, biochemistry, T4, cTSH (dogs), ACTH stimulation test, LDDST, IGF-1
5. Imaging: thoracic radiograph + US (pleural effusion, cardiac), abdominal radiograph + US (adrenals, masses, ascites), whole-body CT for large dogs; MRI or CT for pituitary mass
6. If obesity confirmed: full nutritional and exercise history; identify and treat comorbidities; develop weight management plan

#### Key Monitoring Point
- Practices should weigh dogs and cats at EVERY visit to enable individualized records and early detection of bodyweight changes

### Ch 21 — Abdominal Enlargement
*(Author: Emilia Bourassi)*

#### Classification: The "7 F's" Mnemonic
| Category | Specific Causes |
|---|---|
| **Fat** | Obesity, lipoma, hyperadrenocorticism (mesenteric fat redistribution) |
| **Fluid** | Free peritoneal/retroperitoneal effusion; fluid in GI tract (ileus, obstruction), uterus (pyometra, hydrometra), kidneys/ureters (hydronephrosis), urinary bladder (obstruction, neurologic); cysts; abscesses |
| **Firm** | Organomegaly (non-neoplastic or neoplastic), intra-abdominal lymphadenomegaly, granuloma (foreign body, fungal, parasitic, pythiosis) |
| **Food (GI contents)** | Postprandial, overeating, constipation, megacolon, ileus/obstruction, heavy parasite load |
| **Flatulence (Gas)** | Free in abdomen: iatrogenic (post-surgical), bacterial peritonitis, GI rupture, penetrating wound; In organs: GDV, obstruction, functional ileus, gas-producing bacteria (liver, gallbladder, bladder) |
| **Flabby (weak abdominal muscles)** | Hyperadrenocorticism, muscle-wasting disease |
| **Fetus** | Pregnancy; fetal or placenta retention post-partum |

#### Emergency Signs (Immediate Action Required)
- Lethargy, weakness, dyspnea, tachycardia, pale mucous membranes, poor pulses, CRT >2 seconds, unproductive retching, severe abdominal pain
- Acute onset → more likely an active process (active bleeding)

#### Peritoneal Fluid Classification and Differential Diagnoses
**Low-protein transudate** (severe hypoalbuminemia or presinusoidal portal hypertension):
- Protein-losing enteropathies, protein-losing nephropathies, liver vascular anomalies, hepatic cirrhosis, burn injuries

**High-protein transudate** (sinusoidal or post-sinusoidal portal hypertension):
- Congestive heart failure, liver disease, caudal vena cava thrombosis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, neoplasia

**Exudate** (3000 cells/mcL preferred cutoff in dogs):
- Infectious: bacterial, viral, fungal, protozoal
- Non-infectious: pancreatitis, neoplasia, bile, urine, foreign body
- Most cases of peritonitis → exudate

**Chylous effusion** (uncommon: 6.7% of cat peritoneal effusions, rare in dogs):
- Neoplasia (especially carcinoma) most common; cats also: cardiovascular disease, FIP, biliary cirrhosis, liver steatosis, lymphatic rupture/leakage; dogs also: right-sided heart failure, lymphatic obstruction/rupture

**Hemorrhagic effusion (hemoperitoneum):**
- Cats: evenly distributed neoplastic (46%) vs. non-neoplastic (54%); liver disease most common; hemangiosarcoma (18/30 cats) most common neoplastic cause; coagulopathies and liver necrosis most common non-neoplastic (each 23%)
- Dogs: neoplasia most common; splenic hemorrhage more frequent in large dogs (61.3%) vs. small dogs (43.2%); anaphylaxis-related hemoperitoneum reported
- Peritoneal cPLI: 100% sensitive, 94.7% specific for acute pancreatitis in dogs

**Uroabdomen:** Rupture of urinary tract; most common site = bladder, then urethra; commonest cause = blunt trauma; others: urethral obstruction, traumatic bladder expression/catheterization, bladder neoplasia, post-operative leakage

**Biliary effusion:** Disruption of biliary tract (trauma, cholelithiasis, necrotizing cholecystitis, neoplasia); anicteric gallbladder rupture can occur in dogs — measure bile acids in effusion (40-fold higher vs. serum in one case)

**Neoplastic effusion:** Most common cause of peritoneal effusion overall in small animals

#### Additional Diagnostic Tests on Effusion
- Glucose and lactate (septic peritonitis)
- Flow cytometry and PARR (neoplasia if cytology negative)
- Rivalta's test + RT-PCR (FIP)
- Peritoneal cPLI (pancreatitis)

### Ch 22 — Failure to Grow
*(Author: Hans S. Kooistra)*

#### Definition
Failure to grow = not growing at the anticipated rate or to a normal extent.

**Linear growth:** Primarily occurs during first 6–9 months; can continue to ~2 years in some dogs. Halts when growth plates close.

#### Three Major Categories of Causes
1. Inadequate intake of calories/nutrients
2. Increased use of calories/nutrients
3. Loss of energy generated by consumed calories/nutrients

#### Endocrine Causes

**Growth Hormone (GH) and IGF-1 Deficiency:**
- GH deficiency → "proportionate dwarfism" (all body components equally small); growth at ~1/3–1/4 normal rate
- Pituitary dwarfism in Shepherd dogs: usually caused by LHX3 gene mutation (DNA test available); genetic testing enables identification of carriers
- Diagnosis: low serum IGF-1 (indirect measure); absence of GH increase after IV GH-releasing hormone
- Note: breed-specific IGF-1 reference ranges needed; basal GH concentrations similar between breeds

**Juvenile Hypothyroidism:**
- Thyroid hormone absolutely required for normal growth
- Deficiency → delayed epiphyseal closure, retarded epiphyseal growth, reduced long bone growth, "disproportionate dwarfism" (relatively short limbs vs. trunk); skeletal growth depends on thyroid hormone (chondrogenesis synergy with GH and IGF-1)
- Often symmetrical truncal alopecia; mental dullness
- Diagnosis: low serum T4 + elevated TSH; TSH stimulation test to confirm if TSH not elevated
- Note: delayed epiphyseal closure → chondrodystrophy must be differentiated

**Insulin Deficiency (Juvenile Diabetes Mellitus):**
- Anabolic actions of insulin (protein synthesis, cell division) → growth retardation when deficient
- Energy loss in urine → increased appetite but inadequate weight gain

**Glucocorticoid Excess:**
- Catabolism (gluconeogenesis, ↑protein breakdown, lipolysis); suppresses pituitary GH secretion via somatostatin
- Long-term exogenous glucocorticoids in growing animals → growth retardation

**Gonadal Steroid Excess:**
- Administration of androgens or estrogens at early age → stunted growth (premature growth plate closure)
- Pre-pubertal gonadectomy may result in taller individuals (delayed closure)

**Other Endocrine Deficiencies:**
- Hypoadrenocorticism: Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever predisposed to early onset; → ↓appetite, vomiting, diarrhea → insufficient nutrients
- Hypoparathyroidism: PTH important for bone metabolism → growth retardation
- Central diabetes insipidus: severe polydipsia → impaired appetite → growth failure

#### Non-Endocrine Causes

**Nutrition:** Inadequate caloric intake; selective vitamin/mineral deficiencies

**Oropharyngeal, GI, Pancreatic Exocrine:**
- Oropharyngeal disorders → hyporexia
- Vascular ring anomaly (persistent right aortic arch): stunted growth + chronic regurgitation (especially after weaning)
- Maldigestion (EPI/exocrine pancreatic insufficiency)
- Malabsorption (intestinal wall disorders)
- Intestinal parasites (puppies and kittens)

**Inflammation, Heart, Kidney, Liver:**
- Congenital heart anomalies/endocarditis → tachycardia → excess energy expenditure → fewer calories for growth
- Both congenital and acquired kidney disorders → growth stunting
- Portosystemic shunting/hepatic encephalopathy: most common liver disorder causing growth failure; hepatitis and glycogen storage disease also associated
- Chronic anemia → impaired O2 delivery + increased cardiovascular effort
- Glucosuria: most commonly in juvenile diabetics (also seen in abnormal renal proximal tubules)
- Protein-losing enteropathies and persistent proteinuria decrease growth potential

**Skeletal/Genetic:**
- Chondrodystrophic breeds: angular limb formation/short limbs (intentional selection — not considered a problem)
- Oculoskeletal dysplasia: autosomal recessive in Labrador Retrievers, Samoyed, Northern Inuit dogs
- Mucopolysaccharidoses (storage diseases): crouching gait, paresis/paralysis; metachromatic granules in leukocytes on blood smear
- Osteogenesis imperfecta

**Nutritional Imbalances:**
- Hypovitaminosis D (rickets in growing animals)
- Copper deficiency
- Calcium or phosphorus deficiencies
- Hypervitaminosis A (cats on liver diets): bone exostoses, enthesiophytes, joint laxity, nerve impingement

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 22.1)

**History and Physical Examination:**
- Regurgitation since weaning → vascular ring anomaly or megaesophagus
- Diarrhea/voluminous stools → maldigestion/malabsorption
- PU/PD → renal, hepatic, DM, DI
- Mental dullness → hypothyroidism or hepatic/renal encephalopathy
- Symmetrical truncal alopecia → GH deficiency or hypothyroidism
- **Body proportions:** Short limbs vs. trunk → hypothyroidism or chondrodystrophy; proportionate dwarfism → GH deficiency
- Overweight for height → more likely endocrine (GH deficiency, hypothyroidism)
- Underweight with stunted growth → malnutrition or systemic disorder
- Cardiac: tachycardia, heart murmur → cardiac anomaly
- Ocular changes in dwarfed dog → oculoskeletal dysplasia

**Laboratory Evaluation:**
- Multiple fecal exams (Giardia, parasites; also assess digestion/absorption)
- Urinalysis: proteinuria, glucosuria, hyposthenuria, pyelonephritis sediment
- CBC: anemia, inflammation, eosinophilia (parasitism or hypoadrenocorticism)
- Serum biochemistry: creatinine, urea (renal); liver enzymes, bile acids, NH3 (hepatic); glucose (DM); Na/K (hypoadrenocorticism); total protein (protein-losing conditions)
- Serum TLI ↓ → EPI confirmed (in fasted dog)
- Cobalamin and folate → intestinal malabsorption

**Specific Endocrine Tests:**
- Low IGF-1 → GH deficiency; confirm with absence of GH response to IV GH-releasing hormone
- Low T4 + elevated TSH → hypothyroidism; TSH stimulation test if TSH not elevated
- ACTH stimulation test → hypoadrenocorticism

**Imaging:** Skeletal radiographs; abdominal ultrasound (structural abnormalities, portosystemic shunting); US-guided biopsies for renal/liver disease

**Genetic Testing:** Available for LHX3 mutation (pituitary dwarfism in Shepherd dogs) and oculoskeletal dysplasia

### Ch 23 — Weakness
*(Author: Sean E. Hulsebosch)*

#### Introduction
Weakness is common and its DDx is vast. Clinician must discriminate true weakness (lack of strength) from apparent weakness due to pain/lameness or blindness.

**Useful client descriptors:** Lethargy, listlessness, lack of energy, slow, difficulty rising, dull, cannot complete typical walk, collapse

**Persistent vs. episodic:** Severe infection, inflammation, neoplasia, chronic disease → persistent. Hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, bleeding tumors (hemangiosarcoma), post-prandial hepatic encephalopathy → episodic.

#### Mechanisms and Causes of Weakness

**HEMATOLOGIC**
- Anemia: severe anemia causes pallor; if hematocrit <20% (dogs) / <15% (cats) → tachycardia or physiologic heart murmur
- Regenerative anemia: blood loss (↓total solids, non-pigmented plasma) vs. hemolysis (normal total solids, icteric/hemolyzed plasma)
  - Spherocytes (dogs) or auto-agglutination → immune-mediated hemolysis
  - Heinz bodies → oxidative damage
  - Schistocytes → microangiopathic hemolysis
  - Hemoparasites → certain hemolytic anemias
- Non-regenerative anemia: primary bone marrow disease OR secondary to systemic disease
  - BM disorders: myelofibrosis, myelodysplasia, PIMA, PRCA, aplastic anemia, BM infection (chronic ehrlichiosis, FeLV), round cell neoplasia
  - Systemic diseases: CKD, chronic inflammatory diseases, iron deficiency, hypothyroidism, hypoadrenocorticism, extra-medullary neoplasia

**METABOLIC AND ELECTROLYTE**
- Hypoglycemia → neuroglycopenia → weakness/stumbling → twitching → convulsions; causes: poor nutrition, insulinoma, hepatic failure, hypoadrenocorticism, exogenous insulin overdose
- Uremic encephalopathy (severe azotemia, uremic toxins, GABA-related mechanisms) → mental dullness and weakness
- Hepatic encephalopathy (hyperammonemia + endogenous toxins, especially post-prandial or with GI bleeding)
- Severe hypokalemia (>clinical threshold) → muscle weakness (cats: ventroflexion)
- Severe hyperkalemia (>7 mmol/L) → bradycardia, decreased cardiac output, weakness
- Hypercalcemia (any cause) → hyperpolarization → weakness
- Hypocalcemia → altered nerve excitability → weakness/muscle cramping/stiffness/lameness

**ENDOCRINE (DOGS)**
- Hypothyroidism → lethargy, weight gain, mild non-regenerative anemia, hyperlipidemia
- Hypercortisolism (chronic) → appendicular muscle atrophy and weakness
- Hypoadrenocorticism → malnutrition, hypovolemia, hyperkalemia
- Hypoglycemia (insulinoma or exogenous insulin overdose)
- Hyperparathyroidism and hypoparathyroidism

**ENDOCRINE (CATS)**
- Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (~10% of diabetic cats): plantigrade stance, difficulty jumping
- Hyperthyroidism: weakness/difficulty jumping
- Hypokalemia: generalized muscular weakness, ventroflexion of neck
- Hyperthyroidism in cats: neuromuscular weakness, ventroflexion, plantigrade stance, exercise intolerance

**CARDIAC**
- Arrhythmias → poor cardiac output: bradyarrhythmias (AV block, sick sinus syndrome, atrial standstill); tachyarrhythmias (SVT, atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia)
- Left-sided CHF with pulmonary edema → hypoxia-induced weakness
- Arterial thromboembolism (cats: ~15% with cardiomyopathy): absent/decreased distal pulses, cyanotic nail beds; painful limbs may appear as weakness
- Pericardial effusion with tamponade → forward heart failure → weakness/collapse

**HEMODYNAMIC**
- Hypovolemia: severe dehydration, hemorrhage, third-space losses
- Distributive shock: sepsis, thromboembolic disease, GDV
- PE signs: dull mentation, pallor, delayed CRT, poor pulse quality, tachycardia/bradycardia (especially cats), hypothermia, core-extremity temperature difference, delayed skin turgor

**RESPIRATORY**
- Four mechanisms: hypoxia, hypercapnia, pulmonary hypertension, vasovagal syncope from coughing
- Hypoxemia (PaO2 <80 mmHg) or hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mmHg)
- Common hypoxemic diseases: laryngeal paralysis, severe tracheal collapse, airway foreign body, aspiration/bronchopneumonia, pulmonary thromboembolism
- Pulmonary hypertension: CHF, heartworm disease, chronic inflammatory lung disease, pulmonary fibrosis, PTE
- Vasovagal syncope: advanced airway collapse with paroxysmal coughing

**NEUROLOGIC**
- Peripheral neuropathies (unless peracute): flaccid limbs, altered sensation, severe muscle atrophy
- Neuromuscular junction disorders (myasthenia gravis): reflexes and sensation intact, minimal muscle atrophy, weakness worsens with exercise
- Myopathies: normal spinal reflexes/proprioception/sensation, ↑serum CK
- Central (brain/spinal cord) disorders: reduce voluntary limb movement; changes in mentation, CN deficits, seizures, ataxia

**ORTHOPEDIC**
- Pain on PE: fractures, diffuse OA, immune-mediated polyarthritis, septic arthritis, developmental bone/joint disorders, skeletal neoplasia

**TOXINS**
- Dull mentation, lethargy, ataxia, ptyalism, nausea, vomiting/diarrhea
- Common toxins: marijuana, opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, ethylene glycol (peracute), xylitol, alcohol, antidepressants, macrocyclic lactones

**MISCELLANEOUS**
- Severe obesity, malnutrition, late-term pregnancy, pyometra, sarcopenia, thiamine deficiency, medication side effects, sedation, post-anesthesia

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 23.1)
- History of intoxication → decontamination (ch. 132)
- PE abnormal:
  - Arrhythmia/murmur/gallop/muffled sounds → ECG, thoracic radiographs, echo
  - Respiratory distress/tachypnea/cyanosis/abnormal lung sounds → neck/thoracic radiographs, CBC, ABG
  - Abnormal perfusion → blood pressure, ECG, blood gas, fluid resuscitation
  - Pallor/icterus → CBC, chemistry, UA, ± radiographs, ultrasound
  - Neurologic deficits → referral for advanced imaging/neuromuscular testing
  - Lameness → radiographs ± arthrocentesis
- PE unremarkable → CBC, chemistry, UA, ± thyroid panel, ± bile acids, ± imaging; if still elusive → video episodes

Restlessness is often ignored or attributed to behavior without considering serious medical causes. Failure to consider medical causes may place pet at unnecessary risk.

- Antihistamines (especially cats — paradoxical)
- Digoxin
- Salicylates
- Benzodiazepines (excitatory phase)
- Dysphoria (drug-induced)
- Glucocorticoids (occasionally)
- Thyroid medication excess

*Various Other Toxic Substances:*
- Metaldehyde (snail bait)
- Pyrethrins
- Strychnine
- Nicotine
- Organophosphates/carbamates
- Recreational drugs (cocaine, amphetamines)
- Select mycotoxins (especially tremorgens from Penicillium spp.)
- Latrodectus (black widow spider) envenomation
- Bufo toad toxicosis

- Estrous cycle/mating
- Periparturient
- Pseudopregnancy
- Discomfort: pollakiuria, tenesmus

- Abdominal pain, muscular discomfort, orthopedic pain
- Feline hyperesthesia syndrome (multiple causes)

**Altered Mentation/Encephalopathy**

*Primary CNS Disease:*
- Epileptic aura (pre-ictal) — restlessness can be the seizure event
- Tumors (e.g., pituitary macroadenoma in PDH → wandering aimlessly)
- Inflammation
- Rabies/pseudorabies
- Canine cognitive dysfunction

*Metabolic Encephalopathies:*
- Hepatic encephalopathy (congenital PSS — clues: ↓albumin, ↓glucose, ↓BUN, ↓cholesterol; but some dogs with PSS may have normal chemistries, bile acids, and ammonia until 7–12 years of age)
- Hypoglycemia (insulin overdose, insulinoma, hepatic failure, hypoadrenocorticism)
- Hypocalcemia

**Increased Metabolic Rate**
- Hyperthyroidism (iatrogenic [excess thyroid medication] or spontaneous)
- Pheochromocytoma (paroxysmal episodes) — adrenal mass found on imaging

**Physiologic Distress**
- Shock
- Transfusion reaction
- Anaphylactic reaction
- Overhydration (iatrogenic)
- Dyspnea (esp. nocturnal restlessness in CHF, pleural effusion)
- Overheating
- Fever
- Pruritus

**Emotional Distress**
- Fear
- Stress from altered environment or blindness
- Pending natural calamity (earthquake)

**Pathologic Behavior**
- Various types of anxiety
- Projection of owner's personality on pet

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 24.1)
1. If restlessness has other localizing signs → address those first
2. If no localizing signs:
   - **Acute onset** → intoxication or drug effect first
   - **Chronic problem** → pain, CNS disease, metabolic encephalopathy, hypermetabolism
3. Workup (no localization): CBC, serum chemistry panel, abdominal imaging ± TT4, ± bile acids, ± blood ammonia
4. If no diagnosis → advanced imaging for PSS (contrast CT, nuclear scintigraphy) or intracranial disease (MRI)
5. Only after organic disease tentatively eliminated → pursue behavior problem + behaviorist (ch. 11)

### Ch 25 — Pallor
*(Author: Todd Cohen)*

#### Definition and Detection
Pallor = unusual or extreme paleness. Assessed via mucous membrane color (gums, conjunctiva, lips, tongue, inner pinnae; also vaginal/preputial/anal mucous membranes; occasionally paw pads).
- Dark pigmentation can obscure assessment
- Bilirubin, myoglobin accumulation in blood can also obscure evaluation
- Neonatal mucosa: dark due to high fetal MCV until ~6 weeks

**Normal CRT:** 1–2 seconds; test by pressing gently on maxillary gum above canine tooth

#### Generalized vs. Regional Pallor
- **Generalized:** Anemia (usually visible when hematocrit <25%) or cardiovascular disease
- **Regional:** Inadequate perfusion of specific area — e.g., aortic thromboembolism (hind limbs), right-to-left cardiac shunt (rear body pale; cranial body normal)
- Normal hematocrit varies by species and age: healthy young animals have lower hematocrits than adults

#### Causes of Pallor
**Anemia:**
- Regenerative: blood loss (↓PCV, ↓total solids, non-pigmented plasma) or hemolysis (normal TS, icteric/hemolyzed plasma, ± spherocytes, Heinz bodies, schistocytes, hemoparasites)
- Non-regenerative (pre-regenerative, BM disease, systemic disease)

**Cardiovascular Causes (normal PCV):**
- Hypovolemic shock: vasoconstriction maintains initial PCV — with time, fluid retention dilutes blood and pallor becomes apparent; precipitous PCV drop → pallor at higher hematocrit
- Severe tachycardia or bradycardia
- Pathologic tachyarrhythmias
- Cardiac tamponade (pericardial effusion with muffled heart sounds)
- Severe myocardial failure
- Various types of shock
- Pulmonary arterial hypertension
- Severe systemic hypotension

#### Physical Examination Algorithm (Figure 25.1)
- **Strong arterial pulse:**
  - Prolonged CRT + tacky MM + ↓skin turgor → dehydration
  - Normal CRT → measure PCV/TS:
    - Normal PCV → thorough cardiovascular evaluation (ECG, BP, thoracic radiographs, ± echo, ± pericardial/pleural US)
    - Anemia/↓PCV → determine regenerative vs. non-regenerative
- **Weak arterial pulse:**
  - Tachycardia → blood pressure → hypovolemia/shock; GI, urinary, or intra-cavitary blood loss
  - Bradycardia/dysrhythmia/pulse deficits → ECG
  - Muffled heart sounds → possible cardiac tamponade → confirm with cardiac US

#### Key Diagnostic Point
PCV and TS are NOT reliable in severe acute hemorrhage (vasoconstriction transiently maintains PCV). Serum color: icteric = hemolysis or hepatic disease; hemolyzed = evidence of intravascular hemolysis.

### Ch 26 — Hyperemia
*(Author: Anthony C.G. Abrams-Ogg)*

#### Definition
Hyperemia = increased blood within tissue vasculature.
- **Active hyperemia:** ↑arteriolar blood flow (normal response to exercise, inflammation) → erythema
- **Passive hyperemia:** Congestion (↓post-capillary blood removal) → does NOT cause erythema
- Chapter addresses GENERALIZED ACTIVE HYPEREMIA of peripheral mucous membranes

**Hyperemia visible in:** Gingiva, conjunctiva, genitalia; cats: less common (lower Hb, lower blood volume, sympathetic vasoconstriction in stress, gingivitis common)

**Key rule-outs before diagnosing hyperemia:**
- Local gingivitis/conjunctivitis (local, not generalized hyperemia)
- Erythrocytosis/polycythemia (blood smear/Hb will differentiate)
- Hemoglobinemia
- Oxyglobin™ treatment
- Carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning (experimentally cause bright red mucous membranes)
- Neonatal mucosa (<6 weeks)

#### Causes by Clinical Context (Figure 26.1)

**Animal examined for wellness/non-critical disorder:**
- Excitement (normal physiologic response — Dobermans, Golden Retrievers: waxing/waning color during PE)
- Heat stress
- Mast cell tumor (histamine release)
- Allergen exposure
- Breed-associated blushing (Pharaoh Hounds, Ibizan Hounds, Sphynx cats)

**Animal examined for flushing or recurrent flushing:**
- (Recurrent) heat stress/exercise
- Mast cell tumor (recurrent histamine release)
- Allergen exposure (recurrent)
- Breed-associated blushing

**Animal examined for excessive muscular activity:**
- Excessive muscular activity (status epilepticus, metaldehyde poisoning, respiratory work with polyradiculoneuritis)
- Seizure

**Animal examined for critical illness (shock):**
- Sepsis: hyperdynamic phase = fever + hyperemia + normal-to-fast CRT + tachycardia → may progress to hypodynamic (prolonged CRT → pallor → hypothermia)
- Heat stroke
- Hypoadrenocorticism: profound hyperemia + prolonged CRT; can mimic sepsis; cortisol deficiency → uninhibited NO synthesis → vasodilation; also ↓sodium:potassium ratio; absence of stress leukogram

**Animal examined for weakness, stupor, or ataxia:**
- Ethanol intoxication (peripheral vasodilation; may result from malicious/accidental poisoning, bread dough, treatment of ethylene glycol poisoning)
- Alpha-adrenergic receptor blocker ingestion (acepromazine, phenoxybenzamine, prazosin — NOT tamsulosin)
- Hypercapnia during anesthesia (rebreathing)

**Animal under anesthesia:**
- Acepromazine effect
- Hypercapnia (rebreathing)

#### CRT in Hyperemia
- Normal (0.5–2 sec) to fast (<0.5 sec): normal-to-increased cardiac output
- Prolonged (>2–3 sec) in hyperemic mucous membranes: severe vasodilation → risk of associated hypotension

#### Diagnostic Workup for Recurrent/Unexplained Flushing
1. Rule out mast cell tumor: thorough skin examination → FNA if mass found; if no mass → imaging + aspiration of spleen, liver, bone marrow

### Ch 27 — Cyanosis
*(Author: Jared A. Jaffey)*

#### Definition
Blue/dark discoloration of skin/mucosa caused by high concentrations of deoxygenated hemoglobin (>5 g/dL in capillary blood; =3 g/dL arterial).

**Detection threshold by Hb concentration:**
- Dogs (Hb 14–20 g/dL): cyanosis at SaO2 <79–85% (PaO2 <44–50 mmHg)
- Cats (Hb 11–16 g/dL): cyanosis at SaO2 <73–81% (PaO2 <39–45 mmHg)
- Formula: SaO2 at which cyanosis appears = 100 − ([3/Hb in g/dL] × 100)
- Severely anemic animals may NOT show cyanosis even at critically low SaO2; polycythemic animals show cyanosis at higher SaO2

**Key definitions:**
- **Hypoxemia:** SaO2 <95% or PaO2 <80 mmHg
- **Severe/life-threatening hypoxemia:** SaO2 <90% or PaO2 <60 mmHg
- Normal arterial oxygen tension (breathing room air, sea level): PaO2 80–100 mmHg

#### Classification and Causes

**CENTRAL CYANOSIS** (cyanosis on cutaneous + mucosal surfaces throughout body; low SaO2)

*Mechanism 1 — Reduced inspired oxygen tension (PiO2):*
- High altitude (compensatory mechanisms usually adequate)

*Mechanism 2 — Hypoventilation:*
- Normal alveolar-arterial O2 gradient (<10–15 mmHg) + hypercapnia + good response to O2 supplementation
- Causes: impaired central drive, cervical spinal cord injury, neuromuscular junction diseases, myopathies, severe abdominal distension, chest wall defects

*Mechanism 3 — V/Q Mismatch (MOST COMMON CAUSE):*
Pulmonary parenchymal diseases:
- Pneumonia
- Noncardiogenic edema
- Neoplasia
- Interstitial lung disease
- Hemorrhage

Airway diseases:
- Laryngeal paralysis
- Tracheal collapse
- Brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome
- Bronchomalacia
- Asthma (cats)

Pleural space:
- Effusion
- Mass/soft tissue
- Pneumothorax
- Diaphragmatic hernia

Cardiovascular:
- Pulmonary hypertension
- Pulmonary embolism
- Systemic hypotension

*Mechanism 4 — Diffusion Impairment:*
- Alveolar inflammation, interstitial lung disease, or edema; rarely primary cause at rest; amplifies when combined with V/Q mismatch

*Mechanism 5 — Right-to-Left Shunting:*
Intrapulmonary:
- Severe diffuse pneumonia
- Left-sided CHF
- Pleural space disease
- ARDS
- Complete intraluminal/extraluminal obstruction of trachea or mainstem/lobar bronchi
- Pulmonary arteriovenous malformation

Extrapulmonary (cyanotic congenital heart disease):
- Tetralogy of Fallot
- ASD or VSD with concurrent pulmonary hypertension
- Transposition of great vessels (rare)
- Eisenmenger syndrome: initially L→R shunt → develops ↑pulmonary vascular resistance → R→L reversal → cyanosis + secondary erythrocytosis
- Differential cyanosis (reverse patent ductus arteriosus): caudal body cyanotic; cranial body pink; often transient, more apparent with light exercise

**HEMOGLOBINOPATHY CAUSING CYANOSIS (normal PaO2):**
- Methemoglobinemia (MetHb >2–3% of total Hb) — most common hemoglobinopathy
  - Acquired (most common): exogenous oxidative toxins (acetaminophen, skunk musk, hydroxyurea, benzocaine, nitrites/nitrates) → acute onset cyanosis, concurrent Heinz-body hemolytic anemia, tissue hypoxia
  - Hereditary: cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency → persistent cyanosis, secondary erythrocytosis; clinical signs generally mild in absence of comorbidities

**Spot test for MetHb:** 1–3 drops blood on white filter paper — MetHb = chocolate brown; hypoxemic blood = dark red/violet turning bright red with O2 exposure

**ERYTHROCYTOSIS:** Secondary response to tissue hypoxia → ↑blood viscosity → impairs tissue perfusion → worsens cyanosis

**PERIPHERAL CYANOSIS** (normal SaO2; limited to distal extremities unless central also present):
- Arterial obstruction: arterial tumor embolism or thromboembolism
- Venous obstruction: thrombosis, iatrogenic external occlusion
- Erythrocytosis (viscosity)
- Hypothermia
- Hypotension

#### Diagnostics
- **Arterial blood gas:** Gold standard for PaO2 (not widely available)
- **Pulse oximetry (SpO2):** Noninvasive; normal >95%; tends to overestimate SaO2 at low ranges (<70–80%); affected by pigmentation, perfusion, severe anemia; MetHb → SpO2 reads 75–85%
- **Pulse CO-oximetry:** Multiple wavelengths; neutralizes effect of dyshemoglobins; also estimates Hb concentration
- **Delta-Glu:** Difference in blood glucose between systemic and peripheral affected limbs; cutoffs for arterial thromboembolism: cats ≥30 mg/dL (1.67 mmol/L), dogs ≥16 mg/dL (0.9 mmol/L)
- **Point-of-care NT-proBNP:** For differentiating cardiac vs. non-cardiac causes of respiratory distress in cats
- **Thoracic radiography:** Parenchymal disease, airway disease, pleural space, cardiac
- **Thoracic CT:** Pulmonary thromboembolism; respiratory causes of hypoxemia
- **Echocardiography:** Gold standard for structural cardiac disease; contrast echo/"bubble study" for R→L shunts

#### Management
- **O2 supplementation:** Immediate for all; NOT helpful for R→L shunting
- **Central cyanosis + respiratory distress:** Urgent intervention; see ch. 123
- **Heart failure:** Identify type → specific therapy
- **Pleural space disease:** Thoracentesis
- **Acquired MetHb:** Remove toxin + methylene blue + N-acetylcysteine or blood transfusion (O2 therapy of little use — MetHb cannot bind O2)
- **Hereditary MetHb:** Oral methylene blue for long-term management; no emergency therapy needed if uncomplicated

### Ch 28 — Jaundice
*(Author: Christina A. Bradbury)*

#### Definitions and Thresholds
- **Jaundice (icterus):** Yellow discoloration of sclera, skin, mucous membranes due to bilirubin deposition
- **Hyperbilirubinemia:** Serum bilirubin exceeds reference limits (does not always cause visible jaundice)
- **Jaundice visible when:** Serum bilirubin >2.5 mg/dL (>43 µmol/L)
- **Post-jaundice clearance:** Takes days (staining effect)

#### Bilirubin Metabolism
Senescent/damaged RBCs → mononuclear phagocytic system (spleen, liver, BM) → heme → biliverdin → bilirubin (unconjugated, lipophilic) → liver (passive diffusion + active transport) → conjugation (glucuronidation) → bile → intestines → urobilinogen/urobilin/stercobilinogen/stercobilin (brown feces pigment). Complete biliary obstruction → acholic (grey/clay-colored) feces.

#### Three Categories of Jaundice (Box 28.1 — Complete DDx)

**PRE-HEPATIC JAUNDICE (Hemolysis)**
- Immune-mediated: IMHA (most common in dogs); immune-mediated hemolysis in cats
- Infectious: Mycoplasma spp., Babesia spp., Cytauxzoon felis
- Heinz body anemia: Toxins/drugs (acetaminophen, onions, zinc), hepatic lipidosis in cats
- Hypophosphatemia
- Genetic disorders: Osmotic fragility syndrome (Abyssinian/Somali cats), phosphofructokinase deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency

**HEPATIC JAUNDICE**
*Infectious:*
- Viral: FIP, canine adenovirus-1 (hepatitis)
- Bacterial: Leptospira spp., rickettsial disease
- Protozoal: Toxoplasma gondii, Neospora caninum
- Fungal: histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis
- Parasitic: Heterobilharzia americana, visceral larval migrans

*Inflammatory/Immune-mediated disease*

*Toxins:*
- Amanita spp., aflatoxin, sago palm, cyanobacteria, xylitol

*Drugs:*
- Carprofen, acetaminophen, azathioprine, methimazole

*Neoplasia*

*Copper-associated hepatopathy* (Bedlington Terrier, Labrador Retriever, Dalmatian, others)

*Special cases:*
- Hepatic lipidosis (cats): common; follows rapid peripheral fat mobilization; Heinz body anemia possible; marked ↑ALP with normal GGT; obese jaundiced cats typical
- Sepsis in cats: mild hyperbilirubinemia (<2 mg/dL; rarely causes overt jaundice)

**POST-HEPATIC JAUNDICE**

*Intraluminal bile duct obstruction:*
- Mucocele (dogs >> cats)
- Cholelithiasis
- Inflammation/infection (common in cats)
- Stricture
- Neoplasia
- Parasitic: Platynosomum concinnum, Metorchis conjunctus, Eurytrema procyonis

*Extraluminal bile duct obstruction:*
- Pancreatitis
- Neoplasia
- Lymphadenopathy
- Duodenal foreign body at the papilla

**Clinical findings distinguishing categories:**
- Post-hepatic: moderate-to-marked hyperbilirubinemia, markedly ↑cholestatic enzymes (ALP, GGT), moderate-to-marked hypercholesterolemia, mild-to-moderate ↑ALT/AST
- Hepatic: moderate-to-marked ↑ALT/AST, variable cholestatic enzymes, abnormal liver function tests
- Feline hepatic lipidosis: markedly ↑ALP + mildly-to-markedly ↑ALT + normal GGT in obese jaundiced cat

#### Diagnostic Approach (Figure 28.1)
1. **Minimum database:** CBC, biochemistry (ALP, ALT, AST, GGT, cholesterol, BUN, albumin, glucose, bilirubin), urinalysis
2. **CBC findings:**
   - Hemolysis → macrocytic, hypochromic, regenerative anemia; leukocytosis; thrombocytopenia (IMHA or DIC)
   - Chronic liver disease → microcytic, normochromic, non-regenerative anemia
   - Heinz body anemia → cats with hepatic lipidosis
   - Spherocytosis + autoagglutination → IMHA
3. **Assess HCT/Retic:** If HCT <30% AND retic >60,000/µL → likely hemolytic
   - Spherocytes or agglutination → IMHA pathway
   - No spherocytes/agglutination → other hemolysis (Coombs', infectious screening, zinc radiographs)
4. **If no significant anemia:** Elevated liver enzymes → abdominal US (biliary disease/obstruction?)
   - Complete obstruction → surgical/interventional
   - No complete obstruction → liver cytology (FNA); if inconclusive → liver biopsy (histopathology, culture, copper quantification)
5. **Imaging:**
   - Abdominal radiographs: metallic foreign body (zinc), mass lesions, small liver, choleliths
   - Abdominal US: gallbladder (content, wall thickness), liver morphology, biliary tree, pancreas, mesentery, lymph nodes; splenomegaly common in hemolytic anemias
   - Indications for laparotomy: persistently dilated CBD, worsening hyperbilirubinemia, biliary mucocele, gallbladder rupture, cholelithiasis with obstruction

**Additional tests:**
- FeLV/FIV (cats)
- Travel history, tick exposure, vaccination history, medications/toxins
- Neurologic exam if hepatic encephalopathy suspected
- US-guided FNA liver: useful for neoplasia and hepatic lipidosis
- Liver biopsy: if imaging + cytology inconclusive
- US-guided cholecystocentesis: if cholangitis suspected → bile culture → guide antimicrobials
- Percutaneous US-guided biliary drainage for EHBDO secondary to pancreatitis
- Endoscopic biliary stenting for certain EHBDO

**Breed/Age associations:** Pyruvate kinase deficiency, copper storage disease → specific breeds; canine adenoviral hepatitis → unvaccinated puppies

### Ch 29 — Petechiae and Ecchymoses
*(Author: Marie-Claude Blais)*

#### Definitions
- **Petechiae:** Small (<3 mm), well-defined punctiform hemorrhagic lesion; superficial capillary bleeding
- **Purpura:** Coalesced petechiae (human term)
- **Ecchymosis:** Larger blood collection (often >1 cm); deeper dermal layers; small arterioles and venules
- **Blanching test:** Erythema disappears on finger pressure; petechiae/ecchymoses do NOT blanch

**Important:** Mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae/ecchymoses) strongly suggests PRIMARY hemostatic disorder. "Late bleeding" (hours to days after challenge) → secondary hemostatic disorder (coagulation factor deficiency).

**Risk of spontaneous bleeding:** Progressively increases as platelet count falls below 30,000/µL; however, not all ITP patients bleed and platelet count alone does not reliably predict bleeding.

#### Complete DDx

**THROMBOCYTOPENIA (Most Common Cause)**

*Immune-mediated thrombocytopenia (ITP) — most common acquired primary hemostatic disorder in dogs; seldom in cats:*
- Primary/idiopathic ITP: 57% of dogs with platelet count <50,000/µL
- Secondary ITP: 28% (neoplastic, inflammatory, infectious disease, drug exposure)
- Non-immune thrombocytopenia: 15% (BM aplasia, consumptive coagulopathy)

*DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation):*
- Wide variety of diseases: infection, inflammation, neoplasia, trauma
- Primarily microthrombi → morbidity/mortality; can progress to consumptive coagulopathy → petechiae, ecchymoses, overt bleeding

*Other causes of thrombocytopenia (rarely cause hemorrhage alone):*
- Neoplasia (mild-to-moderate thrombocytopenia in ~10% of all dogs with cancer)
- Decreased BM production: infectious agents, drugs, neoplasia, myelodysplasia, bone marrow disease (usually with other cytopenias)
- Sequestration: splenomegaly, pulmonary sequestration (sepsis/endotoxemia)
- Loss: massive hemorrhage

**THROMBOPATHIA (Platelet Dysfunction)**

*Congenital (breed-specific):*
- German Shepherd: Scott syndrome, kindlin-3 mutation
- Grey Collie: cyclic hematopoiesis
- American Cocker Spaniel: dense granule defects
- Great Pyrenées and Otterhound: Glanzmann thrombasthenia
- Greater Swiss Mountain Dog: P2Y12 defect
- Basset Hound and Spitz: intrinsic platelet defect
- Excessive bleeding during permanent tooth eruption → first sign of congenital thrombopathia

*Acquired thrombopathia:*
- Uremia
- Liver disease
- Neoplasia
- Infectious disease (e.g., rickettsial infections)
- Aspirin, clopidogrel (intentional alteration; rarely spontaneous hemorrhage)
- Other drugs with limited clinical relevance: certain NSAIDs, antibiotics, hydroxyethyl starch, chondroprotective agents, omega fatty acids

**VON WILLEBRAND DISEASE (vWD)**
- Most common inherited bleeding disorder in dogs; reported in >50 breeds
- Particularly high prevalence in Doberman Pinschers (type 1)
- Type 1: partial quantitative deficiency; Type 2: rare qualitative defect; Type 3: most severe, virtual absence of vWF
- Bleeding: nose, vulva, urinary bladder, oral mucosa; less often subcutaneous hematomas, hemarthrosis; evident during surgery or trauma
- Cats: extremely rare

**VASCULAR DISORDERS**
- Vasculitis: immune-mediated (primary or secondary to medications, infections, neoplasia)
- Early signs: petechiae, ecchymoses, edema → skin necrosis, ulcerations, pain, systemic signs

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 29.3)
1. **Platelet count (manual)** — always first test; confirm automated thrombocytopenia on stained blood smear
   - ITP dogs typically have <20,000 platelets/µL (even more severe than secondary thrombocytopenia)
   - Rough estimate: platelets per HPF × 15,000–20,000/µL
   - **High MPV (>12 fL):** 96% PPV for normal/hyperplastic BM megakaryocytes → supports ITP (low/normal MPV does not exclude)
2. **Thrombocytopenia only (no anemia):** Search for underlying disease; bone marrow aspirate if poor response or suspected marrow disease
3. **Thrombocytopenia + anemia:**
   - Reticulocyte count
   - Evidence of regeneration without blood loss → hemolysis: blood smear (spherocytes, ghosts, parasites), saline agglutination test, direct antiglobulin test; hyperbilirubinemia, hemoglobinemia
   - Positive → Evans syndrome (IMHA + ITP) or other combined cause; Search for underlying disease
   - Lack of regeneration ± leukopenia → DIC; coagulation tests (aPTT, PT, D-dimers, fibrinogen)
   - BM evaluation: estrogen level (dog), FIV-FeLV testing (cat)
4. **Normal/increased platelet count:**
   - Buccal mucosal bleeding time (BMBT)
   - Prolonged BMBT → vWF antigen assay first
     - Low vWF → vWD
     - Normal vWF → PFA-100 / platelet aggregometry
       - Normal → vascular disorder (skin biopsy)
       - Abnormal → thrombopathia (special platelet studies, genetic testing for breed-specific defects)

**Underlying disease workup for ITP/DIC:** Serum biochemistry, urinalysis, vector-borne disease serologic testing and/or PCR, thoracic + abdominal imaging

**ITP treatment considerations:**
- Cornerstone: immunosuppressive therapy
- Add doxycycline while awaiting vector-borne disease screening (based on geography)
- Discontinue causative medications
- Surgical/chemotherapy for neoplasia-associated cases
- Platelet transfusions: only for life-threatening bleeding (especially CNS or pulmonary hemorrhage) or surgical prophylaxis (rapidly destroyed in ITP)
- Lyophilized canine platelets commercially available
- cryoprecipitate (ideal) or fresh frozen plasma for vWD
- Thrombopoietin-mimetic drugs: investigational

### Ch 30 — Peripheral Edema
*(Author: Jessica Ward)*

#### Pathophysiology (Starling equation)
Q = Kf ([Pc – Pi] – σ [πc – πi])
Peripheral edema occurs when compensatory mechanisms (interstitial matrix tension, lymphatic uptake) are overwhelmed.

#### Complete Mechanisms and DDx (Table 30.1)

**GENERALIZED PERIPHERAL EDEMA:**

*Decreased Plasma Oncotic Pressure (hypoalbuminemia; albumin <2.0 g/dL [<20 g/L] required for overt edema without other factors):*
- Protein-losing nephropathy
- Protein-losing enteropathy
- Hepatic failure
- Malnutrition

*Increased Intravascular Hydrostatic Pressure:*
- Right-sided congestive heart failure (rare in small animals — almost always with cavitary effusions)

*Increased Vascular Permeability:*
- Sepsis/SIRS
- Anaphylaxis (drug/toxin)
- Myxedema (hypothyroidism — combination mechanism: ↑vascular permeability + hypoproteinemia + ↓lymphatic drainage; causes non-pitting edema)

*Lymphatic Dysfunction:*
- Lymphedema — Congenital (lymph node hypoplasia/aplasia; rare; typically presents within first weeks/months of life; hindlimb most common)
- Acquired (neoplasia — lymphoma or lymphangiosarcoma most common; also prior surgery, trauma, radiation therapy)

**LOCALIZED PERIPHERAL EDEMA:**

*Increased Intravascular Hydrostatic Pressure:*
- Venous obstruction:
  - Cranial vena cava syndrome (forelimb + ventral cervical/thoracic edema): extraluminal (cranial mediastinal mass) or intraluminal (thrombosis, stricture, pacemaker lead)
  - Caudal vena cava obstruction/"Budd-Chiari syndrome" (hindlimb + ventral caudal abdomen edema): caudal abdominal masses or intraluminal thrombosis/neoplastic invasion
- Arteriovenous fistula (focal area of vascular malformation; associated with focal palpable swelling and bruit)
- Trauma/surgery

*Increased Vascular Permeability:*
- Hypersensitivity (allergic) reaction
- Envenomation
- Thermal/chemical burns
- Trauma/surgery
- Myxedema
- Sterile inflammatory dermatopathies (Wells-like and Sweet-like syndromes)
- Various bacterial and parasitic infectious diseases (localized to site of infection)

*Lymphatic Dysfunction:*
- Lymphedema (congenital or acquired)
- Lymphangiosarcoma
- Trauma/surgery
- Previous radiation therapy

**Pitting vs. non-pitting edema:**
- **Pitting:** Fluid displaced within interstitium; persistent depression after digital pressure; most mechanisms listed above
- **Non-pitting:** Fluid within cells, clotted fibrinogen, angioedema (deeper subdermal layers), post-surgical/traumatic, lymphangiosarcoma, myxedema; chronic lymphedema → non-pitting (collagen deposition, interstitial fibrosis)

#### Diagnostic Algorithm (Figure 30.1)
**Generalized edema:**
1. Serum albumin <1.5–2.0 g/dL → consider PLN, PLE, hepatic failure; test: CBC, chem, UA, UPC, bile acids, ammonia
2. Serum albumin >2.0 g/dL:
   - Fever, circulatory shock → SIRS/sepsis, vasculitis; test: CBC, chem, UA, thoracic/abdominal imaging, infectious disease testing
   - Hypothyroid (↓T4) → consider myxedema; test: CBC, chem, UA, thyroid panel, imaging
   - Jugular venous distension, murmur, arrhythmia → consider R-CHF; test: echocardiogram

**Localized edema:**
1. History of envenomation/vaccination/trauma/burns/drugs/toxins → hypersensitivity
2. Primarily forelimbs → cranial mediastinal mass or cranial vena cava obstruction; test: thoracic radiographs, US, CT
3. Primarily hindlimbs → caudal abdominal mass or caudal vena cava obstruction; test: abdominal radiographs, US, CT
4. Warm swelling with bruit → arteriovenous fistula; test: US, angiogram
5. Non-pitting, single limb → consider lymphedema or lymphangiosarcoma; aspirate/biopsy, CBC, chem; lymphatic imaging if needed
6. Non-pitting with signs of infection → cellulitis (aspirate area for organisms/inflammatory cells)

### Ch 31 — Orthopedic Manifestations of Systemic Disease
*(Author: Bing Yun Zhu)*

#### Overview
Dogs/cats with systemic disease may present for lameness, abnormal gait, difficulty rising, or reduced jumping — not "illness." Empirical medications can be contraindicated (immunosuppressives for infectious conditions; NSAIDs may require washout before steroids).

#### Differential Diagnoses (Organized by Tissue)

**DISEASES OF BONE**

*Degeneration/Tissue Injury/Death:*
- Fractures: trauma or secondary to weakened bone (osteoporosis)
- Osteomyelitis: bacterial (hematogenous, direct inoculation, post-surgical); fungal (blastomycosis, systemic aspergillosis, cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis)
- Panosteitis: medullary adipocyte disease with eosinophilic granular degeneration; young dogs, medium/large/giant breeds; lameness, difficulty rising, pain on long bone palpation

*Disorders of Cell Growth:*
- Primary osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, hemangiosarcoma, myeloma
- Metastatic bone disease: mammary carcinoma, urinary tract carcinomas (incl. prostate), lymphoma, melanoma, mast cell tumor
- Benign: osteochondromas, bone cysts, multiple cartilaginous exostoses

*Systemic Diseases Causing Abnormal Skeletal Development:*
- Pituitary dwarfism (GH/IGF-1 deficiencies)
- Congenital hypothyroidism (disproportionate dwarfism)
- Nutritional imbalances: hypovitaminosis D (rickets), copper deficiency, calcium/phosphorus deficiencies
- Hypervitaminosis A (cats on liver diets): bone exostoses, enthesiophytes, joint laxity, nerve impingement
- Mucopolysaccharidoses: inherited storage diseases; crouching gait, paresis/paralysis; metachromatic granules in blood smear leukocytes

*Primary Bone Development Diseases:*
- Osteogenesis imperfecta (↑fracture susceptibility)
- Osteochondrodysplasia syndromes
- Physeal dysplasia in cats → slipped capital femoral epiphyses
- Hypertrophic osteodystrophy (HOD): idiopathic, young large-breed dogs, metaphyses of long bones
- Hypertrophic osteopathy (HO): painful periosteal reaction along appendicular bones; secondary to pulmonary neoplasia or systemic infections

*Vascular Disturbances:*
- Avascular femoral head necrosis (young small breed dogs)
- Medullary bone infarcts (rare; neoplasia or surgery)
- Disuse osteoporosis (within weeks of immobility)
- Osteoporosis: also vitamin D deficiency (inadequate intake or malabsorptive conditions)

*Diffuse Bone Abnormalities (systemic causes):*
- Primary hyperparathyroidism: ↑osteoclastic activity
- Nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism
- Renal secondary hyperparathyroidism (CKD)

**DISEASES OF JOINTS**

*Degenerative:*
- DJD: conformational + ligamentous degeneration; cranial cruciate ligament rupture; patellar luxation; secondary to hypothyroidism or acromegaly

*Inflammation — Septic Arthritis:*
- Single proximal joint (stifle, elbow, shoulder, hip) → highest suspicion for septic
- Hematogenous spread or direct inoculation (cat nail, bite wound, foreign body)
- Infections: systemic fungal (coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, cryptococcosis, sporotrichosis, aspergillosis), rickettsiae (RMSF, ehrlichiosis, anaplasmosis), spirochetes (Lyme disease), viruses (feline calicivirus, coronavirus), protozoal (leishmaniasis, hepatozoonosis, babesiosis)
- Culture sensitivity disappointingly low; blood culture and serology may add value

*Inflammation — Sterile Immune-mediated Polyarthritis:*
- Usually multiple distal joints (carpus and tarsus); young-to-middle-aged female dogs; non-erosive
- Primary/autoimmune or secondary to infectious/neoplastic diseases
- Drug-induced: trimethoprim-sulfur (especially Dobermans), penicillins, cephalosporins
- Juvenile-onset polyarthritis of Akitas; familial swollen hock syndrome (Chinese Shar-Peis)
- Concurrent SRMA + IMPA: commonly in large-breed dogs
- Rheumatoid arthritis (dogs): erosive; anti-IgA/IgG/IgM (RF) complexes; progressive cartilage/bone destruction

*Pigmentation/Tissue Deposits:*
- Systemic amyloidosis (Shar-Pei dogs)
- Calcinosis circumscripta: calcium salt deposition in soft tissue including joints; idiopathic, secondary to neoplasia, rarely CKD (usually footpads in CKD dogs/cats)

*Vascular — Hemarthrosis:*
- Any systemic bleeding disorder (thrombocytopenia, hemophilia, rodenticide toxicity)

**DISEASES OF MUSCLE**

*Degeneration/Atrophy/Dysfunction:*
- Glucocorticoid or thyroid hormone excess → muscle catabolism (within weeks with glucocorticoid treatment)
- Hypokalemia → generalized weakness via altered membrane electrical activity
- Hypocalcemia → painful muscle cramping/rigidity ("tetany") — misinterpreted as weakness
- Hypercalcemia → mild weakness
- Myolysis: rhabdomyolysis from exertion or snake envenomation myolysins
- Primary muscular dystrophies (rare)

*Inflammation:*
- Protozoal myositis: Toxoplasma gondii, Neospora caninum, Hepatozoon canis, Cytauxzoon felis, Leishmania

*Neoplasia:* Soft tissue sarcoma, hemangiosarcoma, histiocytic sarcoma, leiomyoma

*Vascular — Thromboembolic Disease:*
- Cats: aortic thromboembolism → cool affected limbs, reduced pulse quality
- Small vessel emboli in distal extremities (dogs or cats)

**DISEASES OF NERVES**
- Diabetic neuropathy in cats
- Hypothyroid-associated neuropathies
- Insulinoma or other neoplasia-induced neuropathy
- Hypo- and hyperkalemia
- Hypo- and hypercalcemia

**OTHER CAUSES OF "ORTHOPEDIC SIGNS"**
- Prostatic disease (infection or cancer): painful; can spread to vertebral column
- Paw pad disease: superficial necrolytic dermatitis, plasma cell pododermatitis → appear as lameness

#### Diagnostic Approach
1. History (breed, age, sex, travel, ticks, seasonal, medications)
2. PE: gait observation before exam; full orthopedic + neurologic exam; proprioceptive placement reflexes; fundic exam (hypertension, fungal, infectious)
3. Bone/joint: radiography first; arthrocentesis for joint cytology ± culture
4. CBC, biochemistry, UA, urine culture if systemic signs
5. Peripheral lymph node aspiration if enlarged
6. Infectious disease screening before immunosuppressives
7. Assess renal function before NSAIDs

### Ch 32 — Swollen Joints and Joint Pain
*(Author: Jonathan David Dear)*

#### Key Distinctions
- **DJD/degenerative arthropathy:** Intermittent/shifting lameness; otherwise healthy; lacks systemic signs; affects few joints; larger proximal joints more often
- **Inflammatory joint disease:** NOT typically "lame"; lethargy, ↓appetite, fever; affects many joints; distal joints (carpus, tarsus) more common in immune-mediated
- **Monoarthropathy** vs. **polyarthropathy** (number of joints affected)

#### Joint Fluid Analysis
**Normal:** Clear, viscous, low cellularity, <3000 cells/µL, large mononuclear "housekeeper" cells, strings 4–5 cm on viscosity test
**IMPA:** Individual joint counts >50,000/µL; primarily non-degenerate neutrophils
**Septic:** Similar neutrophil counts + degenerate neutrophils + intracellular organisms + positive culture (low sensitivity)

#### Septic Arthritis
- Single proximal joint most common → suspect especially with post-operative, trauma, or penetrating wound history
- Hematogenous bacteremia: endocarditis (incl. Bartonella spp.), discospondylitis, pyelonephritis, prostatitis → blood/urine cultures + imaging
- Culture joint + blood + urine; can lavage or treat empirically with antimicrobials while awaiting cultures
- In cats: add Mycoplasma culture or PCR (non-degenerate joint inflammation despite infection; difficult to culture by conventional methods)

#### Erosive Polyarthritis
**Rheumatoid Arthritis (dogs):**
- Anti-IgA/IgG/IgM antibodies (RF); immune complexes in synovium → intense inflammation → progressive cartilage and bone destruction over months-years
- RF neither sensitive nor specific for diagnosis

**Feline Periosteal Proliferative Polyarthritis:**
- Uncommon; male cats; periosteal proliferation + subchondral osteolysis → ankylosis
- Cytology: aseptic neutrophilic → lymphoplasmacytic
- Radiographic: intense periosteal proliferation, trabecular patterns, subchondral bone destruction
- FeLV implicated in pathogenesis

#### Nonerosive IMPA (Most Common Inflammatory Joint Disease)
**Type III hypersensitivity reaction.** Chronic antigenic stimulation, source rarely identified.

**Inciting causes (when identified):**
- Neoplasia (particularly hemic neoplasia)
- Chronic infection (deep mycoses, discospondylitis, endocarditis)
- Primary immune disease (SLE)
- Drug administration (especially trimethoprim-sulfonamide [TMS] in Doberman Pinschers; also penicillins, cephalosporins)
- Vaccines (canine distemper, Lyme; within 1–2 weeks of vaccination, occasionally longer)
- Juvenile-onset polyarthritis of Akitas
- Familial swollen hock syndrome (Chinese Shar-Peis)
- Enteropathic polyarthropathy (acute or chronic GI inflammatory disease)
- Concurrent SRMA + IMPA (large-breed dogs)

**Idiopathic/primary IMPA:** No inciting cause found

#### Other Causes
- **Hemarthrosis:** Thrombocytopenia or other bleeding disorders → monoleg or multileg lameness
- **Synovial/joint neoplasia:** Lymphoma, histiocytic sarcoma (can affect joint + articulating bone/cartilage)

#### Diagnostic Workup
1. **Arthrocentesis:** Most important diagnostic aid; multiple distal joints (carpi, tarsi) under heavy sedation/anesthesia, aseptic technique; individual samples for cytology; pool remaining for culture
2. CBC, biochemistry, UA with culture
3. FIV and FeLV testing (ALL cats with polyarthritis)
4. Serologic testing: Ehrlichia canis, Borrelia burgdorferi, Anaplasma phagocytophilum, systemic mycoses (geographic basis)
5. Thoracic + abdominal imaging (rule out neoplasia and other diseases)
6. When erosive disease suspected: radiographs of affected + contralateral joints
7. Additional: blood cultures, echocardiogram, vertebral radiographs, CSF analysis (case by case)
8. Serum C-reactive protein: usually elevated in uncontrolled IMPA; differentiates IMPA from DJD
9. Immune markers: Coombs' test, ANA, RF — bolster suspicion but not definitive; sensitivity/specificity controversial
10. Blood cultures if bacteremia suspected

**Idiopathic/primary IMPA:** Diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out all causes above

### Ch 33 — Body Odors
*(Author: Darren Berger)*

#### Clinical Framework
Malodor in a pet is perceived relative to the individual owner's baseline; 89% of owners can distinguish their dog's odor from others. Three categories:
1. **Normal pet** (client education needed)
2. **Normal pet with obvious non-medical cause**
3. **Pet with underlying medical disease causing malodor**

**Complete physical exam including smelling patient** (rostral muzzle to tail); attention to: ear canals, facial folds, mandibular lip folds, areas around collars/bandages, interdigital areas, perivulvar/perianal regions

**Normal odors:** Sebaceous/ceruminous/anal gland secretions + bacterial/fungal decomposition → unsaturated/aromatic fatty acids. Glandular density highest near mucocutaneous junctions, paws, dorsal neck, rump, and tail segments.

**Signalment factors affecting normal odor:** Hound/spaniel family (muskier); oily coats (Labrador); intact male cats vs. castrated; animals with excessive skin folds (English Bulldog); heavy droolers (Mastiff, Saint Bernard, Newfoundland)

#### Non-Medical Causes
- Failure to properly dry coat (frequent swimmers, heavy droolers)
- Poor coat maintenance (Old English Sheepdogs, corded coats)
- Excessive anal sac expression (behavior, diet)
- Rolling/rubbing in odiferous environmental material
- Wildlife interaction (skunks — days to weeks to fully eliminate odor)

#### Medical Causes — Algorithm (Figure 33.4)

**Focal odor from single location:**
- Trauma: laceration, poorly maintained bandage
- Halitosis
- Flatulence
- Focal infection
- Myiasis
- Anal sac abscess
- Perianal fistulas (German Shepherd Dogs typical)
- Otitis externa
- Neoplasia
- Toxin exposure

**Generalized odor with signs of systemic disease (urine/fecal soiling):**
- Arthritis (reduced mobility → poor self-grooming)
- Neuromuscular disease (loss of mobility/continence)
- Spinal cord disease (incontinence)
- Incontinence (any cause)
- Poor grooming in cats: diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, FIV infection
- Demodicosis
- Autoimmune skin disease

**Generalized odor with pyoderma or Malassezia dermatitis:**
- Allergic hypersensitivity: atopy, cutaneous adverse food reaction, parasitic hypersensitivity
- Endocrinopathy: hypothyroidism, hyperadrenocorticism
- Keratinization disorder: ichthyosis, feline acne
- Immune suppression: iatrogenic or naturally occurring
- Miscellaneous: sebaceous adenitis

**Notable metabolic/systemic odors:**
- Diabetic ketoacidosis: ketone odor (nail polish remover or fruity; only percentage of people can detect)
- Uremia: urinous or ammoniacal odor
- Liver disease: musty/hepatic fetor
