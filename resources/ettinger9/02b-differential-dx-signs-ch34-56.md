### Ch 34 — Cough
*(Author: Luca Ferasin)*

#### Definitions and Classification
- **Acute cough**: duration <3 weeks (usually infectious/viral, self-limiting)
- **Sub-acute cough**: 3–8 weeks (often post-infective)
- **Chronic cough**: ≥8 weeks (requires full workup)
- **Productive/wet cough**: associated with expectoration of sputum
- **Dry/honking cough**: little to no phlegm; characteristic of tracheal collapse

#### Key pathophysiological point
Cardiogenic pulmonary edema does NOT typically cause cough (cough receptors absent from deep respiratory tract). Cardiomegaly with concurrent airway disease carries a 10-fold increased risk of cough; LAE alone has only a weak association. Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema also does not reliably cause cough.

#### Full Differential Diagnosis for Coughing (Table 34.1)
*H = high risk; M = moderate risk; L = low risk; X = not reported in species*

**Inflammatory**
- Rhinitis — Dog M, Cat M
- Sinusitis — Dog M, Cat M
- Pharyngitis — Dog L, Cat L
- Tonsillitis — Dog L, Cat L
- Laryngitis — Dog H, Cat H
- Tracheitis — Dog H, Cat H
- Bronchitis — Dog H, Cat H
- Pneumonia — Dog L, Cat L
- Bronchopneumonia — Dog H, Cat H
- Lung abscess — Dog M, Cat M

**Allergic**
- Asthma (with bronchospasm) — Dog X, Cat H
- Eosinophilic bronchopneumopathy — Dog H, Cat H

**Degenerative**
- Laryngeal paralysis — Dog M, Cat M
- Tracheal collapse — Dog H, Cat H
- Bronchomalacia — Dog H, Cat H
- Bronchiectasis — Dog H, Cat H

**Traumatic**
- Near-strangulation — Dog H, Cat H
- Near-drowning — Dog H, Cat H
- Inhaled foreign body — Dog H, Cat H
- Chest contusion — Dog M, Cat M

**Cardiovascular**
- Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema — Dog L, Cat L
- Cardiogenic pulmonary edema — Dog L, Cat L
- Cardiomegaly without concomitant airway disease — Dog L, Cat L
- Cardiomegaly WITH concomitant airway disease — Dog H, Cat L
- Pericardial effusion — Dog M, Cat L
- Pleural effusion — Dog M, Cat M
- Pulmonary thromboembolism — Dog M, Cat M

**Neoplastic**
- Laryngeal tumor — Dog H, Cat H
- Tracheal tumor — Dog H, Cat H
- Lung tumor — Dog M, Cat M
- Mediastinal tumor — Dog H, Cat H

**Gastrointestinal**
- Gastroesophageal reflux — Dog M, Cat L
- Tracheoesophageal fistula — Dog H, Cat H
- Bronchoesophageal fistula — Dog H, Cat H
- Dysphagia — Dog H, Cat H

**Parasitic Infections**
- Larval migration of intestinal nematodes — Dog M, Cat M
- Dirofilaria immitis (heartworm) — Dog M, Cat H
- Angiostrongylus vasorum (French heartworm) — Dog H, Cat X
- Aelurostrongylus abstrusus (cat lungworm) — Dog X, Cat M
- Fungal infections — Dog M, Cat M

**Iatrogenic**
- ACE inhibitors — Dog L, Cat X
- Beta-blockers — Dog L, Cat X

#### Diagnostic Approach
1. **Dyspnea present?** → Immediate: sedation, O₂, thoracic radiographs, bronchoscopy, rule out feline asthma, pneumonia, CHF
2. **Acute (<3 weeks)**: hemoptysis workup (thoracic radiographs, clotting tests, HWD test); if no haemoptysis and quality of life unaffected → monitor; if affected → anti-tussive trial
3. **Subacute (3–8 weeks)**: test for HWD/A. vasorum (endemic areas); post-infection cough (TTW or antibiotic trial); environmental triggers
4. **Chronic (>8 weeks)**: thoracic radiographs first; if diagnostic, treat; if not → check for rhinosinusitis (empirical PNDS treatment), GI signs (empirical GERD treatment), ACEi use (discontinuation trial 4 weeks if not CHF); non-pharmacological management
5. **Advanced tests**: fluoroscopy, thoracic CT, bronchoscopy + BAL, rhinoscopy, GI endoscopy, allergy testing

#### Non-pharmacological management
1. Eliminate cigarette smoke, dust, sprays; frequent vacuuming
2. Gentle long walks (not short intense runs); harness instead of collar
3. Weight reduction if obese
4. Humidification; cool-mist ultrasound humidifier overnight; gentle coupage

#### Pharmacological options (in brief)
1. Inhaled corticosteroids (fluticasone, beclomethasone, budesonide) — minimal absorption, use dedicated spacer with mask
2. Expectorants (guaifenesin, potassium iodide) — limited evidence in pets
3. Mucolytics (acetylcysteine, ambroxol, bromhexine) — for thick mucopurulent secretions
4. Cough suppressants (butorphanol, codeine, hydrocodone, dextromethorphan) — centrally acting; reserve for quality-of-life-affecting non-productive cough
5. Bronchodilators (inhaled salbutamol/albuterol, theophylline, aminophylline, terbutaline) — useful in feline asthma only; not convincingly demonstrated in dogs
6. Neuromodulators (gabapentin, baclofen) — evidence in humans; no data in pets yet

### Ch 35 — Sneezing and Nasal Discharge
*(Author: Julio López)*

#### Definitions
- **Stertor**: snorting/snoring sound indicating nasopharyngeal obstruction; resolves with open-mouth breathing
- **Reverse sneezing**: violent inspiratory snoring without warning; usually benign; investigate if new/increasing
- **Nasal discharge types**: serous (watery, can be normal; excessive → noninfectious inflammation or viral URI), mucopurulent (white/yellow/green; nonspecific), sanguineous (turbinate destruction, coagulopathy, hypertension), food-containing (cleft palate, oronasal fistula)

#### Laterality Rule
- **Unilateral**: foreign body, oronasal fistula, aspergillosis, neoplasia
- **Bilateral**: inflammatory, infectious, allergic

#### Full DDx — Box 35.1: Nasal/Paranasal Causes

**Congenital**
- Cleft palate
- Ciliary dyskinesia
- Nasopharyngeal stenosis
- Choanal atresia

**Inflammatory**

- Allergic rhinitis
- Nasopharyngeal stenosis
- Nasopharyngeal polyp (cats)
- Polypoid rhinitis
- Foreign body

**Infectious — Viral**
- Feline calicivirus (cats)
- Feline herpesvirus-1 (cats)

**Infectious — Bacterial**
- Mycoplasma spp. (cats)
- Bordetella bronchiseptica (cats)
- Pasteurella multocida (dogs)

**Infectious — Fungal**
- Aspergillus
- Penicillium (dogs)

- Cryptococcus (cats)

**Parasitic**
- Pneumonyssoides caninum (dogs)
- Eucoleus [Capillaria] boehmi
- Cuterebra sp.
- Linguatula sp.

**Neoplastic**
- Adenocarcinoma
- Squamous cell carcinoma

**Traumatic**: Blunt or sharp force

**Dental Disease**: Tooth root abscess; Oronasal fistula

**Vascular Malformation**

#### Box 35.2: Systemic Causes of Sneezing/Nasal Discharge
- **Hemostatic disorders**: thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopathia, vWD, coagulation factor deficiency (congenital: hemophilia A and B; acquired: rodenticide intox, DIC, liver failure)
- **Vasculitis** (toxic, inflammatory, immune-mediated: SLE)
- **Neoplastic**: ehrlichiosis (secondary), FIP (rare)
- **Infectious**: ehrlichiosis, FIP, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, leishmaniasis
- **Hyperviscosity**: multiple myeloma, IgM macroglobulinemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, lymphoma, Ehrlichia canis, FIP, amyloidosis, plasma cell leukemia
- **Systemic hypertension** (primary/essential [rare]; secondary: CKD, glomerulonephropathies, pheochromocytoma, hyperadrenocorticism, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, acromegaly, polycythemia, diabetes mellitus, overhydration)
- **Infections** (infectious tracheobronchitis, distemper, bacterial bronchopneumonia)
- **GI disease**: GERD, inflammatory GI disease

#### Signalment Clues
- Young: congenital or infectious; neoplasia and dental disease in older animals
- Brachycephalic dogs: conformational upper airway disease (less commonly nasal neoplasia)
- Brachycephalic cats: higher risk fungal rhinitis
- Dolichocephalic breeds: higher nasal disease incidence, fungal rhinitis, nasal tumors
- Cats: nonspecific inflammatory rhinosinusitis (sequela of viral URI), neoplasia in older cats (dyspnea, hemorrhagic/unilateral discharge)
- 36.3% of canine nasal disease cases remain undiagnosed; 64% of cats diagnosed with nonspecific rhinitis

#### Diagnostic Approach (Fig 35.1)
1. Acute signs → cats (calicivirus, herpesvirus-1, Bordetella, Mycoplasma) vs dogs (consider foreign body + rhinoscopy)
2. Reverse sneezing → nasopharyngeal disease (foreign body, parasites, airborne irritants, polyps, postnasal drip)
3. Chronic signs → oral examination; then imaging (radiographs, CT, MRI); then rhinoscopy/nasopharyngoscopy/nasal biopsy
4. Diagnostics: Cryptococcal serology in cats; Aspergillus AGID (highly specific 98%, not sensitive); viral testing not generally useful in cats
5. Epistaxis workup: platelet count, PT, APTT, or PIVKA; BMBT; blood pressure

### Ch 36 — Epistaxis and Hemoptysis
*(Author: Timothy B. Hackett)*

#### Epistaxis Definitions
- Laterality: intranasal causes often begin unilateral → bilateral as disease progresses; extranasal (coagulopathies, hypertension) may be bilateral from start but not always
- Mild bleeding: platelets <25,000/mcL; moderate: <10,000/mcL; severe: <5,000/mcL
- Spontaneous bleeding uncommon with platelets >50,000/mcL unless concurrent platelet function defect

#### Full DDx — Box 36.1: Causes of Epistaxis

**Extranasal (Systemic) Causes**

*Thrombocytopenia (quantitative platelet abnormality)*
- Decreased production:
  - Infectious: Ehrlichiosis, FeLV, FIV, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, hepatozoonosis, septicemia, endotoxemia, leishmaniasis, Bartonella spp.
  - Drugs: cytotoxic drugs, modified live virus vaccines, estrogens
  - Neoplasia: myelophthisis from myeloproliferative/lymphoproliferative diseases
  - Immune-mediated: antibodies against megakaryocytes
  - Other: bone marrow aplasia, cyclic thrombocytopenia, myelofibrosis, hyperestrogenism (Sertoli cell and granulosa cell tumors), myelodysplasia, toxins, osteosclerosis, idiopathic
- Increased destruction:
  - Immune-mediated: idiopathic or secondary to drugs, neoplasia, infection
  - Microangiopathy: hemangiosarcoma
- Sequestration: neoplasia (large vascular tumors), splenomegaly/torsion, hepatomegaly
- Increased consumption: DIC; vasculitis (RMSF, endotoxemia, neoplasia, HWD, bacteremia, Bartonella); hemorrhage-induced

*Thrombocytopathia (qualitative platelet defect)*
- Congenital: vWD, platelet procoagulant activity deficiency (GSDs), Glanzmann's thrombasthenia (Great Pyrénées, Otterhounds), Basset Hound thrombopathia
- Acquired: vWD (with hypothyroidism), uremia, dysproteinemia (multiple myeloma, ehrlichiosis, leishmaniasis), NSAIDs

*Coagulation factor deficiency*
- Congenital: hemophilia A and B
- Acquired: anticoagulant rodenticide intoxication, liver failure, DIC

*Increased capillary fragility*
- Systemic hypertension: primary or secondary (CKD, glomerulonephropathies, pheochromocytoma, hyperadrenocorticism, hyperthyroidism)
- Hyperviscosity syndrome: multiple myeloma, ehrlichiosis, erythrocytosis, leukemias
- Hyperlipidemia
- Thromboembolic disease
- Neoplasia invading blood vessels

**Intranasal (Localized) Causes of Epistaxis**
- Trauma
- Benign nasal polyps (cats)
- Neoplasia:
  - Epithelial: adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, angiofibroma
  - Mesenchymal: chondrosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, hemangiosarcoma, osteosarcoma, melanoma, hamartoma, leiomyoma, olfactory ganglioneuroblastoma
  - Round cell: lymphoma, transmissible venereal tumor, mast cell tumor
- Infection:
  - Fungal: Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, Penicillium, Rhinosporidium, Exophiala jeanselmei, phaeohyphomycosis, Conidiobolus lamprauges
  - Parasitic: Leishmania, Pneumonyssus, Eucoleus, Cuterebra, Linguatula, Capillaria
  - Bacterial: primary (Bordetella, Pasteurella, Mycoplasma) or secondary
  - Viral: canine infectious tracheobronchitis, canine distemper, feline viral rhinotracheitis, calicivirus
- Inflammation: lymphoplasmacytic (primary or secondary), eosinophilic (allergic rhinitis)
- Dental disease: tooth root abscess, oronasal fistula
- Foreign body
- Vascular malformation
- Nasal tumors (most common cause of epistaxis in older pets)

#### Hemoptysis DDx — Box 36.2

**Pulmonary**
- Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema or ARDS: systemic inflammatory processes/MODS, airway obstruction, strangulation, electrocution, drowning, seizures
- Pulmonary thromboembolism: neoplastic, endocrine, cardiac, metabolic disease
- Pulmonary hypertension: primary; or associated with chronic airway disease, HWD, congenital/acquired cardiac defects with shunting
- Chronic bronchitis/bronchiectasis
- Bacterial and fungal pneumonia
- Pulmonary abscess
- Canine infectious respiratory disease complex (adenovirus 1+2, parainfluenza, respiratory coronavirus, reovirus, herpesvirus-1, influenza; Bordetella, Mycoplasma)
- Parasites: Paragonimus kellicotti, Eucoleus aerophilus (Capillaria aerophila), Aelurostrongylus abstrusus
- Yersinia pestis (pneumonic plague — rare; reported in dogs)
- Eosinophilic bronchopneumopathy
- Neoplasia: primary adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, SCC, chondrosarcoma; metastatic; primary tracheal tumors
- Lung lobe torsion

**Cardiovascular**
- Cardiogenic pulmonary edema
- Heartworm disease
- Arteriovenous fistula
- Bacterial endocarditis

**Systemic**
- Bleeding disorders: primary (quantitative/qualitative platelet defects) or secondary hemostatic (factor deficiencies, rodenticide intoxication, DIC)
- Trauma: pulmonary contusion, tracheal rupture, foreign body
- Iatrogenic: endotracheal intubation, lung biopsy/aspirate, TTW, bronchoscopy

#### Clinical Distinctions (Hemoptysis)
- Blood mixed with inflammatory sputum → bronchopneumonia, chronic bronchitis
- Frothy bloody fluid → pulmonary edema
- Bright red blood → active arterial hemorrhage

### Ch 37 — Tachypnea, Dyspnea and Respiratory Distress
*(Author: M. Lynne O'Sullivan)*

#### Definitions
- **Dyspnea**: difficult or labored breathing; more precisely, conscious sensation of shortness of breath
- **Respiratory distress**: appropriate clinical term for what is observed in veterinary patients
- **Orthopnea**: dyspnea that is positional (present when recumbent/lying down)
- **Tachypnea**: increased respiratory rate; may occur with or without distress
- **Panting** (dogs): rapid shallow open-mouth breathing WITHOUT respiratory distress; thermoregulation, anxiety, pain
- Normal resting breathing rate (dogs and cats): <30 breaths/minute; seek veterinary care if consistently >35/min at rest

#### Thresholds for Hypoxemia/Hypercarbia
- PaO₂ <60 mm Hg or PaCO₂ >50 mm Hg (at time of distress) — may vary with duration
- Causes of hypoxemia: 1) decreased FiO₂, 2) hypoventilation, 3) diffusion impairment, 4) right-to-left cardiovascular shunt, 5) V/Q inequality, 6) abnormal hemoglobin
- Causes of hypercarbia: 1) hypoventilation, 2) V/Q inequality

#### Breathing Pattern Localization
- **Obstructive-inspiratory**: upper airway obstruction (BOAS, laryngeal paralysis, nasal FB, nasopharyngeal polyp, extra-thoracic tracheal collapse) — exaggerated inspiratory effort, stridor
- **Obstructive-expiratory/mixed**: small airway disease (feline asthma, chronic bronchitis, eosinophilic bronchopneumopathy, bronchopneumonia)
- **Restrictive (rapid, shallow)**: pulmonary parenchymal or pleural space disease; paradoxical breathing associated with pleural space disease in dogs and cats

#### Localization and DDx by Region

**Upper Airway (Ch 213-215)**
- Stenotic nares, nasal FB, rhinitis (infectious/inflammatory), nasopharyngeal polyp, neoplasia
- Laryngeal paralysis, neoplasia, FB, BOAS, extra-thoracic tracheal collapse

**Lower Airway (Ch 215-216)**
- Large airway: tracheal/bronchial collapse, tracheobronchitis
- Small airway: feline asthma, chronic bronchitis, eosinophilic bronchopneumopathy, smoke inhalation, bronchopneumonia

**Pulmonary Parenchyma (Ch 217)**
- Cardiogenic pulmonary edema
- Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (strangulation/upper airway obstruction, electrocution, head trauma, post-seizure, vasculitis)
- Pneumonia (viral, bacterial, fungal)
- Hemorrhage
- Neoplasia
- Parasitic disease
- Pulmonary thromboembolism
- Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
- ARDS
- Pulmonary contusions (trauma)

**Pleural Space (Ch 220)**
- Pneumothorax
- Pyothorax
- Hemothorax
- Chylothorax
- Masses
- Diaphragmatic hernia
- Transudative pleural effusion (neoplasia, cardiac disease, lung lobe torsion, hypoproteinemia)

**Thoracic Wall (Ch 222)**
- Neuromuscular disease: peripheral neuropathies, brain disorders, spinal cord disease (C1-C4), phrenic innervation abnormalities
- Trauma: rib fractures, flail chest, penetrating wounds

#### Breed/Age Predispositions
- Young dogs: breed-associated upper airway obstruction; pleural space disorders (trauma)
- Brachycephalic breeds: BOAS
- Smaller-breed dogs: Yorkshire Terriers, Pomeranians → collapsing trachea
- Older small-breed dogs: CHF from MMVD
- Older large-breed dogs: laryngeal paralysis
- West Highland White Terriers: pulmonary fibrosis
- Young-middle-aged cats: asthma
- Middle-aged to older cats: pleural effusion

#### Sedation dosages for respiratory distress
- Butorphanol: 0.1-0.2 mg/kg IM or IV
- Midazolam: 0.1-0.2 mg/kg IM or IV

### Ch 38 — Abnormal Heart Sounds and Heart Murmurs
*(Author: Robert Prošek)*

#### Murmur Grading (Box 38.1)
- **Grade 1**: Very soft, localized; detected in quiet room after intently listening ≥1 minute
- **Grade 2**: Soft but easily heard after a few seconds
- **Grade 3**: Moderate-intensity
- **Grade 4**: Loud but NO palpable thrill
- **Grade 5**: Loud WITH palpable thrill
- **Grade 6**: Very loud, palpable thrill, still audible after stethoscope removed 1 cm from thoracic wall

#### Normal Heart Sounds
- **S1**: Closure/tensing of AV valves (mitral+tricuspid) at onset of systole; coincides with QRS; longer, louder, lower pitched than S2
  - Increased S1: thin chest wall, tachycardia, high sympathetic tone, systemic hypertension, anemia
  - Diminished S1: obesity, pleural/pericardial effusion, diaphragmatic hernia, DCM, hypovolemia, emphysema, prolonged P-R interval
- **S2**: Closure of semilunar valves at end systole; single sound in dogs/cats normally
  - Pathologic S2 splitting (delayed P2): HWD, primary pulmonary hypertension, right-to-left PDA; also L-to-R intracardiac shunts, pulmonic stenosis, RBBB, ectopic beats, ventricular pacing
- **S3 gallop** (protodiastolic/ventricular gallop): rapid ventricular filling; heard with diastolic volume overloading: DCM, PDA, mitral insufficiency; in cats: DCM, anemia, hyperthyroidism
- **S4 gallop** (presystolic/atrial gallop): atrial contraction, just before S1; indicates ventricular hypertrophy/stiffness; also with 3rd-degree AV block
- **Summation gallop**: S3 + S4 merge at fast heart rates

#### Additional Heart Sounds
- **Ejection sounds**: left basilar high-frequency sounds; pulmonic stenosis, aortic stenosis, tetralogy of Fallot, HWD
- **Systolic clicks**: mid-to-late high frequency, mitral valve area; degenerative valve disease, mitral valve prolapse, mitral dysplasia
- **Opening snaps**: early diastolic, mitral stenosis (rare in dogs/cats)
- **Pericardial knocks**: early diastolic, constrictive pericardial disease (uncommon)

#### Systolic Murmurs
- **Mitral insufficiency**: left apex (mitral valve area), plateau-/band-shaped holosystolic, mixed frequency, may radiate dorsally and right thorax; causes: chronic degenerative valvular disease/MMVD, endocarditis, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, congenital malformations, annular dilation (PDA, DCM)
- **Tricuspid insufficiency**: right apex, similar to mitral; causes: congenital malformations, chronic degenerative valve disease, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy
- **Aortic stenosis (SAS)**: left heart base, crescendo-decrescendo, mixed frequency, harsh; radiates right cranial thorax and neck (carotid arteries); in cats: dynamic LVOT obstruction (HCM, SAM) most common
- **Pulmonic stenosis**: left heart base over pulmonic valve, high-frequency crescendo-decrescendo holosystolic; does not radiate along carotids
- **VSD**: right cranial thorax, harsh, mid-to-high-frequency holosystolic; murmur reduced/absent with large VSD and pulmonary hypertension
- **ASD**: pulmonic murmur from increased flow; fixed splitting of S2
- **Physiologic/functional murmurs**: anemia, fever, pregnancy, hyperthyroidism, thin thoracic walls, high sympathetic tone; proto-to-mesosystolic, grade 1-3/6, loudest left heart base; resolve in puppies/kittens
- **Innocent murmurs**: disappear as puppy/kitten matures; relative anemia or larger stroke volumes
- **Cats**: turbulent flow in region of right ventricular outflow tract → soft apical sternal murmur grade 1-3/6 with no structural disease (dynamic right ventricular outflow tract obstruction)

#### Diastolic Murmurs
- **Aortic insufficiency**: decrescendo from S2; young dogs: isolated defect or with SAS/VSD; adults: bacterial endocarditis
- **Pulmonic insufficiency**: rare; pulmonary hypertension, pulmonic valve dysplasia, idiopathic pulmonary artery dilation
- **Mitral stenosis**: low-frequency, mesodiastolic with presystolic accentuation; rare in dogs/cats; Bull Terriers prone (often with aortic stenosis)

#### Continuous Murmurs
- **PDA** (most common): "machinery-like" throughout cardiac cycle, peak intensity near S2; diastolic component disappears with pulmonary hypertension; associated bounding pulses (wide pulse pressure)
- Less common: aorticopulmonary window, ruptured aneurysm of sinus of Valsalva, coronary arteriovenous fistula

### Ch 39 — Pulse Alterations
*(Author: Justin Allen)*

#### Definitions
- **Pulse pressure** = systolic BP − diastolic BP (determines perceived pulse strength, not absolute BP)
- Normal pulse pressure: ~40 mm Hg (120 − 80 mm Hg)
- Normal heart rate (dogs): 60–180 bpm in hospital
- Respiratory sinus arrhythmia: normal in healthy dogs; reduced with advanced heart disease or elevated heart rates

#### Causes of Altered Pulse Pressure (Box 39.1)

**Reduced Preload (weak pulse)**
- Hemorrhage
- Volume depletion (diarrhea, vomiting, anorexia)
- Pericardial effusion
- Tachycardia (reduced filling time)

**Reduced Contractility (weak pulse)**
- DCM
- Sepsis
- Anesthetics

**Extracardiac Factors (reduced palpability)**
- Obesity
- Hirsutism
- Increased intrathoracic pressure

**Augmented Pulse Pressure (bounding pulse)**
- Hyperdynamic circulation: anemia, hyperthyroidism, drugs
- Bradycardia (long filling time)
- PDA (diastolic runoff → low diastolic BP)
- Aortic insufficiency
- Exercise

#### Specific Pulse Abnormalities
- **Pulsus parvus et tardus**: severe SAS (slow upstroke, prolonged ejection, narrow pulse pressure)
- **Bounding/waterhammer pulse**: PDA (diastolic runoff greatly lowers diastolic pressure)
- **Pulsus alternans**: beat-to-beat variation in strength; most common with ventricular dysfunction (DCM)
- **Pulsus bigeminus**: ventricular (or atrial) bigeminy; palpably similar to pulsus alternans
- **Pulsus paradoxus**: classic for cardiac tamponade; pulse falls/disappears during inspiration
- **Pulse deficits**: premature beats (ventricular > atrial); inadequate filling time → insufficient ejection

### Ch 40 — Syncope
*(Author: Mike Martin)*

#### Definitions
- **Collapse**: sudden loss of postural tone; not necessarily with loss of consciousness; any duration
- **Syncope**: subset of collapse; transient loss of consciousness (TLOC) from insufficient blood flow to the brain; short duration with good recovery; BP must fall ~50% before unconsciousness
- **Pre-syncope**: partial loss of consciousness; brief ataxia/stumbling
- Arrhythmia must last minimum 10–30 seconds to cause syncope (depending on activity level)
- Diagnostic accuracy in humans presenting with syncope: 65% initial diagnosis; 12.9% of those referred to epilepsy clinic had neurocardiogenic syncope

#### Full DDx — Syncope vs Non-Syncopal Collapse (Fig 40.1)

**SYNCOPE**

*Cardiogenic Syncope*
- Bradyarrhythmias + vasodilation:
  - Vasovagal syncope (especially young Boxer dogs; triggered by excitement)
  - Tussive syncope
  - Situational syncope (coughing, vomiting, sneezing, micturition, defecating, swallowing, visceral pain)
- Hypotension-inducing arrhythmias:
  - Sinus arrest
  - AV block with ventricular standstill
  - Rapid ventricular tachycardia
  - Iatrogenic (sedative drugs)
  - Intermittent pacemaker failure
- Combination of structural heart disease + hypotension-inducing arrhythmias +/− excitement/exertion
- Structural heart disease (limiting cardiac output on exertion):
  - Cardiomyopathies (DCM, HCM, ARVC)
  - Myocarditis
  - Pulmonary hypertension (64% presented with syncope in one study)
  - HWD
  - Pericardial effusion
  - Cardiac neoplasia
  - Congenital defects

*Neurocardiogenic Syncope*
- Vasovagal syncope (Bezold-Jarisch reflex): young Boxers most common; triggered by excitement; exacerbated by GI/abdominal conditions
- Situational syncope: coughing (tussive), vomiting, sneezing, micturition, defecating, swallowing, visceral pain
- Also: MMVD with pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmias

**NON-SYNCOPAL COLLAPSE**
- Neurological: epileptic seizures, narcolepsy-cataplexy, paroxysmal movement disorders, neuromuscular disease, exercise-induced collapse (EIC)
- Hypoxemia: severe respiratory disease, pulmonary hypertension, intermittent/transient airway obstruction (BUAS, laryngeal paralysis, tracheal collapse)
- Metabolic disorders: hypoadrenocorticism, hypoglycemia, hepatic shunt, shock (hemoabdomen), sepsis, toxemia, anemia

#### Key Differentiating Features (Syncope vs Seizures)
- **Syncope**: often triggered by excitement/exercise; animal initially motionless with flaccid tone ("sleep-like"); may have leg-flailing (not rhythmic paddling); opisthotonos possible with prolonged events; returns to normal within seconds to ~1 minute; eyes often open during unconsciousness
- **Seizures**: often at rest/from sleep; pre-ictal period of anxiety/odd behavior; jaw chopping/chattering, salivation, repetitive paddling; post-ictal period of confusion/anxiety/localizing signs lasting minutes to hours; in cats: can mimic brief focal seizures

#### Cardiac Troponin I Distinction
- Elevated cTnI: sensitivity 75%, specificity 80% for cardiogenic syncope vs epileptic seizures (significant overlap; low discriminatory value for individuals)

#### Diagnostic Tests
- ECG (always indicated; normal resting ECG does not exclude intermittent arrhythmia)
- Holter monitoring (24h count; 5–7 days needed in syncope); 75.5% success rate with cardiac event recorder
- ILRs (implantable loop recorders): 48–66% success rate in diagnosing/excluding intermittent arrhythmia
- Echocardiography (nearly always indicated)
- Thoracic radiographs
- Complete blood count, serum biochemistry (including glucose and electrolytes), thyroid status
- ACTH stimulation if hypoadrenocorticism suspected

### Ch 41 — Movement Disorders
*(Author: Gualtiero Gandini)*

#### Definitions
- **Myoclonus**: sudden, brief, shock-like involuntary muscle contraction; focal, multifocal, segmental, or generalized; epileptic or nonepileptic
- **Tremor**: involuntary rhythmic oscillatory movement of a body part
- **Dystonia**: sustained muscle contraction causing twisting/repetitive movements or abnormal postures
- **Paroxysmal Dyskinesias (PDs)**: abrupt self-limiting episodes of abnormal movement; consciousness retained; no autonomic signs; no post-ictal phase

#### PD Classification (Human-borrowed, modified for veterinary)
1. Paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD) — triggered by sudden movement
2. Paroxysmal nonkinesigenic dyskinesia (PNKD) — related to stress/excitement
3. Paroxysmal exercise-induced dyskinesia (PED) — prolonged heavy exercise

*Most canine PDs = PNKD equivalent in humans*

#### Key PD Features vs Seizures
- PDs: consciousness maintained, no autonomic signs (no hypersalivation, fecal/urinary incontinence), abrupt termination, no post-ictal phase
- Duration: seconds to hours; owners cannot interrupt episodes; discomfort (not overt pain) may be noted

#### Specific Breed-Related PDs

**Episodic Falling Syndrome (EFS) — CKCS**
- Autosomal-recessive; BCAN gene mutation
- First 4 years of life; triggered by exercise/stress/excitement
- Progressive limb hypertonicity, back arching, head lowering → "deer-stalking" posture then falling
- Treatment: clonazepam 0.5–2 mg/kg q8h or acetazolamide; drug tolerance may develop

**Scottie Cramp (SC) — Scottish Terriers**
- Complex genetic pathogenesis (almost 3/4 females affected)
- First episodes <6 months (rarely up to 7 years)
- Progressive dystonia hindlimbs → "deer-stalking" → arching back; 5–10 min (up to 20 min)
- Treatment: fluoxetine, diazepam; behavior modification

**PD — Chinook dogs**
- Autosomal-recessive (presumed)
- 2 months–5 years; PNKD-type (not triggered by sudden movement)
- Inability to stand, dystonia, chorea-like/ballismus-like movements, fine head tremors; minutes to 1 hour
- Minority have both PDs and GTCS

**Gluten-sensitive PD — Border Terriers** ("Canine epileptoid cramping syndrome")
- Now dietary etiology (not epilepsy)
- 2–120 min episodes; incoordination → can't stand; limb/head/neck dystonia + tremors
- GI signs (borborygmi, vomiting, diarrhea) between episodes
- Treatment: gluten-free diet; monitor anti-gliadin and transglutaminase-2 antibodies q3 months

**PD — Soft Coated Wheaten Terriers**
- Autosomal-recessive; PIGN gene mutation
- Hind limb dystonia, alternating flexion/extension; front limb in severe cases; minutes to 4 hours
- Anti-epileptic drugs not beneficial; worsens with age

**PD — Maltese dogs**
- Male overrepresented; first episode median age 5.4 years
- Dystonia starting in front limbs progressing to hind limbs + generalized tremors; conscious
- Treatment: acetazolamide, fluoxetine, gluten-free diet

**PD — Norwich Terriers**
- First episode ~3 years; 2–30 min; sustained hypertonia variably affecting limbs
- Triggered by stress, excitement, exercise, weather changes
- Not progressive

**Idiopathic Head Tremor Syndrome (IHTS) / "Head Bobbing"**
- Breeds: English Bulldog, Doberman Pinscher, Boxer, Staffordshire Bull Terrier, Labrador Retriever
- Paroxysmal rhythmic yes-yes (vertical) OR no-no (horizontal) head movements; direction fixed per episode
- First episode usually young adult (<48 months); episodes variable duration/frequency
- Anxiety may trigger; owners may interrupt mild episodes with food or head turning
- Most dogs do NOT respond to anti-epileptic drugs; may resolve spontaneously in older animals

#### Myoclonic Disorders
- **Nonepileptic myoclonus** in dogs: most common = distemper encephalomyelitis; also after other encephalitis; constant rhythmic; limbs and facial muscles
- **Lafora Disease (LD)**: autosomal recessive; EPM2b gene mutation; Miniature Wirehaired Dachshund, Beagle, Miniature/Standard Poodle, Welsh Corgi, Pointer; diagnosed age 6–13 years; jerky movements triggered by auditory/visual stimuli; treatment: levetiracetam 20 mg/kg q8h
- **Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy** — Rhodesian Ridgebacks (DIRAS1 gene)
- **Audiogenic reflex seizures in older cats** (median age 15 years; levetiracetam-responsive)

### Ch 42 — Tremors
*(Author: Clare Rusbridge)*

#### Definition
Involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part produced by alternating contractions of reciprocally innervated muscles.

#### Classification
**Axis 1 (Clinical Features)**
- Resting: at rest without voluntary muscle contraction (uncommon in vet med); examples: mycotoxicosis, drug-induced
- Action tremors:
  - Postural: holding body part against gravity (physiological, enhanced physiological, cerebellar ataxias, IGTS)
  - Kinetic-Simple: throughout movement (mycotoxicosis, IGTS, cerebellar ataxias, leukoencephalopathy)
  - Kinetic-Intention: crescendo as approaches target (cerebellar ataxia)
  - Isometric: against rigid stationary object
  - Orthostatic: limbs/trunk when standing, disappears when walking/recumbent; benign orthostatic tremor (senile tremor) or primary orthostatic tremor in Great Danes/Scottish Deerhounds (16–18 Hz on EMG)

**Axis 2 (Etiology)**
- Acquired
- Genetically defined: none yet in veterinary medicine
- Idiopathic (breed-related): primary orthostatic tremor in Great Danes/Scottish Deerhounds
- Idiopathic (sporadic): idiopathic generalized tremor syndrome (IGTS)

#### Metabolic Causes (must rule out with blood work)
- Hypoglycemia
- Hypocalcemia
- Hepatic encephalopathy
- Thiamine deficiency
- Thyrotoxicosis
- Hyperchloremic metabolic alkalosis
- Hypophosphatemia
- Hypodipsic hypernatremia

#### Tremorgenic Toxins (Table 42.3 — selected)
- **Penicillium toxins** (penitrem A, roquefortine): most common cause of acute-onset generalized tremor in vet med; whole-body tremor at rest, exacerbated by anxiety; mydriasis, vestibular signs, facial myoclonus, seizures, hyperesthesia, hyperthermia, tachycardia, GI signs
- Mycotoxins are highly lipid-soluble → penetrate brain well
- Other: 4-aminopyridine, 5-fluorouracil, 5-hydroxytryptophan, metaldehyde, organophosphates/carbamates, pyrethroids, SSRIs, macrocyclic lactones (genetic susceptibility), bromethalin, marijuana (cannabis), macadamia nuts, xylitol, methylxanthines (caffeine/theobromine), metaldehyde, DEET, Brunfelsia spp., cycad/sago palm, etc.

#### Idiopathic Generalized Tremor Syndrome (IGTS) / "White Shakers"
- Any breed (not just white breeds); rare in >20 kg dogs; young adults (~2 years); also reported in cats
- Etiology: undetermined; likely noninfectious immune-mediated
- Signs: whole-body tremor worsening with anxiety; hyperthermia, hyperesthesia, opsoclonus, mild cerebellar-vestibular signs; MRI normal; CSF: normal or mild mononuclear/lymphocytic pleocytosis
- Treatment: prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg PO q12–24h; tremor improves 3–10 days; taper over ≥6 months; mycophenolate 10 mg/kg PO q24h (q12h for first 7 days) if poorly responding/relapsing

#### Management of Tremorgenic Intoxication (Fig 42.3)
Drug dosages:
- **Apomorphine** (emesis induction if conscious): 20–40 mcg/kg IV
- **Activated charcoal**: 0.5–4 g/kg PO
- **Intralipid 20%**: 1.5 mL/kg IV over 1 min (loading), then 0.25–0.5 mL/kg/min IV CRI for 30–60 min; reduces median time to improvement from 24–96h to 4h
- **1st choice sedative/AED — Dexmedetomidine**: 375–500 mcg/m² IV; repeat q4h or 25 mcg/m²/h IV CRI
- **2nd choice — Phenobarbital**: 2–20 mg/kg IV (2–5 mg/kg bolus q20min to effect); or Levetiracetam: 30–60 mg/kg IV over 5 min (loading), then 10–20 mg/kg IV q8h
- **3rd choice — Ketamine**: 5 mg/kg initial bolus; 2–5 mcg/kg/min IV CRI; +/− methocarbamol 55–220 mg/kg IV
- **Methocarbamol (muscle relaxant)**: 40–50 mg/kg IV slowly until resolution; follow with 8.8–12.2 mg/kg/h IV CRI (not to exceed 2 mL/min); do not exceed 330 mg/kg q24h

#### Cerebellar Tremor
- Hallmark: intention tremor (crescendo toward target) + postural tremor of head + truncal sway
- Causes: spinocerebellar ataxias, degenerative/metabolic/inflammatory disorders

#### Dysmyelination/Hypomyelination ("Shaker Pup Syndrome")
- Signs from ~10 days of age; gross generalized tremor when aroused; many recover by 5 months
- Breeds: Springer Spaniel (autosomal recessive/sex-linked), Weimaraner (FNIP2 mutation), Chow Chow, Bernese Mountain Dog, Samoyed; Siamese cats

#### Primary Orthostatic Tremor (Great Danes, Scottish Deerhounds)
- High-frequency EMG discharges (13–18 Hz)
- Tremor when standing or moving between static postures; disappears when walking or recumbent
- Treatment: phenobarbital 2–3 mg/kg PO q12h (first-line); gabapentin 10–20 mg/kg PO q8–12h (second-line)

#### Benign Orthostatic Tremor (Senile Tremor)
- Older dogs primarily; pelvic limbs ± thoracic limbs; does not cause distress or interfere with function
- Treatment: gabapentin 10–20 mg/kg PO q8–12h

### Ch 43 — Ataxia, Paresis, and Paralysis
*(Author: Ronaldo Casimiro da Costa)*

#### Definitions
- **Ataxia**: incoordination; sensory (not motor) dysfunction; only identifiable when patient moves
- **Paresis**: partial loss of motor function (weakness)
- **Paralysis/Plegia**: complete loss of motor function

#### Three Types of Ataxia

**1. Vestibular Ataxia** (easiest to recognize)
- Head tilt (side of tilt = side of lesion usually)
- Other signs: leaning, falling, rolling, circling, strabismus, nystagmus
- **Peripheral vestibular**: NO change in mental status, NO proprioceptive deficits; nystagmus same direction, horizontal or rotatory (not vertical); idiopathic vestibular disease always peripheral
- **Central vestibular**: somnolence, proprioceptive/hopping deficits; vertical nystagmus or positional nystagmus; paradoxical: deficits contralateral to head tilt

**2. Cerebellar Ataxia**
- Dysmetria (inability to control rate/range of stepping); usually hypermetria in thoracic limbs
- Head tremors, intention tremors, wide pelvic limb stance/gait
- NO weakness, NO proprioceptive positioning deficits

**3. Proprioceptive Ataxia** (primary spinal cord sign)
- Truncal sway, circumduction, limb crossing; no head involvement
- Accompanied by CP deficits usually; chronic cases may have ataxia without CP deficits

#### DDx by Lesion Localization (Box 43.1)

**Vestibular Ataxia — Peripheral**
- Otitis media-interna
- Idiopathic vestibular disease
- Neoplasia

**Vestibular Ataxia — Central**
- Noninfectious meningoencephalitis (GME, NME, etc.)
- Infectious meningoencephalitis
- Neoplasia

**Cerebellar Ataxia**
- Noninfectious meningoencephalitis
- Infectious meningoencephalitis
- Cerebellar hypoplasia

**Proprioceptive Ataxia / Paresis (Spinal)**
- C1-C5: intervertebral disc disease, atlantoaxial subluxation, neoplasia
- C6-T2: IVDD, cervical spondylomyelopathy, neoplasia
- T3-L3: IVDD, spinal trauma, degenerative myelopathy, neoplasia, fibrocartilaginous embolism (FCE)
- L4-S3: IVDD, FCE, spinal trauma, neoplasia

**Diffuse Lower Motor Neuron Signs**
- Acute polyradiculoneuritis (Coonhound paralysis)
- Myasthenia gravis
- Botulism
- Tick paralysis

**Cauda Equina (L6, L7, Sacrum)**
- Degenerative lumbosacral stenosis
- Spinal trauma
- Discospondylitis
- Neoplasia

#### UMN vs LMN Signs
- **UMN**: paresis/paralysis + increased extensor tone (spasticity/hypertonus), normal-to-increased reflexes (hyperreflexia), slow disuse muscle atrophy
- **LMN**: paresis/paralysis + absent-to-decreased tone (flaccidity/hypotonus), decreased/absent reflexes (hyporeflexia/areflexia), rapid severe neurogenic muscle atrophy
- **Schiff-Sherrington** phenomenon: severe T2-L7 lesion; paraplegia with thoracic limb spasticity when laterally recumbent (due to loss of inhibitory propriospinal tract)
- **Spinal shock**: peracute T3-L3 myelopathy → first 24h: decreased tone and reflexes; after 24-48h: expected UMN signs emerge

#### Nociception Assessment
- Noxious stimulus (e.g., pinch toe) → limb withdrawal (spinal reflex) AND behavioral response (vocalization, looking) = intact nociception
- Limb withdrawal WITHOUT behavioral response = absent conscious pain perception = severe lesion → guarded-to-poor prognosis

### Ch 44 — Stupor and Coma
*(Author: Eileen Kenney)*

#### Definitions
- **Stupor**: rousable unconsciousness; animal can be aroused by noxious stimulus but lapses back when stimulus removed
- **Coma**: unrousable unconsciousness; cannot be aroused even by noxious stimulus
- Note: limb withdrawal from noxious stimulus = spinal reflex, NOT consciousness
- **ARAS**: ascending reticular activating system (pontine/midbrain); modulates arousal; consciousness requires both ARAS and cerebral cortex

#### Cushing Reflex (Cerebral Ischemic Response)
- Triggered by severe acute ICP increase
- Causes: systemic hypertension (to maintain CPP) + concurrent vagally-mediated reflex bradycardia
- Indicates life-threatening intracranial hypertension with imminent herniation → immediate treatment

#### Full DDx — Box 44.1: Causes of Stupor and Coma

**Structural**
- Traumatic brain injury
- Cerebrovascular accident (stroke)
- Infection/Inflammation: meningitis, encephalitis, abscess
- Neoplasia
- Hydrocephalus
- Demyelination

**Toxins**
- CNS depressants: opiates, benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, sympatholytics, macrocyclic lactones, anticonvulsants, cannabinoids, antihistamines, alcohols (ethylene glycol)
- Heavy metals: lead, mercury, aluminum
- Cellular hypoxia: carbon monoxide, cyanide, methemoglobinemia (e.g., acetaminophen)
- Cerebral edema: bromethalin

**Metabolic**
- Hypoperfusion
- Hypertensive encephalopathy
- Sepsis
- Hypo/hyperthermia
- Hyper/hypoglycemia
- Electrolyte disorders: hyper/hyponatremia, hyper/hypocalcemia, hypermagnesemia
- Acid/base derangements: acidosis, hypercarbia
- Organ system dysfunction: hepatic, kernicterus, renal, hypothyroid, adrenal insufficiency, cardiorespiratory
- Seizure-related: nonconvulsive status epilepticus, prolonged postictal states
- Nutritional deficiencies: thiamine, B12
- Anemia

#### Assessment: Neurological Examination Findings
**Pupil signs by lesion location:**
- Bilateral small reactive pupils → metabolic encephalopathy or bilateral diencephalic injury
- Pinpoint pupils → pontine injury (descending sympathetic destruction)
- Fixed dilated bilateral → CN III/midbrain damage bilaterally; or midposition if sympathetic tracts also absent
- Unilateral fixed pupil → ipsilateral midbrain lesion OR unilateral CN III compression (uncal herniation)

**Skeletal motor responses:**
- Decerebrate posture: extensor rigidity all 4 limbs; lesion in midbrain/rostral pons or bilateral cerebral hemispheric dysfunction; stupor/coma present
- Decerebellate posture: opisthotonos, forelimb extension, pelvic/hindlimb flexion; does NOT affect consciousness

**Respiratory patterns:**
- Kussmaul: rapid, deep, labored → severe metabolic acidosis
- Cheyne-Stokes: cyclical apnea-hyperpnea → diencephalon/pons lesion or bilateral cerebral hemisphere dysfunction
- Central neurogenic hyperventilation: persistent deep rapid breathing → pons/medulla lesion
- Apneustic breathing: prolonged inspiration with breath hold → upper pons (pneumotaxic center damage)
- Ataxic respiration: completely irregular → medulla damage → intubate immediately
- Agonal breathing: shallow gasps from anoxic injury → rapidly progresses to apnea/death

#### Treatment
- Mannitol, hypertonic saline for cerebral edema (if traumatic brain injury excluded → injectable corticosteroids also); not corticosteroids in TBI
- Head elevation 30° reduces ICP by facilitating venous drainage
- Hyperventilation to PaCO₂ ~30 mm Hg (brief only; limits cerebral blood flow)
- Modified Glasgow Coma Scale for serial assessment

### Ch 45 — Seizures
*(Author: Karen R. Muñana)*

#### Definitions
- Prevalence: 0.82% dogs; up to 0.16% cats
- **Generalized seizures**: abnormal neuronal activity from both hemispheres at onset; tonic-clonic, loss of consciousness, autonomic signs; also myoclonic and atonic seizures
- **Focal seizures**: discrete region of cerebrum/thalamus; focal motor activity, autonomic signs, behavioral abnormalities; can secondarily generalize
- **Epilepsy**: ≥2 unprovoked seizures ≥24 hours apart; enduring predisposition
- **Idiopathic epilepsy**: genetic predisposition; no other underlying cause; diagnosed in >50% of dogs with recurrent seizures (ages 6 months–6 years)
- **Structural epilepsy**: congenital or acquired lesion (hydrocephalus, encephalitis, CVD, trauma, neoplasia)
- **Epilepsy of unknown cause**: genetic unlikely based on age/findings but structural cause not identified; or full workup not performed
- **Reactive seizures**: extracranial causes (metabolic/toxic); brain otherwise healthy; NOT epilepsy

#### Clinical Features of Seizures vs Other Episodic Disorders (Table 45.1)
- **Seizures**: abnormal pre-episode behavior (prodrome); post-ictal period (fatigue, disorientation, aggression, compulsive walking, blindness; minutes to days); precipitating event: often REST; IMPAIRED consciousness; INCREASED tone; autonomic signs often present
- **Syncope**: immediate return to normal; precipitating event: exercise/excitement/none; LOSS of consciousness (brief); typically FLACCID; possible abnormal heart rate/urination
- **Narcolepsy/cataplexy**: precipitating event: excitement, eating; loss of consciousness (asleep) with narcolepsy, FLACCID; no autonomic signs
- **Transient vestibular**: normal; seconds to hours
- **Behavioral**: normal; possible environmental triggers; minutes to hours
- **Idiopathic head tremor**: normal; none; seconds to minutes
- **Paroxysmal movement disorders**: normal; none or exercise/excitement; increased tone; NO autonomic signs

#### Cats with Focal Seizures
- Orofacial motor signs: lip smacking, chewing, licking, swallowing; behavioral: vocalization, piloerection, aggression, motionless staring, startling/running
- May not be immediately recognized as seizures; associated with specific cat epilepsy syndrome

#### Full DDx — Box 45.1

**Extracranial Causes — Reactive Seizures**
- Metabolic: hepatic encephalopathy, renal/uremic encephalopathy, hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, sodium imbalance, hyperlipoproteinemia, thiamine deficiency
- Toxic: heavy metals, pesticides, ethylene glycol, caffeine/methylxanthines, mycotoxins, drugs

**Intracranial Causes — Structural Epilepsy**
- Degenerative: neurodegenerative disorders, lysosomal storage disorders
- Anomalous: hydrocephalus, lissencephaly, cortical malformations
- Neoplastic: primary, metastatic
- Infectious: viral, bacterial, protozoal, rickettsial, fungal/algal, parasitic
- Inflammatory: meningoencephalitis of unknown etiology (granulomatous, necrotizing)
- Traumatic: brain trauma
- Vascular: cerebrovascular disease, systemic hypertension

**Idiopathic Epilepsy**: genetic (proven or presumed)

**Epilepsy of Unknown Cause**: no structural cause identified

#### Diagnostic Approach by Age (Dogs)
- **6 months–6 years**: high probability idiopathic epilepsy (up to 75% of this age group); work up extracranial causes first; if negative → Tier I: presumptive idiopathic epilepsy; Tier II: requires MRI + CSF
- **<6 months**: idiopathic epilepsy still most likely; but structural (brain anomalies, infectious) also high probability → more extensive workup warranted; ultrasound through open fontanelle for ventriculomegaly
- **>6–7 years**: structural epilepsy most likely; neoplasia most common in older dogs; serial blood pressure measurements; thoracic radiographs + abdominal ultrasound to screen; MRI + CSF if unremarkable

#### Cats
- Structural epilepsy diagnosed in up to 2/3 of cats with recurrent seizures (vs dogs where idiopathic is majority)
- MRI + CSF recommended for all cats with recurrent seizures regardless of age
- Cats with infectious disease: test for FIV, FeLV, toxoplasmosis, cryptococcosis
- Idiopathic epilepsy occurs but proportionally less frequent

#### Red Flags → Structural Workup
- Onset <6 months or >6 years
- Neurological abnormalities between episodes
- Progressive increase in frequency/severity
- Cluster seizures or status epilepticus

### Ch 47 — Gagging and Dysphagia
*(Author: Megan Grobman)*

#### Definitions
- **Dysphagia**: swallowing impairment; clinical finding not diagnosis
- **Gagging**: airway protective behavior; stimulated by touching base of tongue, palate, epiglottis, caudal pharynx; CN V (sensory afferent) → CN X (efferent)
- **Retching**: unsuccessful attempt to expel gastric contents; rhythmic heaving; functions: trigger vomiting, provide rostral momentum, mix/buffer gastric contents
- **Expectoration**: clearance of mucoid secretions from airways; "hacking" mistaken for gagging/retching; terminal retch at end of coughing paroxysm suggests respiratory disease

#### Swallowing Phases
1. **Oral preparatory** (voluntary): prehension, mastication, bolus formation
2. **Pharyngeal/cricopharyngeal** (involuntary): food bolus through upper esophageal sphincter
3. **Esophageal/gastroesophageal** (involuntary): primary peristalsis → LES relaxation

#### Full DDx — Table 47.2

**Gagging (respiratory causes)**
- Nasal: nasopharyngeal mass or FB
- Upper airway: pharyngeal collapse, epiglottic retroversion, laryngeal paralysis
- Lower airway: productive or severe cough of multiple etiologies

**Dysphagia**

*Oral Preparatory*
- Mechanical: periodontal disease, FB, oral mass/swelling, sialadenosis/sialadenitis, oral/facial trauma, cleft palate, TMJ disorder, stomatitis, retrobulbar abscess
- Functional: CN V, VII, XII dysfunction; masticatory myositis; trigeminal neuritis; IMPA; lingual myositis/glossitis; polymyositis

*Pharyngeal (including cricopharyngeal)*
- Mechanical: neoplasia, FB, tonsillar swelling, elongated soft palate, pharyngeal abscess/trauma, nasopharyngeal polyp, mucocele, disruption of hyoid apparatus, retropharyngeal lymphadenopathy
- Functional: CN IX, X, XI dysfunction; cricopharyngeal achalasia or dyssynchrony; extra-esophageal reflux disease (EERD); myasthenia gravis (MG); polyradiculoneuritis; tetanus; GOLPP; hypophosphatemia; hypocalcemia; rabies; pseudorabies; polymyositis; hypoadrenocorticism; dystrophin deficiency

*Esophageal (including gastroesophageal)*
- Mechanical: FB, vascular ring anomaly, stricture, diverticulum, neoplasia, hiatal hernia, gastroesophageal intussusception, spirocercosis, pythiosis, trauma
- Functional: CN X or diffuse autonomic dysfunction, dysautonomia, GERD, idiopathic esophageal dysmotility (IED), primary or secondary megaesophagus, LES achalasia-like syndrome (LES-AS), esophagitis, botulism, tetanus, distemper, MG, hypothyroidism, hypoadrenocorticism, lead intoxication, thallium intoxication, acetylcholinesterase inhibitor intoxication, polymyositis, polyneuritis, polyradiculoneuritis, glycogen storage disease, trypanosomiasis, dystrophin deficiency

#### Breed/Familial Predispositions (Table 47.3)
- **Dystrophin deficiency**: Belgian Shepherd, Brittany Spaniel, German Shorthaired Pointer, Golden Retriever, Irish Terrier, Japanese Spitz, Miniature Schnauzer, Rat Terrier, Rottweiler

- **Vascular ring anomalies**: German Shepherd Dog, Irish Setter
- **Gastroesophageal intussusception**: German Shepherd Dog
- **Hiatal hernia**: Brachycephalic breeds, Chinese Shar-Pei
- **Hereditary megaesophagus**: Miniature Schnauzer, Wire-Haired Fox Terrier
- **Congenital megaesophagus**: Chinese Shar-Pei, GSD, Great Dane, Irish Setter, Labrador Retriever, Newfoundland
- **Familial megaesophagus**: GSD, Great Dane, Irish Setter, Newfoundland, Siamese cat

#### Diagnostic Gold Standard
- Videofluoroscopic Swallow Studies (VFSS): free-feeding, free-standing protocol preferred; criterion standard
- LES-AS identified in subpopulation of dogs with esophageal dysphagia → responds to targeted intervention (balloon dilation, botulinum toxin A, surgical myotomy + fundoplication)
- Thoracic radiographs (minimum 3 views) in all patients with dysphagia to identify megaesophagus and aspiration pneumonia

### Ch 48 — Regurgitation and Vomiting
*(Author: Alex Gallagher)*

#### Definitions and Differentiation (Table 48.1)
| Feature | Regurgitation | Vomiting |
|---|---|---|
| Nausea/hypersalivation | No | Common |
| Retching/abdominal contractions | No | Common |
| Presence of bile | Rare | +/- |
| Cervical esophageal distension | +/- | No |
| Amount | Any | Any |
| Time after eating | Variable | Variable |
| pH | Variable | Variable |

- **Regurgitation**: passive expulsion from pharynx or esophagus; no abdominal contractions; animal simply lowers head and material falls out; mucus or bile rare (reflux from stomach into esophagus possible)
- **Vomiting**: active centrally-mediated reflex; prodromal nausea, lip-smacking; retching; closures of nasopharynx and glottis to protect airway; bile indicates patency of gastric outflow
- **Vomiting food >8 hours post-ingestion**: suggests delayed gastric emptying (obstruction or hypomotility)
- Regurgitation uncommon in cats → assume vomiting in cats unless otherwise confirmed
- pH not reliable differentiator

#### Regurgitation — Common Causes
- Megaesophagus (primary: idiopathic/hereditary; secondary: MG, hypothyroidism, hypoadrenocorticism, lead intox)
- Esophagitis
- Foreign body
- Vascular ring anomaly
- Stricture
- Diverticulum
- Neoplasia
- Hiatal hernia

#### Vomiting Pathophysiology
- Emetic center: medullary nucleus group; serotonergic (5HT1) + alpha2 adrenergic receptors; adjacent NK1 receptors in nucleus tractus solitarii
- **CRTZ** (area postrema; no blood-brain barrier): D2, H1, alpha2, 5HT3, M1, enkephalinergic (ENK-mu, delta), NK1 receptors → samples blood for toxins/drugs
- Species difference: apomorphine (D1+D2 agonist) = potent emetic in dogs but NOT cats; xylazine (alpha2 agonist) = effective emetic in cats; cisplatin-induced emesis in dogs = visceral rather than central 5HT3 receptors

*Large Intestinal Diseases*
- Constipation
- Colitis

### Ch 49 — Diarrhea
*(Author: Alison C. Manchester)*

#### Definitions
- **Diarrhea**: increased frequency, fluidity, and/or volume of bowel movements
- **Chronic diarrhea**: observed for ≥3 weeks
- Small vs large bowel differentiation:
  - **Small bowel**: ↑↑ stool volume, ↑ stool frequency, +/− weight loss, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, bloating
  - **Large bowel**: tenesmus, urgency, ↑↑ stool frequency (small volume), +/− dyschezia, mucus, fresh blood

#### Signalment/Breed Predispositions
- **EPI**: German Shepherds, Rough-Coated Collies, Cavalier King Charles Spaniels
- **Lymphangiectasia**: Yorkshire Terriers, Maltese, Chow Chows
- **Granulomatous colitis** (E. coli): Boxers, French Bulldogs
- **Protothecosis**: Rough-Coated Collies, Boxers
- **FIP**: Abyssinian, Bengal cats
- **Tritrichomoniasis**: Abyssinian, Bengal cats

- Clinically well, normal vital parameters → minimal diagnostics; bland diet, deworming, probiotics, subcutaneous fluids
- Antibiotics (metronidazole, tylosin) only when: sepsis/SIRS, neutropenia, documented non-responding enteropathogen
- Note: metronidazole causes DNA damage in feline lymphocytes → not benign in cats
- Unwell: minimum database (CBC, serum biochemistry, urinalysis, fecal parasite screen)

### Ch 51 — Constipation, Tenesmus, Dyschezia, and Fecal Incontinence
*(Author: Emily N. Gould)*

#### Definitions
- **Constipation**: reduced frequency or absence of defecation; retention of hard, dry feces; total fecal volume often decreased
- **Obstipation**: inability to defecate without medical intervention; result of prolonged constipation; not all constipated animals become obstipated
- **Tenesmus**: repeated nonproductive straining to defecate or urinate; usually accompanied by LI disease signs (hematochezia, mucus, increased frequency/reduced volume); precedes defecation in obstructive disease; follows defecation in inflammatory disease
- **Dyschezia**: painful defecation; typically anorectal rather than colonic etiology
- **Fecal incontinence**: failure to retain feces; involuntary "leakage"

#### DDx — Constipation
**Mechanical**
- Low-insoluble-fiber diet → decreased colonic motility
- Pica (bones, hair, wool, cat litter, plant material)
- Congenital: sacral spinal cord abnormalities (Manx cats, some brachycephalic dogs); atresia ani (Poodles, Boston Terriers)
- Lumbar/sacral/pelvic canal trauma
- Severe prostatomegaly (extraluminal obstruction)
- Idiopathic: feline idiopathic megacolon (recurrent constipation → obstipation)

**Metabolic/Nutritional**
- Hypokalemia, hypercalcemia, hypomagnesemia
- Prolonged dehydration
- PU/PD diseases (CKD, diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism)
- Obesity; prolonged inactivity

**Neurologic/Endocrine**
- Dysautonomia (rare; more often cats, also dogs)
- Hypothyroidism (cats: especially congenital kittens)

#### DDx — Tenesmus and Dyschezia
**Intraluminal obstruction**: neoplasia, FB, strictures
**Extraluminal obstruction**: compressive abdominal mass/organomegaly, pelvic fractures, pseudocoprostasis
**Infectious**: Trichuris, Ancylostoma spp.; Tritrichomonas foetus (cats; tenesmus, dyschezia, foul-smelling LI diarrhea); Pythium, Histoplasma, Prototheca (can cause marked colonic disease)
**Inflammatory**: CIE, fiber-responsive LBD (FRLBD), food-responsive enteropathies (FRE), stress colitis (dogs)
**Structural**: perianal fistulas (GSDs overrepresented), anal sacculitis, perineal hernia
**Congenital**: atresia ani (Poodles, Boston Terriers)

#### DDx — Fecal Incontinence
**Neurogenic**
- UMN sphincter incompetence: focal (arachnoid cysts, IVDD, masses) or diffuse (inflammatory CNS disorders, dysautonomia)
- IVDD; degenerative myelopathy (DM in GSDs, Pembroke Welsh Corgis); sacrocaudal luxations ("tail-pull"); discospondylitis; cauda equina syndrome
- Diffuse LMN disease

**Non-neurogenic**
- Reservoir incontinence: severe colon/rectum inflammation; subtotal colectomy
- External sphincter incompetence: aging, behavioral disorders, trauma, neoplasia
- Intoxications (vincristine, macadamia nuts) → transient peripheral neuropathies

### Ch 52 — Flatulence
*(Author: Genevieve Mak)*

#### Definitions and Physiology
- Flatulence: gas expelled through anus; dogs/cats normally pass flatus multiple times daily
- >99% of GI gas = odorless (CO₂, H₂, methane, nitrogen, oxygen); <1% sulfur-based (odiferous)
- ~75% of flatus = H₂, methane, CO₂ (from bacterial fermentation)
- Brachycephalic breeds: aerophagia → increased gas production → higher frequency and more odorous feces

#### Differential Diagnosis
**Primary causes of excessive flatulence**
- High-FODMAP diet (oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, polyols): wheat, rye, leeks, zucchini, dairy (lactose), fruits (apples, pears, peaches, mangoes, apricots), lentils, legumes, cabbage, brussels sprouts, green beans
- Any primary GI disease that impairs fermentation/absorption: EPI (when associated with malabsorption), IBD, diarrhea
- Aerophagia: brachycephalic breeds, excited or stressed animals
- High protein/fermentable-fiber diets

**"Emergency treatment"**: open windows

#### Management (Fig 52.1)
1. Diet change (avoid FODMAPs); trial each diet 6–8 weeks
2. Probiotics (conflicting evidence; may reduce flatulence/bloating in some)
3. Prebiotics: modulate microbiota; beneficial fecal metabolite shifts
4. Yucca schidigera extract: improves fecal odor (binds ammonia); does not reduce total gas or frequency
5. Activated charcoal: binds sulfur gases (reduces odor, not gas production)
6. Simethicone: anti-foaming agent; theoretical reduction in bloating; dose 20–200 mg/dog PO up to q6h; no studies in dogs/cats
7. Antibiotics: NOT recommended for flatulence alone (promotes resistance); only if concurrent SIBO/IBD

### Ch 53 — Polyuria and Polydipsia
*(Author: Robert E. Shiel)*

#### Definitions/Thresholds
- **Polydipsia (PD)**: water intake >90–100 mL/kg/day (dogs); >50 mL/kg/day (cats)
- **Polyuria (PU)**: urine output >50 mL/kg/day (both species)
- USG in health: 1.006–1.050 (dogs); 1.005–1.090 (cats)
- Results consistently >1.030 (dogs) or >1.035 (cats) = strong evidence against PU unless glycosuria or intermittent PU
- **Marked hyposthenuria** (USG <1.006) in dogs: most commonly hyperadrenocorticism, CDI, hypercalcemia, hypoadrenocorticism, liver disease, or primary polydipsia

#### Serum Osmolality Formula (conventional units)
`Serum Osmolality (mOsm/kg) = 2[Na⁺] + [glucose/18] + [BUN/2.8]`

#### USG interpretation note
- USG increases ~0.004 per g/dL glucose (may vary up to 0.008 in dilute urine with 2.4 g/dL/133 mmol/L glucose added)

#### Full DDx — Box 53.1: Causes of PU/PD

**Central Diabetes Insipidus (CDI)**
- Neoplasia, inflammation, trauma, developmental defects, idiopathic
- Complete or partial AVP deficiency

**Primary Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus (NDI)**
- Rare hereditary disorder; severe PU/PD at early age
- Dogs only (*not reported in cats)

**Secondary Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus**
- Hyperadrenocorticism
- Hypercalcemia
- Hypoadrenocorticism
- Liver disease
- Hyperthyroidism
- Pyometra/E. coli endotoxemia
- Pyelonephritis
- Hypokalemia
- Erythrocytosis* (dogs only)
- Hyperaldosteronism
- Hypersomatotropism (acromegaly)
- Leptospirosis* (dogs; rare as isolated PU cause)
- Drug administration: glucocorticoids, phenobarbital

**Disturbed Osmotic Gradient (osmotic diuresis)**
- Diabetes mellitus
- Fanconi syndrome* (dogs)
- Primary renal glycosuria* (dogs)
- Post-obstructive diuresis
- Chronic kidney disease (CKD)
- Syndrome of inappropriate diuresis (SIAD)*
- High-salt or -protein diet
- Drug administration: mannitol
- Decreased medullary tonicity: renal medullary washout; low-protein or -salt diet

**Other/Unknown Mechanisms**
- Polyuric phase of acute kidney injury
- Pheochromocytoma
- Splenic hemangiosarcoma* (dogs only)
- Intestinal leiomyosarcoma* (dogs only)
- Primary polydipsia (PP; psychogenic)

*Described as cause of PU/PD in dogs but not cats

#### Cats-Specific Note
- PU/PD most common in CKD, diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, CDI
- Primary NDI not reported in cats; one possible PP case in literature
- Outdoor cats/multi-cat households: difficult to measure water intake and urine output
- Cats may be resistant to PU due to greater urine concentrating ability and tolerance to dehydration

#### Diagnostic Approach (Fig 53.1)
**Step 1**: Confirm PU/PD with measured water intake at home; multiple urine samples over several days
**Step 2**: Signalment, history, PE (age, breed, sex, neuter status, medications)
**Step 3**: CBC, serum biochemistry, urinalysis (± electrolytes); glycosuria confirms DM or renal tubular disease (exclude stress hyperglycemia in cats); isosthenuria/hyposthenuria = renal azotemia; concentrated urine = pre-renal azotemia; hyposthenuria in pre-renal pattern = hypoadrenocorticism
**Step 4**: Additional testing based on clinical suspicion
- Urine culture: for pyelonephritis (challenging to exclude)
- Abdominal ultrasonography: adrenal, liver, kidney diseases; pyometra; splenic hemangiosarcoma; intestinal leiomyosarcoma
- Specific tests: liver function, hyperadrenocorticism (LDDS/HDDS/ACTH stim), hyperthyroidism (T4), acromegaly (IGF-1)
- SDMA or GFR: for early/preclinical CKD
- MRI: if brain disease suspected (CDI)

**Step 5**: Modified water deprivation test or desmopressin trial (ONLY after all other DDx excluded)
- Modified water deprivation test: distinguishes PP vs CDI vs primary NDI; risk of severe hypertonic dehydration (potentially fatal)
- Desmopressin trial: alternative to water deprivation test (especially in cats)

### Ch 54 — Pollakiuria, Stranguria, and Urinary Incontinence
*(Author: Mary Anna Labato)*

#### Definitions
- **Urinary incontinence**: involuntary escape of urine during storage phase; most common = intermittent or continuous dribbling + episodes of normal voiding
- **Detrusor hyperspasticity/instability**: spontaneous uninhibited detrusor contractions (overactive bladder); frequent voiding of small volumes; often underlying cystitis; cats: FIC/Pandora syndrome
- **Pollakiuria**: abnormally frequent passage of small amounts of urine
- **Stranguria**: slow, painful discharge of urine from urethral/bladder spasms; urine passed drop by drop with pain and even tenesmus
- **Dysuria**: difficult or painful urination; "catch-all" term for stranguria or pollakiuria
- Residual urine volume: should not exceed 0.4 mL/kg in normal animals

#### Causes of Pollakiuria (Box 54.1)
- Infection: bacterial, mycoplasmal, viral, fungal, parasitic (bladder, prostate)
- Inflammation: polypoid cystitis, pyogranulomatous cystitis, granulomatous urethritis, follicular vaginitis
- Urocystolithiasis
- Neoplasia: transitional cell carcinoma, prostatic adenocarcinoma, vaginal leiomyoma/sarcoma
- Detrusor atony (partial obstruction)
- Urethral stricture
- Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC)/Pandora syndrome
- Chemical/drugs (e.g., cyclophosphamide)
- Anatomic abnormalities: perineal hernia with retroflexed bladder, spay granuloma, uterine stump disease
- Iatrogenic: urethral irritation from catheterization, palpation, voiding urohydropropulsion, overdistension from flushing

#### Causes of Stranguria (Box 54.2)
- Cystitis: infectious vs sterile inflammatory
- Urethritis: infectious vs sterile inflammatory
- Urocystolithiasis
- Vaginitis: follicular (inflammatory) vs infectious
- Neoplasia: urethral TCC, prostatic adenocarcinoma, vaginal leiomyoma/sarcoma
- Prostatic disease: prostatitis, prostatic abscess, neoplasia
- Trauma, foreign body
- FIC/Pandora syndrome
- Ruptured bladder
- Urethral stricture

#### Causes of Urge Incontinence (Box 54.3)
- Detrusor hyperspasticity (idiopathic)
- Cystitis/urethritis: infectious vs sterile inflammatory
- Anatomical abnormality: ectopic ureter(s)
- Pelvic bladder, urethral dysplasia
- Neurological abnormality: UMN bladder
- Urethral sphincter mechanism incompetence
- Urethral stricture

#### Neurological Examination in Incontinence
- LMN bladder: reduced sphincter tone, easy manual expression
- UMN bladder: increased sphincter tone, difficult manual expression
- Perineal reflex: evaluates pudendal nerve
- Bulbospongiosus reflex: pudendal nerve + sacral spinal segments

#### Key Distinction: FIC in Cats
- FIC: self-limiting; most common age 1–6 years; equal males and females; male cats risk urethral obstruction (life-threatening emergency)
- Older cats: bacterial UTI more likely; also stones (struvite, calcium oxalate)

### Ch 55 — Hematuria and Other Conditions Causing Discolored Urine

### Ch 56 — Vulvar and Preputial Discharge
*(Author: Jeffrey de Gier)*

#### Overview
- Physiological vaginal discharges (not worrisome): bitches during proestrus, pregnancy, parturition, days-to-weeks post-parturition; queens during/after parturition
- Physiological preputial discharge in males: mucopurulent without clinical signs = normal/inconsequential
- Invasive vaginal examination usually not feasible in awake cats or prepubertal bitches

#### Serosanguineous Vaginal Discharge — Intact Bitches/Queens (Fig 56.2)

**With estrogen influence (confirmed by vaginal cytology, vaginoscopy)**
- Physiological: proestrus
- Prolonged duration:
  - Ovarian neoplasia
  - Cystic ovarian follicles
  - Failure to ovulate ("split heats") — persistent low plasma progesterone
  - Exogenous estrogen
  - Portosystemic shunt
  - Pituitary hypofunction

**Without estrogen influence**
- Endometritis (most common pathological cause after estrus or parturition)
- Neoplasia of urogenital tract (often benign: leiomyoma, fibroma; evaluate by cytology/histopathology)
- Subinvolution of placental sites (SIPS): prolonged bloody discharge >2–3 weeks post-parturition in otherwise healthy bitches; smear shows erythrocytes, occasional neutrophils, vacuolated giant cells; resolves at next estrus
- Rarely: trauma, hemorrhagic diathesis, foreign body (vagina)
- In queens: endometritis or (partial) abortion → bloody discharge; rarely trauma, diathesis, foreign body

#### Serosanguineous After Ovariectomy/OVH

**With estrogen influence**
- Remnant ovarian tissue (ROT): confirmed by plasma progesterone >2 ng/mL; GnRH stimulation test or AMH to confirm; basal LH alone not reliable
- Exogenous estrogen administration

**Without estrogen influence**
- Stump endometritis: only possible with ROT (progesterone influence) or progestins
- Uterine stump hemorrhage (1–2 weeks post-OVH)
- Neoplasia of urogenital tract (more often malignant than in intact bitches)
- Rarely: trauma, hemorrhagic diathesis, foreign body

#### Mucopurulent Vaginal Discharge — Intact (Fig 56.7)

**Physiological**
- Onset of diestrus (several days)
- Pregnancy (~day 30: clear mucus)

**Pathological**
- Endometritis (post-estrum, post-partum)
- Abortion (often dark green discharge)
- Vestibulitis/vaginitis (primary or secondary):
  - Primary vaginitis: prepubertal (puppy vaginitis) — NOT seen in cats; or after 1st/2nd estrus (especially large/brachycephalic breeds; often self-limiting after 1st estrus); treatment with antibiotics not recommended pre-1st estrus
  - Secondary vaginitis: various causes
  - Infrequently: canine herpesvirus infection (vestibule)
- Neoplasia (usually benign)
- Foreign body (vagina)

**"Puppy Vaginitis"**
- Prepubertal large/brachycephalic breeds (as early as 3 months)
- Slightly swollen vulva, mucoid white/yellow discharge, attraction of males
- Self-limiting; disappears after 1st estrous cycle or after 2nd–3rd luteal phases
- Treatment pre-1st estrus NOT recommended; antibiotics do not help

**After OVH (mucopurulent)**
- Vaginitis (usually secondary)
- Stump endometritis (requires ROT + progesterone influence or exogenous progestins)
- Hypersecretion of vaginal mucosa (if continuing estrogen influence, e.g., ROT)
- Rarely: neoplasia (lower prevalence; more often malignant), foreign body

#### Preputial Discharge (Fig 56.14)

**Mucopurulent**
- Balanoposthitis
- Prostatitis
- Penile neoplasia
- Foreign body

**Serosanguineous**
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia
- Balanoposthitis
- Prostatitis
- Urethral prolapse
- Penile/urethral: trauma, calculus, neoplasia
- Hemorrhagic diathesis
- Foreign body
