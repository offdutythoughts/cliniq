### Ch 57 — Anemia and Erythrocytosis

#### Reference Thresholds / Definitions
- **Anemia**: PCV/HCT/HB/RBC below the lower reference limit for the species.
- **Erythrocytosis**: PCV/HCT/HB/RBC above the upper reference limit.
- **PCV vs HCT distinction**: PCV is direct (microhematocrit); HCT is calculated (MCV × RBC). Median PCV–HCT difference = −0.02 L/L (dog) and −0.03 L/L (cat).
- **Breed caution (dog)**: Greyhounds normally have higher HCTs — a result of 0.45 L/L (45%) may indicate anemia in this breed despite being within standard reference intervals.
- **Regenerative response timing**: Bone marrow takes ~3–5 days to mount a regenerative response; do not classify as non-regenerative before this window.

#### Reticulocyte Interpretation
- **Moderate to marked polychromasia** (>7 RBCs per 100× oil immersion field): likely regenerative regardless of severity.
- **Mild polychromasia** (<3 RBCs per 100× field): "adequate" regeneration difficult to assess.
- **Absolute reticulocyte count** (above upper reference limit for the lab) = most reliable marker of adequacy.
- Example regenerative case: dog with 0.25 L/L HCT (RI 0.41–0.58) → reticulocytes 10%, absolute 348 × 10⁹/L (RI 11–92 × 10⁹/L).
- Cats: only aggregate reticulocytes are counted; punctate reticulocytes are not included.

#### 4-Step Mechanistic Approach to Anemia
1. **How acute is the anemia?** (Pre-regenerative if <3–5 days old)
2. **Is the anemia regenerative?** (Use absolute reticulocyte count ± polychromasia)
3. **Is a cause identifiable on blood smear?**
4. **Which additional tests are indicated?**

#### DDx for Regenerative Anemia (Hemorrhage vs Hemolysis)
| Mechanism | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Hemorrhage** | Low total protein (esp. albumin + globulins); iron deficiency if chronic external loss (hypochromic microcytic RBCs, low iron/transferrin saturation) |
| **Hemolysis — extravascular** | Normal/high total protein; high bilirubin (unconjugated); high iron/transferrin saturation; siderocytes |
| **Hemolysis — intravascular component** | As above plus hemoglobinemia + hemoglobinuria; ghost RBCs on smear |

**Blood smear clues for hemolysis mechanism:**
- Spherocytes (moderate to many): IMHA
- Heinz bodies + eccentrocytes: oxidant injury (zinc, onions [dog], acetaminophen [cat], skunk musk)
- Acanthocytes + schistocytes + keratocytes: fragmentation (DIC, vasculitis)
- Organisms on smear: Babesia spp., Mycoplasma haemofelis (cats), M. haemocanis (dogs; uncommon cause of anemia)

#### DDx — Hemolysis (by cause)
- **Immune-mediated (IMHA)**: Primary (autoimmune) or associative (secondary to infection, drugs, blood-group incompatibility). Common in dogs; uncommon in cats.
- **Infectious**: Babesia sp., Theileria sp., Mycoplasma haemocanis/haemofelis; only M. haemofelis is a common cause of hemolytic anemia in cats.
- **Oxidant-induced**: Zinc (dogs), onion/garlic (dogs and cats), acetaminophen (cats), skunk musk (dogs).
- **Fragmentation (microangiopathic)**: Vasculitis, DIC. Often non-regenerative due to concurrent anemia of inflammatory disease.
- **Aberrant macrophage activity**: Hemophagocytic histiocytic sarcoma.
- **Inherited RBC defects**: Phosphofructokinase deficiency (dog), pyruvate kinase deficiency (dogs and cats), stomatocytosis (Alaskan Malamute), spherocytosis (Golden Retriever).
- **Miscellaneous**: Severe hypophosphatemia, envenomation (snakes, bees).

#### DDx — Non-Regenerative Anemia

| Index type | Consider |
|---|---|
| Macrocytic normochromic | FeLV (cats); rule out breed-associated (Miniature/Toy Poodles) |
| Normocytic normochromic (mild–moderate) | Anemia of inflammatory disease; anemia of chronic kidney disease; endocrine disease (hypothyroidism, hypoadrenocorticism); cancer-associated anemia |
| Normocytic normochromic (severe) | Immune-mediated (precursor-directed IMHA); FeLV; acute leukemia; bone marrow aplasia |
| Microcytic hypochromic | Iron deficiency (chronic blood loss); functional iron deficiency (portosystemic shunts); rule out breed-associated (Shar-Pei, Siberian Husky) |

**Other non-regenerative causes:**
- **Bone marrow disease**: Ineffective erythropoiesis (immune-mediated, drugs/estrogen, toxins, Ehrlichia canis, histiocytic sarcoma); myelodysplastic syndrome; leukemia; myelofibrosis; parvovirus (destruction of erythroid progenitors); recombinant human EPO antibodies.
- **Decreased HB production**: Iron deficiency; lead toxicosis (chronic).
- **Decreased DNA synthesis**: Vitamin B12/folate deficiency (rare); FeLV infection.
- Bone marrow aspiration indicated for: pancytopenia; severe non-regenerative anemia without another cause; suspected leukemia or neoplastic infiltration.

#### Erythrocytosis
**Relative (not true) increase**: Dehydration (high total protein); splenic contraction (transient, epinephrine).

**Absolute (true) erythrocytosis:**
- **Appropriate secondary**: Hypoxia → EPO release (cardiac disease, right-to-left shunting PDA, respiratory disease).
- **Inappropriate secondary**: Non-hypoxic EPO release — most often renal disease (neoplastic: renal carcinoma, T-cell lymphoma; non-neoplastic: pyelonephritis); other paraneoplastic (cecal leiomyosarcoma).
- **Primary (polycythemia vera)**: Rare; JAK2 mutation documented in dogs; diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out secondary causes.

**Algorithm**: Increased HCT → Rule out high total protein/dehydration → If persistent: measure pO₂ → If no hypoxia: assess kidneys → If no renal lesion: assess for other neoplasia → If none: consider polycythemia vera (confirm JAK2 mutation or by exclusion).

### Ch 58 — Leukopenia, Leukocytosis

#### Key Thresholds
- **Neutropenia thresholds** (dog and cat):
  - Mild: 1,500–3,000 cells/mcL
  - Moderate: 500–1,500 cells/mcL
  - Severe: <500 cells/mcL — risk of fever and opportunistic infection; treat emergently if febrile
- **Severe leukocytosis threshold**: >35,000 cells/mcL (previously called "leukemoid response")
- **Degenerative left shift** prognostic: hazard ratio for death/euthanasia = 1.9 in dogs; 1.75 in cats

#### Causes of Severe Neutrophilic Leukocytosis (WBC >50,000/mcL)
| Category | Dogs (Lucroy) | Dogs (Weltan) | Cats (Lucroy) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infection | 34% (40/115) | 58%* (106/182) | 37% (38/104) |
| Immune-mediated | 32% (38/115) | 11% | 22% |
| Neoplasia | 20% (24/115) | 14% | 23% |
| Tissue necrosis | 10% (12/115) | 13% | 18% |
*Includes Babesiosis group (50/182).

**Other causes of severe neutrophilic leukocytosis**: Leukocyte adhesion deficiency; paraneoplastic leukocytosis; Babesia infection; Hepatozoon canis; IMHA; bacterial prostatitis.

**Physiologic neutrophilia limit**: Should not exceed 2× upper reference limit and should not produce a left shift.

#### Toxic Neutrophil Changes
(Indicate accelerated maturation; raise suspicion for infection)
- Cytoplasmic basophilia
- Döhle bodies
- Increased vacuolization
- Decreased nuclear condensation
- Increased granules

**Causes of Neutropenia** (dogs/cats, Brown et al., n=261):
| Category | % |
|---|---|
| Infectious disease | 52%* |
| Increased demand | 11% |
| Drug-induced | 11% |
| Primary bone marrow disease | 4% |
*123/124 dogs: parvovirus; 10/11 cats: FeLV or FIV.

**Drug causes of neutropenia**: Chloramphenicol, azathioprine, phenobarbital, phenylbutazone, methimazole, sulfa derivatives, chemotherapy agents.

#### Differential Diagnosis Summary (E-Figure 58.2)

**Neutrophilia:**
- Glucocorticoid-associated: stress, hyperadrenocorticism, glucocorticoid therapy, ACTH administration
- Catecholamine-associated: fight-or-flight, catecholamine administration
- Inflammatory: bacterial/fungal/protozoal/viral/parasitic infections; necrosis (hemolysis, ischemia, thermal, neoplasia); sterile inflammation (pancreatitis, FIP, immune-mediated)
- Neoplastic: granulocytic leukemia; paraneoplastic
- Other: regenerative anemia; leukocyte adhesion deficiency; G-CSF/GM-CSF administration; early estrogen toxicosis

**Lymphocytosis:**
- Catecholamine-associated (fight-or-flight)
- Chronic antigenic stimulation (bacterial, rickettsial, fungal, viral, protozoal especially Babesia)
- Other: hypoadrenocorticism, recent vaccination, lymphoplasmacytic enteritis/hepatitis
- Neoplastic: lymphoma with leukemic phase; lymphoid leukemia
- Note: cats more likely than dogs to develop reactive lymphocytosis with inflammation; usually mild but can be 2–3× upper reference limit

**Eosinophilia:** Hypersensitivity; parasitism (ectoparasites, heartworms, tissue nematodes); mast cell disorder; fungal infections; hypereosinophilic syndrome; eosinophilic myositis/gastroenteritis/panosteitis; paraneoplastic; eosinophilic leukemia; mast cell tumors; lymphoma.

**Basophilia:** Hypersensitivity; parasitism; drugs; insect envenomation; inflammation; paraneoplastic; mast cell tumor; basophilic leukemia; feline myeloproliferative disease; essential thrombocythemia; polycythemia vera.

**Monocytosis:** Inflammatory (infections, necrosis, sterile inflammation); glucocorticoid-associated; monocytic/myelomonocytic leukemia; immune-mediated neutropenia; cyclic hematopoiesis; GM-CSF administration.

**Neutropenia:** Cyclic hematopoiesis (Gray Collie); bone marrow disorder (necrosis, infection, drug reaction, toxicosis, chemotherapy, radiation, immune-mediated, myelophthisis, myelodysplasia, neoplasia, nutrient deficiency, trapped neutrophil syndrome); overwhelming inflammation.

**Lymphopenia:** Glucocorticoid-associated (stress, hyperadrenocorticism, therapy); lymphoid hypoplasia/aplasia (irradiation, immunosuppressive drugs, multicentric lymphoma); depletion (chylothorax, protein-losing enteropathy, lymphangiectasia); acute inflammation (bacterial/viral infection, endotoxemia).

#### Chronic Idiopathic Neutropenia in Cats
- Mild to moderate; counts occasionally <1,000/mcL; no left shift, toxic changes, clinical signs, or recurrent infection.
- Diagnosis by exclusion after ruling out retroviral infections; bone marrow assessment not needed.

### Ch 59 — Thrombocytopenia, Thrombocytosis

#### Reference Intervals (Cornell, Advia 2120)
| Species | Platelet count (× 10³/mcL) | Mean Platelet Volume (fL) |
|---|---|---|
| Canine | 186–545 | 8.4–14.1 |
| Feline | 195–624 | 9.1–24.3 |

#### Clinical Bleeding Threshold
- Spontaneous hemorrhage rarely the **sole** cause at platelet count >30,000/mcL.
- Primary ITP: typically platelet count <50,000/mcL at diagnosis.
- Severe thrombocytopenia (immune-mediated destruction): often <20,000/mcL.

#### Smear Estimation Formula
Platelet count (per mcL) = average platelets/field (10 oil-immersion fields) × 15,000

#### Classification of Thrombocytopenia (Figure 59.1)

**1. Production Defects** (high MPV, high % reticulated platelets indicate newly released):
- **Infiltrative disorders**: Leukemia (ALL, AML, CLL), non-leukemic neoplasia (lymphoma, myeloma, mast cells, histiocytes, metastatic carcinoma), myelodysplasia, myelofibrosis.
- **Drug toxicosis**: See Box 59.1 (antibiotics: cephalosporins, chloramphenicol, griseofulvin, penicillins, sulfonamides; anti-inflammatories: acetaminophen, carprofen, ibuprofen, phenylbutazone; cardiac/respiratory: procainamide, quinidine, thiazide diuretics; cytotoxic: azathioprine, chlorambucil, cisplatin, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin; miscellaneous: estrogens, phenobarbital, propylthiouracil, methimazole).
- **Infectious**: See Box 59.2.
- **Endocrine**: Estrogen toxicosis (functional ovarian granulosa cell tumor, Sertoli cell tumor, exogenous estrogen); hypothyroidism (rarely causes clinically relevant thrombocytopenia).
- **Hereditary defects**:
  - Greyhounds: mild subclinical reduction.
  - Cavalier King Charles Spaniels: macrothrombocytopenia, platelet count 25,000–75,000/mcL; beta1-tubulin gene mutation. Same mutation also in Chihuahuas, Labrador Retrievers, Shih Tzus.
  - Norfolk/Cairn Terriers: distinct beta1-tubulin mutation.
  - Cats: felid-specific beta1-tubulin structural variant causes wide variation in platelet size/count; does not cause bleeding tendency.

**2. Destruction and Consumption Defects:**
- **Primary ITP**: Middle-aged dogs, females over-represented; Cocker Spaniels, Poodles, Old English Sheepdogs predisposed. Cats less commonly affected. Diagnosis: exclusion of underlying disease + severe thrombocytopenia (<50,000/mcL) + normal to increased megakaryopoiesis + response to immunosuppressives.
- **Secondary immune thrombocytopenia**: Associated with infection, neoplasia, drug exposure, transfusion.
- **Activation/consumption**: DIC; infectious/non-infectious vasculitides.
- **Sequestration (hypersplenism)**: Neoplasia (mast cell, lymphoma, hemangiosarcoma), inflammation, congestive portal hypertension/heart failure.

**Pathogens causing infectious thrombocytopenia (Box 59.2):**
- Viral (feline): FeLV, FIV, panleukopenia virus, FIPV
- Viral (canine): distemper, herpesvirus, parvovirus, adenovirus
- Parasitic/arthropod-borne: Anaplasma (platys, phagocytophilum), Angiostrongylus, Babesia, Bartonella, Borrelia, Cytauxzoon felis, Dirofilaria, Ehrlichia (canis, platys, ewingii), Leishmania, Mycoplasma haemofelis, Rickettsia rickettsii
- Fungal/bacterial: Gram-negative/positive bacteria (septicemia), Candida, Histoplasma, Leptospira spp.

**3. Dilutional Defects**: Massive transfusion (>1 blood volume in 1 day) or high-volume resuscitation (>50% blood volume in 4 hours).

#### Thrombocytosis

**Primary (Essential) Thrombocythemia**: Autonomous; marked platelet elevation **>900,000/mcL**; rare myeloproliferative disease; JAK2, CALR, MPL driver mutations. Signs: fever, splenomegaly, anemia, thrombosis, hemorrhage.

**Reactive (Secondary) Thrombocytosis**: Chronic blood loss, infection, inflammation, malignancy, tissue necrosis; mediated by TPO and other cytokines. No specific signs; resolves with treatment of underlying disorder.

### Ch 60 — Hypoproteinemia and Hyperproteinemia

#### Albumin: Key Concepts
- ~80% of colloid oncotic pressure; synthesized by liver; requires >80% hepatocyte loss for decreased production.
- **Albumin thresholds**:
  - Mildly low: 2.1–2.5 g/dL (21–25 g/L)
  - Moderately low: 1.5–2.0 g/dL (15–20 g/L)
  - Severely low: <1.5 g/dL (<15 g/L) → risk of effusions, edema, thromboembolic complications (concurrent AT loss)
- Serum albumin >1.5–2 mg/dL usually does not result in significant effusion unless concurrent portal hypertension.
- Fluid protein >2.5–3 g/dL (>25–30 g/L) in an effusion: consistent with exudative process.

#### Algorithm: Hypoalbuminemia (Figure 60.1)
**Low albumin + Normal globulins → Decreased production or specific protein-losing conditions:**
- Hepatic dysfunction/PSS → serum bile acids, ammonia, CT/angiogram, liver biopsy
- Hypoadrenocorticism → baseline cortisol; if <2.0 mcg/dL (<55 nmol/L) → ACTH stimulation test
- PLN → urinalysis/UPC; if UPC >2.0 → renal biopsy
- Critical illness / acute-phase reaction

**Low albumin + Low globulins → GI loss most likely:**
- PLE → fecal alpha-1-protease inhibitor; GI biopsies
- Also: vasculitis/sepsis ("third-spacing")

**Rule-out thresholds:**
- PLN: UPC <2.0 rules out significant proteinuria
- Hypoadrenocorticism: baseline cortisol >2 mcg/dL (>55 nmol/L) = unlikely

#### Hyperglobulinemia
- Serum globulins >5.0 mg/dL (50 g/L): consider SPE
- **Polyclonal gammopathy** (broad beta–gamma peak): Chronic antigenic stimulation, acute-phase proteins, inflammatory/infectious/immune-mediated/neoplastic processes.
- **Monoclonal gammopathy** (narrow "spike" in beta or gamma): Neoplastic (multiple myeloma, extramedullary plasma cell tumor, lymphoma, CLL, Waldenström macroglobulinemia) or non-neoplastic (heartworm, ehrlichiosis, leishmaniasis, FIP, bartonellosis).
- **Hyperviscosity syndrome**: Globulins >6 g/dL (>60 g/L), particularly IgM, IgA, IgG → epistaxis, gum bleeding, retinal hemorrhage, hyphema, neurological signs.

### Ch 61 — Hypoglycemia and Hyperglycemia

#### Sample Handling Pearls
- Gold standard: serum or plasma separated promptly OR fluoride oxalate / citrate buffer tubes (prevent in vitro glycolysis).
- Storage without centrifugation at room temperature: significant BG decrease within 1–2 hours (lithium heparin and serum samples lose ~15–20% by 4h).
- Human PBGMs underestimate BG in dogs and cats; always confirm with reference laboratory analyzer.
- Severe polycythemia or leukocytosis can cause in vitro hypoglycemia.
- Anemia can cause falsely **increased** BG (may mask hypoglycemia).

#### Renal Glucosuria Thresholds
- Dogs: glucosuria when blood glucose >170–180 mg/dL (>9.4–10 mmol/L)
- Cats: glucosuria when blood glucose >260–310 mg/dL (>14.4–17.2 mmol/L)

#### ALIVE* Criteria for Diabetes Mellitus Diagnosis

**Dogs:**
1. Random BG ≥200 mg/dL (≥11 mmol/L) with classic signs (PU/PD, polyphagia, weight loss) or hyperglycemic crisis; OR
2. Fasting BG >125 mg/dL to ≤200 mg/dL (>7 to ≤11 mmol/L) with persistent fasting hyperglycemia >24h OR increased glycated proteins.

**Cats (stress hyperglycemia warrants caution):**
1. Random BG ≥270 mg/dL (≥15 mmol/L) with classic signs (with no other plausible cause) or hyperglycemic crisis AND at least one of: increased glycated protein OR glucosuria on ≥2 naturally voided samples at home ≥2 days after stressful event; OR
2. Random BG >125 to ≤270 mg/dL (>7 to ≤15 mmol/L) with at least two of: classic clinical signs, increased glycated protein, glucosuria (as above).

#### Potential Causes of Hypoglycemia (Table 61.1)

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Neonatal/juvenile/toy dog breed | Inadequate glycogen stores |
| Iatrogenic insulin overdose | Excess glucose utilization |
| Xylitol (dogs), ethanol, oral hypoglycemic agents, beta-blockers, alpha-lipoic acid | Various; usually hyperinsulinism |
| Refeeding syndrome | Excess glucose utilization due to hyperinsulinism |
| Malnutrition/starvation | Inadequate intake, depleted glycogen |
| Extreme exercise/hunting dog hypoglycemia | Excess utilization, inadequate stores |
| Insulinoma (common in dogs; less common in cats) | Hyperinsulinism |
| Extra-pancreatic neoplasia (dogs: hepatocellular carcinoma, hepatoma, leiomyosarcoma, leiomyoma; also cats) | Increased glucose utilization; insulin-like analogs |
| Severe infections (sepsis, babesiosis [dog], parvovirus) | Decreased hepatic gluconeogenesis, increased utilization |
| Hypoadrenocorticism (dogs and cats) | Decreased gluconeogenesis and counter-regulation |
| Severe liver disease (PSS, hepatitis, lipidosis, neoplasia, hepatotoxins) | Decreased hepatic gluconeogenesis |
| Chronic kidney failure | Decreased gluconeogenesis; impaired catecholamine counter-regulation |
| Hypothyroidism | Hyperinsulinism (reduced insulin clearance); decreased catecholamine counter-regulation |
| Islet cell hypertrophy/hyperplasia (nesidioblastosis) | Hyperinsulinism |
| Hyposomatotropism/panhypopituitarism | Decreased gluconeogenesis and counter-regulation |

#### Potential Causes of Hyperglycemia (Table 61.2)

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Diabetes mellitus | Absolute or relative insulin deficiency |
| Stress hyperglycemia | Increased hepatic gluconeogenesis; counter-regulatory hormones |
| Postprandial (dogs) | Glucose absorption exceeds insulin secretory capacity |
| IV dextrose-containing fluids | Glucose delivery exceeds insulin secretory capacity |
| Hyperadrenocorticism / glucocorticoid treatment | Insulin resistance; predominantly via hepatic gluconeogenesis |
| Diestrus (bitch)/pregnancy/progestogen treatment | Insulin resistance due to progestogens and GH |
| Pancreatitis and pancreatic neoplasia | Absolute insulin deficiency |
| Pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma | Insulin resistance from catecholamines |
| Renal failure | Urea suppresses insulin secretion; decreased capacity for glucosuric correction |
| Head trauma | Stress hyperglycemia |
| Thiazide diuretics | Hypokalemia → impaired K-dependent insulin release |

**Mild hyperglycemia**: 100–180 mg/mL (5–10 mmol/L) = typically clinically silent; may indicate subclinical DM ("pre-diabetes").

### Ch 62 — Blood Urea Nitrogen, Creatinine, and SDMA

#### Key Concepts and Non-renal Influences

**BUN:**
- 60–65% of filtered urea is reabsorbed proximally; additional reabsorption with ADH in collecting ducts.
- Non-renal increases: high-protein diet, GI hemorrhage, catabolic states (infection, prolonged exercise, rhabdomyolysis, fever, glucocorticoids, tissue necrosis, starvation).
- Non-renal decreases: hepatic insufficiency (PSS, cirrhosis, acute liver failure), polyuria.

**Creatinine (sCr):**
- Constant production from skeletal muscle creatine/phosphocreatine; production rate depends on muscle mass.
- Higher baseline in high-muscle-mass breeds (e.g., Greyhounds); lower in cachexia/sarcopenia.
- Thyroidal effects: untreated hypothyroid dogs → higher sCr; untreated hyperthyroid cats → lower sCr.
- **75% of functional renal mass must be lost** before significant changes in sCr (corresponds to ~48% decrease in GFR in dogs).

**SDMA:**
- Synthesized by all nucleated cells; >90% renally excreted by glomerular filtration.
- Less interindividual variability than sCr.
- SDMA rises with **20–40% reduction in GFR** — more sensitive than sCr.
- SDMA rises on average **9.8 months** (dogs) and **17 months** (cats) earlier than sCr in CKD patients.
- Minimal non-renal influences (not significantly affected by mitral regurgitation, pancreatitis, hyperthyroidism, muscle mass, age, sex, breed).

#### Azotemia Classification (Figure 62.1)

| Category | USG (Dog) | USG (Cat) | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-renal | >1.030 ("appropriate") | >1.035 | Hypovolemia, decreased cardiac output, altered vascular tone |
| Renal | 1.007–1.030 ("inappropriate") | 1.007–1.035 | Nephron loss, decreased glomerular patency |
| Post-renal | Variable | Variable | Obstruction or urine leakage; detected by imaging, fluid creatinine/K ratios |
| Isosthenuria | 1.008–1.012 | 1.008–1.012 | Neither concentrating nor diluting |

**Pre-renal causes:** Dehydration, shock, hemorrhage, GI losses (vomiting/diarrhea), third-spacing, hypoadrenocorticism; cardiomyopathies, tachyarrhythmias, bradyarrhythmias, sepsis/SIRS, anaphylaxis, hypoadrenocorticism; sepsis, SIRS, anaphylaxis.

**Renal causes:** CKD (glomerulonephritis, tubulointerstitial nephritis, amyloidosis, polycystic kidney disease, renal dysplasia, hydronephrosis, neoplasia); AKI (intoxications, leptospirosis, Lyme nephritis, pyelonephritis, sepsis/SIRS, ischemia).

**Post-renal causes (obstruction):** Ureteral calculi, ureteral ligation, TCC, urethral calculi, urethral plugs, urethral ligation, prostatic neoplasia, bladder herniation.

**Post-renal causes (urine leakage):** Blunt/penetrating trauma, iatrogenic procedural trauma, dehiscence after urinary surgery, non-traumatic rupture from obstruction, neoplasia.

#### Uroperitoneum Diagnostic Ratios
- **Fluid-to-blood creatinine ratio** usually >2:1 → 86% sensitivity, 100% specificity in dogs.
- **Fluid-to-blood potassium ratio** >1.4:1 (dogs) or >1.9:1 (cats) → 100% sensitivity and specificity for uroperitoneum.

### Ch 63 — Cholesterol and Triglycerides

#### Key Definitions and Thresholds
- **Lipemia** (grossly visible turbidity): typically triglycerides >200–300 mg/dL (>2.3–3.4 mmol/L)
- **Chylomicron test**: leave lipemic serum undisturbed at 4°C for 12h; cream layer = chylomicrons; turbid remaining serum = excess VLDL.
- **Post-prandial hyperlipidemia**: resolves within 7–12h after a meal; minimum 12h fast required before testing triglycerides/cholesterol.
- **Lipoprotein lipase activity test**: measure triglycerides before and 10 min after 40–90 IU/kg heparin IV; decrease in triglycerides = functional lipoprotein lipase.

### Ch 64 — Amylase and Lipase

#### Serum Amylase Activity
- **NOT clinically useful** for diagnosing pancreatitis: neither specific (amylase present after total pancreatectomy) nor sensitive. Increases in renal, hepatic, intestinal, and neoplastic disease.

#### Serum Lipase Assays — Comparison

| Assay | Substrate | Specificity | Utility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,2-DiG-based | 1,2-diglyceride | Low (non-pancreatic lipases measured) | Historical; low sensitivity and specificity |
| DGGR-based (PSL, Antech) | DGGR | Moderate but not specific | Better than 1,2-DiG; heparin administration increases result (releases hepatic/lipoprotein lipases) |
| Triolein-based (V-Lip, Fuji) | Triolein | Limited (hemolysis/icterus interfere) | Only 58% EPI dogs have low 20th-percentile result |
| **PLI — Spec cPL/Spec fPL (IDEXX)** | Immunoassay (pancreatic lipase-specific) | **High** — analytically validated | Most sensitive/specific; gold standard |

**PLI key points:**
- cPLI is non-detectable/severely decreased in EPI dogs → confirms pancreatic origin.
- cPLI is NOT raised in experimentally induced renal disease → specific to pancreas.
- Spec cPL and Spec fPL are analytically validated (not all commercial PLI assays are).
- SNAP cPL/fPL (IDEXX): semiquantitative rapid rule-out. Normal = pancreatitis unlikely. Positive = pancreatitis possible or in "grey zone"; requires quantitative PLI for confirmation.
- Sensitivity varies widely (33–100% across studies); highest for acute severe disease; lower for chronic mild disease.
- Elevated PLI can occur with secondary pancreatic damage (cardiac, infectious, neurological, endocrine, renal diseases).

### Ch 65 — Liver Enzymes

#### Four Enzymes Used in Veterinary Practice
1. **ALT** — hepatocellular leakage enzyme
2. **AST** — hepatocellular leakage enzyme (also in skeletal muscle, cardiac, kidneys, brain, erythrocytes)
3. **ALP** — cholestatic enzyme (canalicular membrane)
4. **GGT** — cholestatic enzyme (canalicular membrane and microsomes)

#### ALT
- "Liver-specific" in dogs and cats; also in lesser amounts in heart, kidney, skeletal muscle.
- Half-life: 40–60 hours (dog); ~3–4 hours (cat).
- **Sensitivity**: 80–100% for hepatic necrosis/failure; 45–80% for hepatitis, cirrhosis, vacuolar hepatopathy, passive congestion, portovascular anomalies (dog).
- Cats: 80–100% sensitive for extrahepatic bile duct occlusion and cholangitis/cholangiohepatitis.
- Largest increases: necroinflammatory disease (drug/toxin, infectious, copper-associated hepatopathy, autoimmune).
- **Aflatoxin B1 exception**: inhibits biosynthesis of ALT and AST — not usable as biomarkers post-intoxication.
- Glucocorticoids in dogs: may induce ALT transcription but do not cause significant increases in cultured hepatocyte production.
- Cats: ALT may increase with hemolysis, hyperthyroidism, or rhabdomyolysis.

#### AST
- Greatest concentrations in skeletal muscle, cardiac myocytes, kidneys.
- Concurrent increase in CK = muscular source of AST elevation.
- Half-life: ~22 hours or longer (dog); ~77 minutes (cat).
- In cats, AST is more sensitive indicator of hepatocellular damage than ALT; most markedly increased in necroinflammatory diseases, infiltrative lymphoma, myeloproliferative disease, chronic bile duct obstruction.

#### ALP
- Dog: Two hepatic canalicular isoenzymes — L-ALP and G-ALP (glucocorticoid-induced, expressed from intestinal ALP gene).
  - G-ALP induced by exogenous glucocorticoids (delayed ~10 days), chronic diseases, anticonvulsants (phenobarbital).
  - L-ALP and G-ALP half-life: 2–3 days.
  - Feline L-ALP half-life: 6 hours (much shorter than dog → smaller magnitude of increase in cats is still significant).
- **Cats do NOT express G-ALP** → glucocorticoid therapy does not typically cause significant ALP elevation in cats.
- Bone ALP (B-ALP): present in growing dogs and cats up to 7 months; also in renal secondary hyperparathyroidism, bone tumors (negative prognostic sign for osteosarcoma), osteomyelitis, feline hyperthyroidism.
- High sensitivity but **low specificity** for hepatobiliary disease (dog); high specificity when concurrent GGT also elevated.
- **Scottish Terriers**: breed-related hyperphosphatasemia; associated with G-ALP, progressive vacuolar hepatopathy, adrenal dysfunction, hepatocellular carcinoma.
- Cats: ALP increase in hepatic lipidosis often predominates; also intrahepatic/extrahepatic biliary injury.

#### GGT
- Dog: largest tissue concentration in proximal renal tubules and pancreas (lost into urine/pancreatic lumen rather than circulation → low serum GGT from these sources).
- **Sensitivity (dog)**: ~40% for liver disease — not sensitive; **higher specificity** than ALP.
- **Cats**: Sensitivity 55%; ALP exceeds GGT specificity in cats; GGT most markedly increased in necroinflammatory diseases of portal tract, biliary tree, and pancreas.
- Both ALP and GGT elevated: more likely to be true liver disease.
- Extrahepatic bile duct obstruction: moderate to severe GGT increases (generally greater in dogs than cats).
- GGT NOT significantly elevated in simple hepatic lipidosis (unlike ALP which increases markedly).
- Neonatal dogs: markedly increased GGT for first 1–3 days due to colostrum.

#### Non-Hepatic Causes of Increased Liver Enzymes (Table 65.1)
| Condition | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Congestion, hypotension, ischemia |
| Endocrinopathies | Adrenal (cortisol/sex steroid excess), DM, hyperthyroidism (cats), hypothyroidism (dogs) |
| GI disease | Diarrhea, constipation/obstipation, GDV, pancreatitis |
| Infectious disease | Abscessation, rickettsial, pyelonephritis, pyometra/prostatitis, septicemia, parvovirus, coronavirus |
| Miscellaneous | Strenuous exercise, trauma, myopathies, severe anemia, malignant hyperthermia |
| Neoplasia | Bone, mammary, metastatic |
| Osseous | Growth, metabolic bone disease, osteomyelitis |

#### Hepatotoxic Drug List (Box 65.1)
Acetaminophen, amiodarone, anabolic steroids, azathioprine, carprofen, cyclosporine, diazepam, diethylcarbamazine/oxibendazole, fluoroquinolones, griseofulvin, halothane, itraconazole, ketoconazole, lomustine (CCNU), mebendazole, methimazole, mibolerone, pennyroyal oil, phenobarbital, sulfonamides, tetracyclines.

#### Monitoring Parameters
- Following acute hepatic injury: transaminases rise within 24–48h, begin to decrease within 2–3 days, normalize in 2–3 weeks.
- Waxing and waning values: suggest chronic inflammatory condition.
- Reduction in enzyme activity in chronic liver disease may reflect improvement OR loss of hepatocytes.

### Ch 66 — Creatine Kinase

#### Key Facts
- CK is predominantly CK-MM (100% in skeletal muscle, >90% in cardiac muscle of dogs/cats).
- CK-BB: found in nervous system; can increase non-specifically with neurological conditions.
- **Serum half-life**: 2–4 hours (dog) → increases within hours of cell damage, return to reference levels within 48h if damage ceases.
- Measure in **serum** (not plasma — platelets release CK during clotting).
- Healthy puppies and kittens (<8 weeks): CK often ~1.5–2× adult reference values.
- Stability: stable ~7 days at +4°C; ~1 month at −20°C.

#### Causes of Elevated [CK]
- **Common**: Blunt trauma, IM injection, surgery, excessive physical restraint.
- **Other**: Recent excessive exercise, hyperthermia/heat stroke, chronic recumbency.
- **Hereditary myopathy**: Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, Chow Chows, Irish Setters.
- **Infectious**: Toxoplasmosis, neosporosis.
- **Endocrine**: Hypothyroidism; hyperthyroid cats (hypermetabolic state, hypoperfusion, chronic skeletal muscle ischemia).
- **Immune-mediated**: Dermatomyositis, masticatory myositis.
- **Nutritional**: Hypokalemia (cats).
- **Intoxication**: Snake/spider envenomation.
- **Neurological**: Seizures, space-occupying intracranial lesions, meningitis.
- **Neoplasia**.
- **Anorexia (cats)**: Almost any cause of anorexia can increase CK, presumably due to muscle catabolism; normalizes with nutritional support.

#### Prognostic Notes
- Ill cats with CK >7,500 U/L: higher mortality, longer hospitalization, increased costs.
- Persistent confirmed [CK] increase in cats = significant; warrants further investigation.
- Mild increase (<5× reference range) with history of mild trauma: excellent prognosis; should normalize within days.

### Ch 67 — Sodium and Chloride

#### Key Formulas (Box 67.1)
```
Osmolality (mOsm/kg) = 2(Na) + (BUN/2.8) + (Glucose/18)
  [for mmol/L units: remove denominators 2.8 and 18]

Effective osmolality (mOsm/kg) = Osmolality – (BUN/2.8)
  [BUN in mg/dL]

Corrected Cl = (Normal Na/Patient Na) × Patient Cl
  [Normal Na = midpoint of reference interval]

Total body water (TBW) = Weight (kg) × 0.6

Expected change in Na with 1 L of fluids:
  = (Fluid Na + K – Patient Na) / (TBW + 1)

Corrected Na for glucose:
  = Measured Na + [1.6 to 2.4 × (Glucose – 100)/100]
  [Metric: measured Na (mmol/L) + (1.6 to 2.4 × [glucose (mmol/L) – 5.6] / 5.6)]

Corrected Na for lipid:
  1 mg/dL triglyceride decreases Na by 0.002 mEq/L
  [Metric: 1 mmol/L triglyceride decreases Na by 0.18 mmol/L]

Corrected Na for protein:
  1 g/dL protein above 8 g/dL decreases Na by 0.25 mEq/L
  [Metric: each 10 g/L above 80 g/L decreases Na by 0.25 mmol/L]
```

#### Hypernatremia
**Causes by volume status:**
- *Hypovolemic*: Loop diuretics/mannitol; DM; CKD; post-obstructive diuresis; GI disease; pancreatitis; toxin ingestion.
- *Euvolemic*: Diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic); inability to ingest water; neurological disease; primary hypodipsia.
- *Hypervolemic*: Inappropriate fluid therapy; hyperaldosteronism; hypercortisolism; salt intoxication (homemade playdough, clay).

**Pseudohypernatremia**: Hypoproteinemia can impact reported [Na] — verify with direct ion-selective electrode.

**Chronic hypernatremia**: Accumulation of non-ionic organic osmolytes (amino acids, polyalcohols) protects CNS — can be relatively well tolerated; rapid correction is dangerous.
**Acute hypernatremia** (e.g., salt intoxication): quickly causes ataxia, disorientation, seizures (CNS cell shrinkage).

#### Hyponatremia
**Causes by volume status:**
- *Hypovolemic*: GI disease (vomiting/diarrhea); pancreatitis; hypoadrenocorticism; trichuriasis.
- *Euvolemic*: Diuretics; DM; SIAD; psychogenic polydipsia; desmopressin-treated psychogenic polydipsia.
- *Hypervolemic*: Excess/inappropriate fluid therapy; advanced CKD; nephrotic syndrome; liver failure; cardiac compromise/CHF.
- Also: pancreatitis, uroabdomen, peritonitis (third-spacing mechanisms).

**Pseudohyponatremia**: Hyperlipidemia and hyperproteinemia can decrease measured [Na] depending on assay method → verify with direct ion-selective electrode.

**Dilutional (corrected Na normal)**: Hyperglycemia → water drawn out of cells → dilutes Na. Calculate corrected Na for glucose to reveal "true" Na status.

**SIAD (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuresis)**: Impaired urinary dilution with urine [Na] >20 mEq/L; associated with neurologic, neoplastic, or inflammatory disorders; reported in dogs with babesiosis.

**Hypoadrenocorticism**: Aldosterone deficiency → renal Na loss; cortisol deficiency → increased ADH → water retention and dilution.

#### Hyperchloremia
- True hyperchloremia = metabolic acidosis (normal anion gap) → chloride is the predominant strong ECF anion.
- **Causes**: Iatrogenic (0.9% NaCl, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, zonisamide, KCl administration, TPN); disproportionate Na loss (diarrhea); DM; AKI/CKD; renal tubular acidosis; HCO3 loss; babesiosis.
- **Pseudohyperchloremia**: Hemolysis, hyperlipidemia, bromide therapy → use direct ion-selective electrode.

#### Hypochloremia
- Corrected Cl normal → likely free water excess; pursue hyponatremia differentials.
- **Causes**: Glucocorticoids; loop/thiazide diuretics; NaHCO3 administration; vomiting (especially gastric outflow obstruction → hallmark: hyperchloremic metabolic alkalosis + paradoxical aciduria); chronic respiratory acidosis (compensatory); hypercortisolism; tumor lysis syndrome; trichuriasis.

(Ch 67)

### Ch 68 — Potassium, Magnesium

#### Potassium Reference Intervals
- Dogs and cats: approximately **4.0–5.5 mEq/L** (= 4.0–5.5 mmol/L).

#### Pseudohyperkalemia (Artifactual Elevation)
- High intra-RBC K+ breeds: **Akita, Shiba Inu, English Springer Spaniel** — hemolysis releases dramatically elevated K+.
- Other causes: severe leukocytosis (>100,000 leukocytes/mcL); severe thrombocytosis (platelets release K during clotting in serum); neonates; EDTA/oxalate contamination; K-containing IV fluids.
- Photometric methods can give false K decreases with lipemic/hyperproteinemic samples; ion-selective electrodes are not affected.
- Severe hyperglycemia (>750 mg/dL, >42 mmol/L) or azotemia (BUN >115 mg/dL, >41 mmol/L) can cause pseudohypokalemia.

#### Hypokalemia Thresholds and Clinical Signs
- Mild: 3.0–4.0 mEq/L
- Moderate: 2.5–3.0 mEq/L
- Severe: <2.5 mEq/L

**Clinical signs expected when:**
- Cats: <3.5 mEq/L
- Dogs: <2.5 mEq/L
- Severe (<2 mEq/L): rhabdomyolysis, respiratory muscle paralysis.

**Classic feline sign**: Cervical ventroflexion (hypokalemic myopathy); also generalized weakness, plantigrade stance, hypermetria; may increase serum CK.

**Cardiac effects**: Decreased T-wave amplitude, shortened S-T segments, prolonged Q-T intervals, prominent U waves, arrhythmias.

**Renal effects**: Impairs ADH release → polyuria/polydipsia; hypokalemic nephropathy → tubulointerstitial nephritis, impaired renal function, azotemia.

#### FEk Formula
FEk = (Uk/Sk) / (Ucr/Scr) × 100 (%)
- Uk = urinary K+ (mEq/L); Sk = serum K+ (mEq/L); Ucr = urinary creatinine; Scr = serum creatinine (mg/dL)
- FEk <6% = K+ loss is NOT renal.

#### DDx — Hypokalemia (Figure 68.1)
- **GI/renal losses**: Vomiting/diarrhea (FEk >6%); CKD (especially cats); post-obstructive diuresis; primary hyperaldosteronism; distal renal tubular acidosis type 1; hypercortisolism (dogs).
- **Decreased intake/absorption**: Prolonged anorexia; K+-deficient diet; clay ingestion.
- **Iatrogenic**: K+-deficient IV fluids (0.45% NaCl, 0.9% NaCl, 5% dextrose, Ringer's solutions with only 4 mEq/L K+); furosemide; hydrochlorothiazide; glucocorticoids; mineralocorticoids; amphotericin B; penicillin derivatives; NaHCO3; peritoneal dialysis.
- **Translocation to ICF**: Alkalemia; insulin + glucose; catecholamines (albuterol); beta-adrenergics; TPN; hypothermia.
- **Other**: Hypomagnesemia; hyperthyroidism; rattlesnake envenomation; clay cat litter (bentonite) ingestion; pheochromocytoma; Burmese cats (familial hypokalemic periodic paralysis); hypokalemia does NOT cause alkalosis in dogs/cats but metabolic alkalosis can cause K+ shift to ICF.

#### Hyperkalemia Thresholds and Clinical Signs
- Severe: **>8.0 mEq/L**
- Clinical signs generally not expected until >7.5–8.0 mEq/L.

**ECG abnormalities** (severity progression): Tall narrow T waves → decreased P-wave amplitude → prolonged QRS → prolonged PR → P-wave abolishment → ST deviation → heart block → ventricular arrhythmias → cardiac arrest.

#### DDx — Hyperkalemia (Figure 68.2)
- **Decreased excretion**: AKI; CKD (late stages); urethral or bilateral ureteral obstruction; uroabdomen (ruptured ureter or bladder, usually within 48h in dogs); primary hypoadrenocorticism (most common significant cause); late pregnancy in Greyhounds.
- **GI causes**: Trichuriasis, salmonellosis, perforated duodenal ulcer.
- **Third-spacing**: Chylothorax, pleural effusion, ascites.
- **Transcellular shift**: DKA (osmotic shift + acidosis); metabolic/respiratory acidosis; massive tissue breakdown (acute tumor lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, reperfusion injury); hyperkalemic periodic paralysis (young Pit Bull reported); hypothermia.
- **Iatrogenic**: Excessive IV K+ supplementation; K+-containing drugs (K bromide, K citrate); non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol); alpha-agonists (phenylpropanolamine); ACE inhibitors; ARBs; K+-sparing diuretics (spironolactone); cardiac glycosides; prostaglandin inhibitors (NSAIDs); some antibiotics (trimethoprim, K-penicillin G); succinylcholine; heparin; cyclosporine/tacrolimus; trilostane (→ aldosterone suppression → potentially fatal hyperkalemia).

#### Magnesium Reference Intervals
| Species | Total (tMg++) | Ionized (iMg++) |
|---|---|---|
| Dog (general acceptable) | 1.5–3.0 mg/dL (0.6–1.2 mmol/L) | 1–1.9 mg/dL (0.4–0.6 mmol/L) |
| Dog (reported RI) | 1.7–2.5 mg/dL (0.7–1.03 mmol/L) | 0.51–1.4 mg/dL (0.21–0.58 mmol/L) |
| Cat (reported RI) | 1.3–2.6 mg/dL (0.53–1.07 mmol/L) | 1.04–1.53 mg/dL (0.43–0.63 mmol/L) |

**Hypomagnesemia clinically evident**: tMg++ <1.0 mg/dL; iMg++ <0.4 mmol/L.

**Critical threshold for hypermagnesemia**: tMg++ >12 mg/dL (>4.9 mmol/L) → can block calcium channels → cardiac arrest.

#### Hypomagnesemia
- Most common electrolyte disorder in critically ill pets (54–67%).
- Mg++ depletion → impairs PTH secretion → transient hypoparathyroidism → hypocalcemia refractory to Ca supplementation until Mg++ corrected.
- Mg++ depletion → impairs Na+/K+-ATPase → K+ exits cells → excreted → hypokalemia refractory to K+ supplementation until Mg++ corrected.

**DDx (Figure 68.3):**
- GI: Protein-losing enteropathy; malabsorption; short bowel syndrome; colonic neoplasia; chronic diarrhea; acute pancreatitis.
- Renal: DM/DKA; AKI; post-obstructive diuresis; renal tubular acidosis.
- Drugs: Gentamicin, cisplatin, digoxin, cyclosporine, ticarcillin, carbenicillin, diuretics (except K+-sparing), osmotic agents, proton pump inhibitors, insulin.
- ICF shift: Hypothermia; catecholamine excess; insulin/glucose administration; alkalosis.
- Other: Hyperthyroidism; primary hyperaldosteronism; hypoparathyroidism; lactation; brachycephalic dogs (predisposed).

#### Hypermagnesemia
**DDx (Figure 68.4):**
- Most common: Renal failure; urinary tract obstruction.
- Iatrogenic: Mg-containing fluids, antacids, cathartics; methylprednisolone acetate (cats); spironolactone; ACE inhibitors.
- Other: Primary hyperparathyroidism; hypothyroidism; hypoadrenocorticism; thoracic neoplasia/pleural effusion (cats).

### Ch 69 — Calcium and Phosphorus

#### Calcium Fractions
- ~50% ionized (iCa — physiologically active)
- ~40% protein-bound (albumin)
- ~10% chelated with lactate, citrate, bicarbonate

**Sample handling**: iCa measured by ion-specific electrode; transport anaerobically and assay promptly (sample pH affects results). Ca cannot be measured in EDTA, citrate, or oxalate anticoagulants.

**Corrected calcium formula for hypoalbuminemia:**
While a corrected Ca formula is commonly used, this chapter notes iCa measurement is superior. The author recommends confirming ionized hypercalcemia before investigating.

#### Hypercalcemia — DDx (Box 69.1)

**Common causes:**
- Malignancy: Lymphoma (most common malignancy-related cause; inconsistent association — not all lymphoma dogs have hyperCa); adenocarcinoma of apocrine glands of anal sac; multiple myeloma; miscellaneous (SCC, OSA, lung carcinoma, mammary carcinoma, malignant melanoma, metastatic bone tumors).
- Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHP): Most dogs have moderate to marked hyperCa and low-normal or low phos.
- Hypoadrenocorticism: ~30% of dogs hyperCa at diagnosis; increases in tCa and iCa tend to be mild.
- Chronic kidney disease: Small number are hyperCa (most are normoCa or hypoCa).
- Granulomatous disease: Ectopic vitD production.
- Hypervitaminosis D: Rodenticides containing cholecalciferol; topical vitD analogs (calcipotriene); dietary over-supplementation; vitD overdose treating hypoparathyroidism.
- Cholecalciferol rodenticide intoxication.

**Less common causes:** Bone lesions (osteolytic); hyperlipidemia; juvenile dogs; lab error; hyperproteinemia.

**Cats (differences from dogs):** CKD and malignancy (especially SCC) are the most frequent. Anal sac adenocarcinoma and hypoadrenocorticism uncommon. **Idiopathic hypercalcemia** is an important differential in cats: both tCa and iCa moderately increased, PTH normal or low, PTHrp within reference range. Diagnosis by exclusion.

| tCa | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ or N or ↓ | N or ↑ |
| iCa | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | N or ↓ | N or ↑ |
| Phos | N or ↓ | N or ↓ | N or ↑ | ↑ | N or ↑ |

#### Diagnostic Algorithm for Hypercalcemia (Figure 69.1)
1. History + PE + hematology/biochemistry/UA + imaging + FNA/biopsy of masses.
2. Confirm ionized hypercalcemia.
3. Measure basal cortisol → screen for hypoadrenocorticism (even if classic electrolyte abnormalities absent).
4. Measure PTH:
   - Increased → Primary hyperparathyroidism (consider CKD if concurrent azotemia, hyperphos, isosthenuria).
   - Decreased → Measure PTHrp:
     - Increased → Malignancy (normal PTHrp does not rule out malignancy).
     - Normal → Measure vitamin D metabolites:
       - 25(OH)D increased → Hypervitaminosis D (rodenticide/dietary).
       - 1,25(OH)₂D increased → Hypervitaminosis D; consider granulomatous disease.

#### Hypocalcemia — DDx (Box 69.2)

**Common causes:** Hypoalbuminemia (low tCa but usually normal iCa); acute and chronic kidney failure; hypoparathyroidism; eclampsia; protein-losing enteropathies; pancreatitis; exocrine pancreatic insufficiency; trauma; SIRS.

**Uncommon causes:** Phosphate-containing enemas; tumor lysis syndrome; hypomagnesemia; nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism.

**Mechanisms:**
- **Hypoalbuminemia**: Low tCa but iCa usually normal; PLE, PLN, liver failure.
- **Impaired PTH secretion**: Idiopathic primary hypoparathyroidism (uncommon in dogs/cats); iatrogenic (thyroidectomy in cats → damage/removal of parathyroid glands).
- **Increased Ca loss**: Eclampsia (young small dogs, 1–5 weeks post-whelping; rare in cats); CKD (reduced 1-alpha-hydroxylase → reduced vitD → decreased GI Ca absorption).
- **VitD deficiency**: CKD; PLE (malabsorption of vitD); exocrine pancreatic insufficiency.
- **Precipitation/chelation**: Ethylene glycol → Ca oxalate in renal tubules; AKI (rising phos complexes Ca); urinary tract obstruction; acute tumor lysis syndrome; pancreatitis (Ca sequestration in peripancreatic fat/saponification); blood transfusion (citrate chelation); furosemide.

#### Phosphate

**Hyperphosphatemia (Box 69.3) — Common causes:**
- Young growing healthy animals
- Hypervitaminosis D
- AKI or CKD (most common)
- Hypoparathyroidism

**Hyperphosphatemia — Uncommon causes:** Excessive dietary intake; osteolytic bone lesions; uroabdomen; hyperthyroidism; hyperadrenocorticism; metabolic acidosis; tumor cell lysis syndrome; iatrogenic (phosphate-containing enemas, IV phosphorus).

**Hypophosphatemia (Box 69.4) — Common causes:**
- Primary hyperparathyroidism
- Malignancy-associated hypercalcemia
- Diabetic ketoacidosis

**Hypophosphatemia — Uncommon causes:** Vitamin D deficiency; decreased dietary intake; renal tubular disorders; respiratory and metabolic acidosis; iatrogenic (phosphate binders, parenteral glucose or NaHCO3 administration).

**Clinical significance of hypophosphatemia**: Mild = typically no signs; marked = hemolysis, respiratory distress, neurological abnormalities.

#### Glucosuria Thresholds (Renal Tubular Reabsorption Capacity)
- Dogs: glucosuria when BG >170–180 mg/dL (>9.4–10 mmol/L)
- Cats: glucosuria when BG >260–310 mg/dL (>14.4–17.2 mmol/L)

#### Proteinuria Interpretation
- Dipstick 1+ protein: unlikely significant if USG >1.012 or pH >8.
- All dipstick "1+" results with USG <1.012 → further assess with UPC.
- Significant glomerular proteinuria: causes hypoalbuminemia when severe (UPC typically >2.0).
- Post-glomerular proteinuria (UTI, pyelonephritis, genital origin): presence of RBCs and WBCs on sediment.
- **Persistent proteinuria** affects ~20% of elderly dogs.
- **Functional/pre-renal proteinuria causes**: Hemolysis (hemoglobin), rhabdomyolysis (myoglobin), plasma cell cancers (immunoglobulins); also exercise, fever, seizures.
- **Tubular proteinuria**: Fanconi syndrome, gentamicin toxicosis (impaired resorption of filtered LMW proteins and albumin).

#### Bilirubinuria
- Dogs: Renal threshold for bilirubin is low; canine kidneys synthesize bilirubin → low but detectable bilirubin in concentrated urine from healthy male dogs is normal.
- Cats: Bilirubinuria is **always significant**.
- Bilirubinuria before hyperbilirubinemia: possible in dogs.

#### Hematuria/Hemoglobinuria/Myoglobinuria Differentiation
- Dipstick sensitivity for blood: 5–20 RBCs/mcL (vs >2,500 RBCs/mcL needed for visual recognition).
- Confirm with sediment examination.
- **Hemoglobinuria**: plasma is pink/red; hemolysis present.
- **Myoglobinuria**: plasma is clear; serum CK ± AST elevated; urine discolored red/brown (not pink/red); due to trauma, ischemia, or necrosis of muscle.
- **Hematuria**: RBCs in sediment; causes include bleeding disorder, UTI, genital origin, neoplasia, calculi, trauma.

#### Casts
- **<2 hyaline/lpf**: can be normal.
- **Cellular casts** (WBC, epithelial), fatty, waxy casts: indicate significant renal injury/necrosis (ischemia, inflammation, toxins).
- **RBC casts**: localize bleeding to nephrons.

#### Crystalluria Interpretation
- Struvite, amorphous phosphate, oxalate in healthy animals: normal.
- **Urate crystals**: usually abnormal (exception: Dalmatians).
- **Cysteine crystals**: usually abnormal.
- **Large quantities of calcium oxalate**: usually abnormal.
- Crystalluria does NOT predict urolithiasis.

### Ch 71 — Fluid Analysis: Thoracic, Abdominal, Joint

#### Sample Handling
- Collect into EDTA (purple top) for cytology; EDTA inhibits bacterial growth and preserves cellular features.
- Also collect into non-anticoagulant tube if biochemical tests or culture desired.
- Make slides immediately after collection; rapidly air-dry.
- Storage artifacts (occurring within 30–120 min): cell deterioration, neutrophil swelling (mimics degeneration), in vitro phagocytosis of RBCs and bacteria (erythrophagia can occur in vitro), bacterial proliferation.
- Ship refrigerated; smears at room temperature.

Pleural (uncommon): neoplasia, cardiac disease, lung lobe torsion |
| **Transudate (high-protein / "modified")** | >25 | <3–5 | As above; variable RBCs from hemorrhage (microtrauma or altered vascular integrity) | Peritoneal: CHF (dog, post-sinusoidal), liver disease (sinusoidal), non-exfoliating neoplasia. Pleural: neoplasia, cardiac disease, lung lobe torsion |
| **Lymphocyte-rich effusion (chylous when chylomicrons present)** | >25 (can be falsely increased by chylomicrons) | Variable; >3.0 | High proportion of small lymphocytes; lipid vacuoles in macrophages; neutrophils may dominate if longstanding | Pleural: cardiac (cats), idiopathic (dogs/cats), neoplasia (thymoma), parasitic granuloma. Peritoneal (rare): neoplasia (lymphoma), lymphangiectasia, abdominal abscess/granuloma/adhesions |
| **Classic exudate** | >25 | >5.0 | Neutrophils dominate (may be degenerate) or mixed neutrophils and macrophages; cause may be identified | Bacteria, fungi, parasites (Mesocestoides), neoplasia, foreign body, ruptured/leaking viscus, tissue/organ inflammation (pancreatitis, pneumonia) |
| **FIP effusion** | >25, often >50 | <5.0 (sometimes higher) | Mixed mildly degenerate neutrophils (60–80%) + macrophages (20–40%); fibrin clots; proteinaceous (stippled) background; few mesothelial cells and lymphocytes | Feline coronavirus/FIP |
| **Hemorrhagic effusion** | Usually >25 | Depends on PCV | Many RBCs; no/few platelets; evidence of RBC breakdown (erythrophagia, hemosiderophages, hematoidin crystals); leukocytes are blood-associated unless concurrent inflammation | Trauma, hemostatic disorder (anticoagulant rodenticide), neoplasia (hemangiosarcoma). Pleural: parasites (Angiostrongylus vasorum), bacteria (Streptococcus zooepidemicus) |
| **Biliary rupture/leakage** | Variable, often >30 | Variable, often >5.0 | Mixed mostly neutrophils + macrophages; yellow-brown bile in background or phagocytes; "white bile" (mucus); concurrent hemorrhage | Trauma, mucocele (dogs), cholelithiasis, neoplasia, necrotizing cholecystitis |
| **Uroperitoneum** | Variable | Variable (initially low, becomes exudative with time) | Initially low protein/cell count (urine dilution); then sterile peritonitis | Trauma, urolithiasis, neoplasia, severe necrotizing inflammation |
| **GI rupture/leakage** | Variable | Variable | Food material and/or phagocytized bacteria with peritoneal fluid cells | Trauma, severe inflammation, obstruction, torsion |
| **Neoplastic effusion** | Variable, often >25 | Variable | Neoplastic cells in fluid (more common with round/epithelial cell tumors — lymphoma, carcinoma, mast cell, mesothelioma) | Lymphoma, carcinoma, mast cell tumor, mesothelioma |

**Key rule**: Low-protein vs. high-protein transudates → helps localize site of transudation in peritoneal effusions:
- **Low-protein transudate** (<25 g/L): prehepatic or presinusoidal hepatic hypertension (intestinal lymph leaks); liver disease, hypoalbuminemia.
- **High-protein transudate** (≥25 g/L): late sinusoidal or post-sinusoidal hypertension (hepatic lymph leaks); liver disease, CHF (dogs), cancer.

#### Diagnostic Threshold Values
- **Chylous effusion confirmation**: Fluid triglyceride >1:1 ratio vs serum OR >1.1 mmol/L (>100 mg/dL). Note: anorectic animals may have triglycerides <1.1 mmol/L but fluid is still typically lymphocyte-rich.
- **Bile peritonitis confirmation**: Effusion bilirubin usually ≥2× serum.
- **Uroperitoneum confirmation**: Fluid creatinine ≥2× serum creatinine (often).
- **Hemorrhagic effusion definition**: PCV usually >5%; RBC count usually >1 × 10¹²/L; does not clot in non-anticoagulant tube (confirms in vivo hemorrhage vs blood contamination).
- **FIP**: Total protein often >50 g/L with TNCC usually <5 × 10⁹/L (unusual pattern for an exudate).

#### Hypoalbuminemia as Cause of Effusion
- Severe hypoalbuminemia <1.5 g/dL (<15 g/L) can cause transudative effusion, but rarely sole cause.

#### Synovial Fluid

**Normal synovial fluid:**
- TNCC (dogs): 0.5–3.0 × 10⁹/L between joints; most joints <1.0 × 10⁹/L; general upper limit = **<3.0 × 10⁹/L**.
- TNCC (cats): generally <1 × 10⁹/L.
- Total protein (refractometer): <25 g/L (<2.5 g/dL); ideally <15 g/L.
- Cytology: Large mononuclear cells (macrophages/synoviocytes); <20% lymphocytes; **<10% neutrophils**.
- Viscosity: Strand ≥2 cm (normal).

**Algorithm for Synovial Fluid Interpretation (Figure 71.5):**

| TNCC (×10⁹/L) | TP (g/L) | Viscosity | Predominant cells | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| <3.0 | <25 | Normal | Small + large mononuclear; <10% PMN; few reactive macs | Normal joint or DJD |
| <5.0 | <35 | Normal or decreased | Small + large mononuclear; <10–<60% PMN; few to increased reactive macs | DJD; acute trauma; acute-on-chronic DJD |
| >5.0 | Variable, usually >25 | Decreased or normal | >60% PMN (usually non-degenerate); ± reactive macs; ± leukophagia | Inflammatory joint disease (IJD) |

**IJD differentiation (immune-mediated vs infectious):**
- Immune-mediated: Ragocytes; LE cells; positive ANA; positive RF; steroid-responsive; erosive vs non-erosive on radiographs.
- Infectious: Morulae in PMN (Ehrlichia ewingii, Anaplasma phagocytophilum in dogs); bacteria in PMN; positive serologic/genetic tests; positive culture (only 44–63% sensitive); doxycycline-responsive.
- Note: Acute joint trauma (e.g., cranial cruciate rupture) can cause mild inflammation — TNCC up to 12 × 10⁹/L with up to 65% neutrophils — localized to affected joint.
- Cats: Infectious arthritis (Mycoplasma, calicivirus) more common than immune-mediated.
- Anaplasma phagocytophilum occurs in cats and causes limb pain; synovial findings not described.
- Polyarthropathy involving carpi and tarsi: highly suspicious for immune-mediated disease.
- Single affected joint: raises suspicion for bacterial infection (penetrating foreign body).
- Concurrent thrombocytopenia: prompts testing for infectious organisms.
- Bacterial culture positive in only 44–63% of suspected septic arthritis cases.
