## Ch 132 — Toxin Exposure Therapy / Decontamination

### Emesis — Induction

**Apomorphine (dogs)**
- Dose: 0.03 mg/kg IV, or placed in conjunctival sac
- Mechanism: centrally acting; stimulates dopamine receptors in CRTZ
- Expected recovery: 40–60% of stomach contents at best; most effective within 30–90 min post-ingestion

**Ropinirole (Clevor) eye drops (dogs)**
- Also stimulates dopamine receptors in CRTZ; induces vomiting

**Hydrogen peroxide 3% (dogs only)**
- Dose: 0.5 mL/kg PO, up to 50 mL total
- May repeat once if no emesis after 10 minutes
- **CONTRAINDICATED in cats** — can cause necroulcerative hemorrhagic gastritis

**Dexmedetomidine (cats)**
- Dose: 7 mcg/kg IV
- Cats' CRTZ is regulated by alpha receptors

**Xylazine (cats)**
- Dose: 0.44 mg/kg IM

**Emesis contraindications (all species)**
- Caustic/corrosive substances
- Petroleum distillates
- Patient showing clinical signs / unable to protect airway
- Uncontrolled vomiting or regurgitation

---

### Oral Decontamination — Dilution

- Milk or water: 2 mL/kg PO, maximum 70 mL, in conscious patients
- Used for: petroleum distillates, caustic agents, insoluble calcium oxalate plants
- Calcium in milk additionally reduces absorption of fluoride, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, bisphosphonates

---

### Activated Charcoal

- **Initial dose**: given with a cathartic (sorbitol) to facilitate movement through GI tract
- **Subsequent doses** (for enterohepatic recirculation): half the initial amount, WITHOUT sorbitol or cathartic
- **Does NOT bind**: heavy metals, corrosive agents, hydrocarbons, fluoride, xylitol, ethanol, petroleum distillates
- **Hypernatremia risk**: small patients, dehydrated patients, or those that ingested osmotically active substances (chocolate, paintballs, soft-chew supplements); monitor serum sodium
- Recent research indicates multidose AC may not be more effective than single dose for some enterohepatic-recirculating toxicants

---

### Cholestyramine

- Dose: 0.3–1 g/kg PO q8h (duration depends on toxin)
- Used for: cholecalciferol, sago palm (Cycas revoluta), blue-green algae, some NSAIDs

---

### Enemas

- Warm water enemas: 5–10 mL/kg — for elimination of toxicants from lower GI tract

---

### Intravenous Lipid Emulsion (ILE)

- **Solution**: 20% lipid solution
- **Dose**: initial bolus 1.5 mL/kg IV, followed by 0.25 mL/kg/min IV for 30–60 minutes
- Can be repeated q4–6h if still showing clinical signs and no longer lipemic
- **Used for**: bupivacaine/local anesthetics, moxidectin, ivermectin, calcium channel blockers, baclofen, permethrin, THC, ibuprofen
- **Side effects**: persistent gross lipemia, hypertriglyceridemia, volume overload, corneal lipidosis
- Adjust dosing in small patients to avoid volume overload

---

### Therapeutic Plasma Exchange (TPE)

- Removes toxicants with high protein binding and low volume of distribution
- Suggested for: ibuprofen, meloxicam, naproxen NSAID exposures
- Side effects: hypotension, bleeding, clot formation, sepsis, allergic reaction

---

### Hemodialysis / Charcoal Hemoperfusion

- Hemodialysis: for low molecular weight, low protein binding, small volume of distribution toxicants
  - Used for: ethylene glycol, ethanol
- Charcoal hemoperfusion: for higher molecular weight, higher protein binding, higher volume of distribution
  - Used for: theophylline, barbiturate toxicosis
- Combination used for: baclofen, metaldehyde, THC, enrofloxacin

## Ch 133 — Neurotoxicoses

### Key Neurotoxicants (Table 133.1)

#### Methylxanthines / Chocolate (Ch 133)
- **Toxic thresholds (total methylxanthines)**:
  - GI upset (dogs and cats): 20 mg/kg
  - Agitation, tachycardia: >40 mg/kg
  - Tremors, seizures: >60 mg/kg
- **Methylxanthine content (mg/g)**:
  - White chocolate: 0.04
  - Milk chocolate: 2.26
  - Semisweet (dark) chocolate: 5.65
  - Unsweetened chocolate: 15.52
  - Cocoa powder: 28.47
- Signs begin within minutes (caffeine-only) to 6–12 hours (chocolate)
- **Treatment**: emesis if not showing signs; IV fluids; beta-blockers for cardiac tachyarrhythmias; seizures respond to barbiturates (phenobarbital); activated charcoal rarely given due to hypernatremia risk from fluid shifts

> ClinIQ: ✓ CHOC

#### Bromethalin (Ch 133)
- **Mechanism**: uncouples oxidative phosphorylation in neuronal/glial mitochondria → fluid accumulation within myelin
- **Low dose**: ataxia, CNS depression, paresis, paralysis (signs may not occur until up to 5 days after ingestion)
- **High dose**: hyperesthesia, agitation, tremors, seizures, severe CNS depression, death (signs within 12 hours)
- **Treatment**: decontamination is most important; pets showing clinical signs often don't respond well to treatment

> ClinIQ: ✓ BROM

#### Cannabis / THC (Ch 133)
- Mechanism: delta-9-THC activates cannabinoid receptors in CNS and PNS
- Signs: lethargy, ataxia, urinary incontinence, hyperesthesia, bradycardia, hypotension
- **Treatment**: Supportive; ILE (ch. 132)
- Onset may be rapid or delayed a few hours

> ClinIQ: ✓ CANNABIS

#### Organophosphates / Carbamates (Ch 133)
- Mechanism: inhibits acetylcholinesterase
- Signs: tremors, seizures, bradycardia, tachypnea, increased lung sounds, hypersalivation, GI upset
- **Atropine dose (from Table 133.1)**: 0.1–0.2 mg/kg, ¼ IV, ¾ SQ or IM
- **Atropine dose (from treatment section)**: 0.02–0.2 mg/kg; ¼ IV, ¾ SQ or IM
- Benzodiazepines for seizures

> ClinIQ: ✓ OP (dose note: table says 0.1–0.2 mg/kg vs. Ch 132 table 0.2–0.3 mg/kg — Ch 133 treatment text gives 0.02–0.2 mg/kg range)

#### Pyrethrins / Pyrethroids / Permethrin (Ch 133)
- Mechanism: prolongs sodium influx, reduces potassium efflux → repetitive nerve discharge
- **Cats are more sensitive** (concentration determines toxicosis)
- Signs: tremors, dermal paresthesia, agitation, seizures, GI upset with oral exposure
- **Treatment**: bathe if dermal; methocarbamol for tremors (55–220 mg/kg slow IV PRN); ILE (ch. 132)

> ClinIQ: ✓ PERM

#### Metaldehyde (Ch 133)
- Mechanism: decreases serotonin, norepinephrine, monoamine oxidase and GABA
- Signs: rapid onset of tremors, seizures, nonfebrile hyperthermia, tachycardia
- **Treatment**: methocarbamol for tremors; hemodialysis (ch. 103)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — METAL protocol exists (metaldehyde has dedicated ClinIQ entry per prompt), but verify METAL tag is metaldehyde. If METAL = metaldehyde, ✓ METAL.

#### Tremorgenic Mycotoxins (Penitrem A, Roquefortine A) (Ch 133)
- Mechanism: inhibits GABA-A, increases aspartate and glutamate, inhibits inhibitory interneurons
- Signs: tremors, ataxia, seizures, GI upset, hyperesthesia, hyperthermia
- **Treatment**: methocarbamol for tremors; ILE (ch. 132)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — tremorgenic mycotoxins are a distinct entity from other neurotox)

#### Lead (Ch 133)
- Mechanism: competes with zinc ions disrupting GABA; alters synaptic transmission at smooth muscle neuromuscular junctions
- **Acute signs**: GI upset, ataxia, tremors, agitation, seizures
- **Chronic signs**: GI upset, lethargy, behavior changes, intermittent seizures, anemia, megaesophagus
- Lab: blood lead level; microcytic hypochromic anemia, basophilic stippling, nucleated RBCs (ch. 138)
- **Chelation options** (approximate descending preference — cost, availability, ease):
  - Succimer (DMSA): 10 mg/kg PO q8h for 10 days
  - Edetate calcium disodium (CaEDTA): 25 mg/kg SQ q6h for 5 days OR 75 mg/kg IV q6h for 2–5 days, diluted to 10 mg/mL
  - d-Penicillamine: 30–110 mg/kg/day PO divided q6h for 7 days
  - Dimercaprol (BAL): 3–6 mg/kg IM q6–8h

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — lead chelation protocol absent)

#### Macrolide Lactones — Ivermectin, Moxidectin, etc. (Ch 133)
- P-glycoprotein (ABCB1/MDR-1 gene mutation) limits CNS entry; mutant animals at high risk
- Signs: CNS depression, ataxia, weakness, blindness, respiratory depression, bradycardia, hypothermia, coma
- **Treatment**: Supportive care; ILE (ch. 132)

#### Baclofen (Ch 133)
- Signs: CNS depression, ataxia, respiratory depression/arrest due to muscle paralysis, arrhythmias, hypotension, vocalization, seizures
- **Treatment**: Ventilatory support; ILE (ch. 132); hemodialysis

#### Serotonin Syndrome (Ch 133) — multiple serotonergic agents
- **Cyproheptadine**: Dog: 1.1 mg/kg PO or rectally q6–8h; Cat: 2–4 mg/cat PO or rectally q6–8h
- **Propranolol**: 0.02–0.06 mg/kg IV OR 0.1–0.2 mg/kg PO q8h (also antagonizes serotonin activity)

#### Seizure Management — Neurotoxic (Ch 133)
- Diazepam: 0.5–1 mg/kg IV PRN
- Levetiracetam: 30–60 mg/kg IV q8h (preferred when ILE given)
- Midazolam: 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IV or IM PRN
- Phenobarbital: 4–8 mg/kg IV slowly over 5 min; repeat 4 mg/kg IV q20min up to total 20–24 mg/kg IV
- Inhalant anesthesia for refractory cases

#### Acepromazine — agitation (Ch 133)
- 0.05–0.1 mg/kg IV initially; can repeat PRN after 15 minutes to take effect

## Ch 134 — Hepatotoxicoses

### Acetaminophen / APAP / Paracetamol (Ch 134)

**Toxic doses**:
- Dogs: ≥75–100 mg/kg (hepatotoxicity)
- Cats: ≥10 mg/kg

**Mechanism**: NAPQI (N-acetyl-para-benzoquinone imine) — generated by CYP450 when glucuronidation/sulfation pathways saturated; depletes glutathione; cats have reduced n-acetylation capacity + PAP causes RBC oxidative damage

**Timeline**:
- Methemoglobinemia: onset 2–4 hours
- Liver injury: up to 24–48 hours
- Facial/paw edema more common in cats

**Treatment**:
- Oxygen if dyspneic
- IV fluids
- GI decontamination based on time/condition
- **N-acetylcysteine**: 140 mg/kg PO or IV loading dose, then 70 mg/kg q6h for 6 doses
- **S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe)**: 20 mg/kg PO q24h or divided q8–12h
- **Ascorbic acid**: 30 mg/kg PO, SQ, or IV q6h (reduces MetHgb to Hgb; unknown efficacy for liver protection)
- Monitor for KCS within 24–96 hours; facial/paw edema resolves over 72 hours without specific treatment

> ClinIQ: ✓ PARA (DIS-TOX-APAP)

---

### Aflatoxins (Ch 134)

- **Produced by**: Aspergillus spp.; aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) most common (corn, grains, peanuts, soybeans)
- **Acute oral LD50**:
  - Dogs: 0.5–1 parts per million (ppm)
  - Cats: 0.3–0.8 ppm
- Clinical cases have occurred in dogs ingesting food contaminated with ≥60 ppb AFB1
- US maximum allowable concentration in pet food: 20 ppb
- Signs can develop within 1–2 days (high-level) or weeks to months (chronic)
- **Treatment**: supportive (no antidote)
  - N-acetylcysteine: 70 mg/kg PO or IV q6–12h
  - SAMe: 20 mg/kg PO q24h or divided q8–12h
  - Silymarin: 50–250 mg/kg PO q12h
  - Vitamin K1: 5 mg/kg SC, then 0.8 mg/kg PO q8h or 1.25 mg/kg PO q12h (for coagulopathy)
  - Antiemetics, GI protectants, IV fluids

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — aflatoxin hepatotoxicosis)

---

### Cyanobacteria / Blue-Green Algae (Ch 134)

- Hepatotoxic cyanotoxins (HCs): microcystins and nodularins (most common in US)
- **Mechanism**: bind and disable cytoskeletal proteins, initiate lipid peroxidation, DNA fragmentation → hepatocyte necrosis/apoptosis; severe cases = intrahepatic hemorrhage → rapid death from hypovolemia
- Signs: acute collapse/death (no signs) to vomiting + apparent recovery ~12 hours → signs of liver injury; weakness, depression, icterus, dyspnea, oliguria/anuria, seizures, coagulopathy
- **Treatment**:
  - Do NOT induce emesis (rapid onset life-threatening signs)
  - Activated charcoal NOT beneficial for HCs
  - Cholestyramine: 300–1000 mg/kg PO q8h (reduces enterohepatic recycling)
  - Fluid/blood replacement, seizure management, dextrose for hypoglycemia
  - Hepatoprotectants: NAC, SAMe, silymarin

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — cyanobacteria/blue-green algae hepatotoxicosis)

---

### Cycads / Sago Palm (Ch 134)

- Cycas revoluta (sago palm), Cycas circinalis, Zamia spp.
- **All parts are toxic; ingestion of a single seed has been lethal to dogs**
- No published reports of sago palm toxicosis in cats
- Toxin: cycasin → methylazoxymethanol (MAM) in intestine; hepatotoxic, neurotoxic, mutagenic, teratogenic, carcinogenic
- Signs: vomiting (80–90% of dogs), diarrhea (~⅓), lethargy, ataxia, icterus, coagulopathy; liver failure signs 24–48h
- **Treatment**:
  - Emesis + activated charcoal (ch. 132)
  - Vitamin K1: 5 mg/kg SC, then 0.8 mg/kg PO q8h or 1.25 mg/kg PO q12h
  - Blood replacement, IV fluids, antiemetics, GI protectants, dextrose for hypoglycemia, hepatoprotectants
  - Cholestyramine (ch. 132 dose: 0.3–1 g/kg PO q8h)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — sago palm / cycad toxicosis)

---

### Hepatotoxic Mushrooms — Amatoxins (Ch 134)

- Genera: Amanita (incl. A. phalloides — death cap), Conocybe, Galerina, Lepiota, Pholiotina
- **One mushroom can be lethal**
- Toxin: cyclopeptide amanitin — inhibits RNA polymerase → protein synthesis failure
- Clinical timeline: initial GI signs 6–24 hours post-ingestion → apparent recovery → liver failure 72–96 hours later; fulminant necrosis can cause death <24 hours with massive ingestion
- **Treatment**:
  - Emesis + activated charcoal (ch. 132)
  - Vitamin K1: 5 mg/kg SC, then 0.8 mg/kg PO q8h or 1.25 mg/kg PO q12h
  - Blood replacement, IV fluids, antiemetics, GI protectants, dextrose for hypoglycemia, hepatoprotectants
  - Biliary drainage + octreotide (reduces enterohepatic recirculation of amanitin) — no controlled studies

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — amatoxin / hepatotoxic mushroom protocol)

---

### Xylitol (Ch 134)

- **Mechanism**: dogs have unique physiologic response — rapid, sustained, dose-dependent insulin release → precipitous hypoglycemia; some develop liver injury (ATP depletion ± reactive oxygen species)
- Activated charcoal does NOT appreciably adsorb xylitol
- **Hypoglycemia onset**: within 30 minutes; gum-based products may delay to 12 hours
- **Liver enzyme changes**: 4–24 hours; not all dogs with enzyme elevation progress to hepatic failure
- **Treatment**:
  - Decontaminate if patient not showing signs; baseline blood glucose BEFORE decontamination
  - IV dextrose bolus then CRI for hypoglycemia
  - Frequent small meals once stable
  - Hepatoprotectants for liver injury concern; vitamin K1 for coagulopathy; plasma transfusion
  - Note: xylitol appears to have less severe effects in cats (reference 28)

> ClinIQ: ✓ XYLITOL

## Ch 135 — Renal Toxicoses

### Ethylene Glycol (Ch 135)

**Minimum Lethal Dose (MLD)**:
- Cats: 1 g/kg
- Dogs: 6.7–7.3 g/kg

**Serum EG level of concern**:
- Cats: 20 mg/dL
- Dogs: 50 mg/dL

**Treatment must begin**:
- Dogs: within 8 hours of exposure
- Cats: within 3 hours of exposure

**Diagnosis**:
- Cage-side EG tests (good sensitivity, lacking specificity; false positives with alcohols, propylene glycol, glycerol, sorbitol)
- Gold standard: gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy quantitative analysis
- Osmolal gap >20 mOsm/kg within 1–2 hours = indicative of exogenous solute
- Osmolal gap formula: measured serum osmolality − calculated osmolality. Calculated = 1.86 × (Na+ + K+ + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8) [if Na/K in mEq/L, glucose/BUN in mg/dL]; metric formula (all mmol/L): 1.86 × (Na+ + K+ + glucose + BUN)
- Progressive anion gap increase or pH/bicarbonate decrease = suspect significant EG exposure
- Very high blood lactate levels with intoxication signs = suspect EG toxicosis

**Lab markers**: calcium oxalate crystals in urine, high anion gap, azotemia, metabolic acidosis

**Fomepizole (4-methylpyrazole) Protocol**:
- **Dogs**: 20 mg/kg slow IV over 15–30 minutes, THEN 15 mg/kg slow IV at 12 h and 24 h later, THEN 5 mg/kg slow IV 36 h after initial dose
- **Cats**: 125 mg/kg slow IV over 15–30 min, THEN 31.25 mg/kg slow IV at 12 h, 24 h, and 36 h after initial dose
- Continue until ≥36 hours post-exposure OR until EG test is negative
- NOTE: fomepizole is also dialyzable — increase dosage if hemodialysis used concurrently

**Ethanol Protocol** (dogs and cats):
- Using 7% solution: load at 600 mg/kg (8.6 mL/kg) slow IV over 30–60 min, THEN maintain at 100 mg/kg/h (1.4 mL/kg/h) IV CRI
- Continue for ≥24 hours beyond point where renal and acid-base values are normal
- To make 7% ethanol from 80 proof (40% ethanol) vodka: remove 175 mL from 1 L isotonic crystalloid bag and replace with vodka

**Adjunctive treatment**:
- IV isotonic crystalloid fluids sufficient to promote diuresis, correct dehydration, compensate for losses
- Antiemetics for vomiting + CNS depression patients
- IV calcium gluconate for clinical hypocalcemia (until signs abate)
- Sodium bicarbonate for severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.2 or HCO3 <12 mEq/L)
- Seizures: diazepam
- Pyridoxine (vitamin B6): 1–2 mg/kg IV q6h (promotes nontoxic glyoxylic acid metabolism)
- Thiamine (vitamin B1): 50 mg slow IV over 5 min q6h
- Hemodialysis very effective at removing EG and metabolites

> ClinIQ: ✓ EG

### Cholecalciferol / Vitamin D (Ch 135)

- **Level of concern** (dogs and cats): 0.1 mg/kg (note: 1 IU = 0.000025 mg)
- Sources: rodenticides, human medications
- Mechanism: hypercalcemia from enhanced GI Ca++ absorption, osteoclast mobilization, enhanced renal Ca++ resorption → AKI within 4 days
- **Decontamination**: emesis + single dose activated charcoal, then cholestyramine resin

**Monitoring**: baseline serum total Ca++, P, BUN, creatinine; recheck daily until 96 hours post-exposure; ionized Ca++ can substitute (keep analyzer and reference interval consistent)

**Hypercalcemia Management Table (Table 135.2)**:
| Treatment | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0.9% NaCl | 100–180 mL/kg/d initially, then adjusted to maintain hydration | Avoid Ca-containing fluids (e.g., LRS) |
| Low Ca diet | N/A | Macaroni + lean ground beef, many renal/urinary tract diets |
| Pamidronate | 1.3–2 mg/kg IV slowly in 150 mL saline over 2 hours | Peak effects 72 hours; can cause AKI if infused too fast/slow |
| Zoledronate | 0.25 mg/kg in 50–100 mL D5W IV over 15 min | Peak effects 10 days |
| Prednisone/prednisolone | 0.5–1.5 mg/kg PO q12h | Increases fluid supplementation needs |
| Furosemide | 2.5–4.5 mg/kg PO q6–8h OR 5 mg/kg IV bolus then 0.5 mg/kg/h CRI | Promotes calciuresis; increases fluid needs |
| Aluminum hydroxide | 15–45 mg/kg PO q12h | Reduces intestinal phosphorus absorption |

Post-hypercalcemia: wean off saline diuresis; monitor serum Ca++ daily ≥5–7 days, then 2–3×/week for 2–3 weeks. Treatment may be required for weeks (long half-life of calcidiol). Prognosis: good if treated promptly; guarded once hypercalcemia develops; grave once soft-tissue mineralization develops.

> ClinIQ: ✓ VITD (DIS-TOX-CHOLE)

### Lilium / Hemerocallis spp. (True Lilies) (Ch 135)

- **CATS ONLY** — any exposure to any part of any Lilium or Hemerocallis plant (including pollen) warrants treatment
- One case report of suspected toxicity in a meerkat; dogs not known to be affected
- Specific toxin unknown; water-soluble component of Lilium longiflorum
- Mechanism: necrosis of proximal renal tubular epithelium; also degeneration of pancreatic acinar cells; tubular basement membrane often remains intact (recovery possible)
- Signs: vomiting, AKI, anuria, death
- **Treatment**:
  - Emesis + AC if large amounts ingested; bathe if dermal exposure
  - Mainstay: IV fluid diuresis ≥48 hours
  - Start within 18 hours → good prognosis
  - Continue ≥48 hours to prevent anuric renal failure
  - Hemodialysis for severe cases
- Prognosis: poor prognosis signs = ataxia, oliguria/anuria, weakness, elevated Ca×P product, hyperkalemia

> ClinIQ: ✓ LILY

**NSAID Treatment**:

- IV isotonic crystalloid fluids at 2× daily maintenance ≥48 hours
- Proton pump inhibitor: omeprazole 0.85 mg/kg PO or pantoprazole 1 mg/kg IV q12h (continue 10–14 days)
- Sucralfate and misoprostol may also benefit; misoprostol 3–5 days
- ILE (20% lipid) described for ibuprofen, naproxen, carprofen toxicosis
- TPE used for ibuprofen, naproxen, meloxicam, carprofen toxicosis

### Vitis spp. (Grapes and Raisins) (Ch 135)

- **Dogs only** — uniquely sensitive; cats and other species not reported
- Toxic component: suspected tartaric acid (high levels in some grapes/raisins)
- Prevalence of AKI after exposure: 6.7% in retrospective study (139 dogs)
- **No established individual toxic dose; any amount ingested should be of concern**
- **Most common/initial sign**: spontaneous vomiting
- Any dog developing vomiting within 48 hours of grape/raisin exposure = at risk for AKI → treat intensively
- Clinicopathologic: azotemia, hypercalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, elevated ALT, hyperglycemia, hyper/hypokalemia, isosthenuria, hyposthenuria, proteinuria, glucosuria, pyuria, crystalluria, cylindruria
- **Treatment**:
  - Emesis if no contraindications; rewarding even up to 12 hours post-exposure
  - AC only if large exposure and poor emesis results
  - Baseline renal chemistry + urinalysis
  - If decontamination highly successful and AKI risk low: monitor at home; do NOT give antiemetic (vomiting is earliest AKI indicator); recheck renal chemistry + UA at 24–48 hours
  - If incomplete decontamination: admit for 48h IV isotonic crystalloid fluid diuresis
  - Prognosis: most patients with prompt fluid diuresis do not develop AKI; prognosis worsens with ataxia, oliguria, weakness, elevated Ca×P, hyperkalemia

> ClinIQ: ✓ GRAPE

## Ch 136 — Gastrointestinal Toxicoses

### Acids and Alkalis (Ch 136)

- Acids (pH <2): coagulative necrosis; up to 30% of patients with esophageal ulcers have no oral lesions
- Alkalis (pH >12): liquefactive necrosis; more esophageal lesions than acids; damaged nerve endings = prolonged contact
- Signs: ptyalism, vomiting, dysphagia, abdominal pain, oropharyngeal edema/erosions; alkalis may cause nonfebrile hyperthermia; signs 1–8 hours; ulcers resolve 7–14 days
- **Treatment**: do NOT induce emesis; AC ineffective; dilution with milk or water 0.5 mL/kg PO if given early
- Sucralfate slurries: 0.25–1 g PO q6–8h
- Omeprazole: 1 mg/kg PO q12h
- Both continued 10–14 days

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — caustic ingestion protocol)

---

### Insoluble Calcium Oxalate Plants (IO) (Ch 136)

Common genera: Dieffenbachia, Philodendron, Alocasia, Aglaonema, Spathiphyllum (peace lily), Epipremnum, Monstera, Anthurium, Calla, Arisaema, Caladium, Syngonium, Xanthosoma, Zantedeschia, Schefflera (umbrella tree)

- Mechanism: raphides (calcium oxalate crystal needles) propelled into oral mucosa; proteolytic enzymes trigger kinin/histamine release
- Signs: head shaking, ptyalism, oral irritation (nearly immediate to 2–3 hours); GI signs possible; airway obstruction rare
- Calcium oxalate crystals do NOT reach kidneys — no systemic/renal signs expected
- **Treatment**: do NOT induce emesis; do NOT give AC; dilute with dairy product (milk, yogurt, cottage cheese); rinse mouth; sucralfate slurries 0.25–1 g PO if needed; antiemetic + IV fluids for severe vomiting

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — IO plant toxicosis)

---

### Ornamental Bulbs (Ch 136)

Toxic genera: Amaryllis, Narcissus (daffodil), Iris, Cyclamen, Hyacinthus, Tulipa, Clivia, Gladiola, Hippeastrum, Colchicum autumnale (autumn crocus — also causes bone marrow suppression), Galanthus, Crocus

- Most common: vomiting, diarrhea; bulb ingestion worse than foliage
- Narcissus can cause seizures and death
- Iris and Gladiola: GI ulceration
- Cyclamen: bloody diarrhea (saponins)
- Colchicum (autumn crocus): bone marrow suppression — **see Table 136.1, treatment: ILE**
- Treatment: supportive; monitor BP and treat hypovolemia/hypotension with fluids/blood products if bulbs ingested

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — ornamental bulb toxicosis; Colchicine/Colchicum bone marrow suppression is partially covered under NSAID)

## Ch 137 — Cardiorespiratory Toxicoses

### Albuterol (Ch 137)

- Sources: tablets, nebulization solutions, inhalers (dogs biting inhalers most common)
- Mechanism: beta-2 agonist → overdose also stimulates beta-1 → tachycardia, potential myocardial necrosis
- Signs: initial agitation then depression; sinus tachycardia 200–300 bpm; tachypnea, weakness, hypokalemia; death
- Longer sinus tachycardia → more pathological arrhythmias → higher risk of myocardial necrosis
- **Treatment**:
  - Emesis + AC only if done quickly after tablet ingestion
  - IV fluids; monitor heart rate and rhythm for 12–24 hours
  - If tachycardic: propranolol 0.02–0.06 mg/kg IV (titrate to <200 bpm); also normalizes serum K+
  - If serum K+ still <2.5 mEq/L after beta blocker: supplement potassium
  - Signs usually resolve by 12 hours, can persist up to 48 hours

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — albuterol/beta-agonist toxicosis protocol)

---

### Calcium Channel Blockers (CCB) (Ch 137)

- Nondihydropyridine (diltiazem, verapamil): bradycardia via AV node; narrow margin of safety
- Dihydropyridine (amlodipine, nifedipine): systemic hypotension via smooth muscle; cross-over effects possible
- Additional: hyperglycemia, hyperkalemia, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia possible
- **Treatment**:
  - Standard decontamination: emesis + activated charcoal
  - Fluid replacement
  - Calcium (calcium chloride or calcium gluconate)
  - ILE if no cardiovascular change
  - Atropine and isoproterenol for bradyarrhythmias
  - Norepinephrine, phenylephrine, dopamine, dobutamine for hypotension
  - Insulin/dextrose: improved survival in canine model (improves cardiac inotropy)
  - Note: noncardiogenic pulmonary edema possible with massive overdose

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — CCB toxicosis protocol)

---

### Cardiac Glycosides (Ch 137)

Sources: digoxin (pharmaceutical), plants (foxglove/Digitalis purpurea, lily-of-the-valley/Convallaria majalis, oleander/Nerium, kalanchoe/Kalanchoe spp., milkweed/Asclepias spp.), toads, fireflies

- Mechanism: inhibit Na+/K+ ATPase → increased intracellular Na+ → exchanged with Ca++ → increased contractility; also increases vagal tone
- Digoxin half-life: dog 14.4–56 h; cat 23.8–42.8 h
- Signs: arrhythmias (all types), AV block 1°/2°/3°, premature complexes, atrial/ventricular tachycardia, ST changes; extracardiac: vomiting, lethargy, inappetence
- **Treatment**:
  - Emesis if not showing clinical signs
  - Activated charcoal binds cardiac glycosides (decreases digoxin absorption by 96%)
  - Cholestyramine and ILE (ch. 132) also shown beneficial
  - Monitor for arrhythmias; non-calcium containing fluid preferred
  - Monitor and manage potassium; insulin/dextrose if hyperkalemia
  - **Digoxin-specific immune Fab** (Digibind) — human product used off-label; directly inactivates digoxin; some cross-reactivity with plant-based cardiac glycosides; cost-prohibitive

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — cardiac glycoside/digoxin toxicosis)

---

### Toad Toxicosis (Rhinella marina / Incilius alvarius) (Ch 137)

- US distribution: R. marina (Cane toad) — Florida, Texas, Hawaii; I. alvarius (Colorado River toad) — southern Arizona, SE California, SW New Mexico
- Toxins: parotid gland secretions; cardiac glycoside-like (inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase); bufogenins may have sodium channel blocking; secondary toxins affect serotonin/catecholamines
- Signs begin almost immediately: hypersalivation, vomiting, weakness, lethargy, vocalization, arrhythmias (brady- and tachyarrhythmias), tachypnea, ataxia, tremors, collapse, seizures, death
- Severity related to secretion volume and patient size; signs may last 12–36 hours
- **Decontamination**: rinse mouth from back to front with tap water (point hose/syringe rostrally to avoid swallowing); if toad swallowed: emesis or endoscopic/surgical removal if no clinical signs
- Treatment same as cardiac glycosides above; Digoxin immune Fab may provide antidotal treatment

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — toad toxicosis protocol)

---

### Grayanotoxins (Ch 137)

Plants: Kalmia spp. (mountain laurel), Pieris spp., Rhododendron spp. (azalea)
- Mechanism: bind sodium channels → prolonged depolarization → negative chronotropic action on SA node → arrhythmias, potentially sinus arrest
- Non-ruminants rarely eat enough for cardiac signs; vomiting is most common
- Signs can last 1–2 days
- **Treatment**: emesis + AC if not showing clinical signs; monitor heart rate/rhythm; antiarrhythmic treatment as needed

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — grayanotoxin/azalea/rhododendron toxicosis)

---

### Imidazoline Decongestants (Ch 137)

Drugs: oxymetazoline, xylometazoline, tetrahydrozoline, naphazoline
- Mechanism: alpha-adrenergic effects
- Signs within 30 min–4 hours: vomiting, weakness, lethargy, bradycardia, respiratory depression, coma
- Induction of emesis and AC usually not indicated (quick onset of signs)
- **Reversal**: atipamezole 50 mcg/kg (¼ IV, rest IM) or yohimbine 0.1 mg/kg IV; may need to repeat in 1–2 hours

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — imidazoline decongestant toxicosis)

---

### Paraquat (Ch 137)

- Mechanism: actively transported into pneumocytes; superoxide radical production → cell membrane/organelle damage → cell death; healed by fibrosis
- Signs: initial vomiting; renal + liver enzyme elevations 48–72 hours; pulmonary fibrosis ≥1 week; concentrated product causes severe signs; diluted product = only GI signs
- **No effective treatment once pulmonary fibrosis has occurred**
- Treatment: timely/intensive; emesis + AC can be given even to patients showing signs (GI absorption is poor)
- Paraquat levels peak in lungs 4–5 days after exposure
- Hemodialysis gives best prognosis
- **Oxygen INCREASES oxidative injury in lungs** — avoid or use cautiously
- Prognosis: guarded to poor

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — paraquat toxicosis)

---

### Phenylpropanolamine (PPA) (Ch 137)

- Threshold for signs: 1 mg/kg (within therapeutic range)
- Serious signs: above 20 mg/kg
- Initial: agitation, tachycardia; then BP increases (vasoconstriction) → lethargy, bradycardia, piloerection, tremors, seizures
- Signs within 30–90 min; may continue up to 48 hours
- **Treatment**: emesis if no clinical signs; AC if concerning dose; close BP monitoring; nitroprusside or other vasodilators for hypertension; **atropine CONTRAINDICATED** (worsens hypertension); IV fluids for excretion/renal protection/thermoregulation; cyproheptadine if serotonin syndrome develops

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — phenylpropanolamine toxicosis)

---

### Cyanide (Ch 137)

- Mechanism: inhibits oxidative phosphorylation → cellular hypoxia
- **Treatment**:
  - Hydroxocobalamin: 75–150 mg/kg IV over 7.5 min
  - OR sodium nitrite: 16 mg/kg IV of a 3% solution, followed by sodium thiosulfate: 150–500 mg/kg IV bolus (dogs)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — cyanide toxicosis)

---

### Sulfuryl Fluoride (Ch 137)

- Fumigant gas (termite treatment); colorless/odorless (combined with chloropicrin lacrimator); most exposures from animals returning to tented homes
- Mechanism: inhaled free fluoride binds calcium → hypocalcemia
- Signs: hypocalcemia, tetany, seizures, arrhythmias, death; chloropicrin causes respiratory irritation
- **Treatment**: ventilation, oxygenation, correct hypocalcemia + other electrolytes; lidocaine for ventricular dysrhythmias; fluids judiciously (risk of pulmonary edema); prognosis poor

---

### Tricyclic Antidepressants (Ch 137)

- Mechanism: sodium channel blockade → arrhythmias; also serotonin syndrome
- **Treatment**: titrate pH to 7.55 with IV sodium bicarbonate (see also ch. 133 for serotonin syndrome management)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — TCA toxicosis; serotonin management partially covered)

---

### Yew (Taxus spp.) (Ch 137)

- Mechanism: taxine alkaloids inhibit normal Na/Ca exchange across myocardial cells → depresses cardiac depolarization → arrhythmias
- All parts toxic EXCEPT flesh of red berry; sudden death not uncommon within hours
- Signs: bradycardia, dyspnea, weakness, ataxia, collapse, hypothermia, ventricular arrhythmias
- **Treatment**: activated charcoal binds taxine alkaloids but stress of administration could worsen signs; supportive care; patients surviving 12 hours likely to survive

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — yew/Taxus toxicosis)

---

## Ch 138 — Hematologic Toxicoses

### Anticoagulant Rodenticides (Ch 138)

- Mechanism: block recycling of vitamin K (inhibit vitamin K 1,2,3-epoxide reductase) → depletes clotting factors II, VII, IX, X
- **Bleeding onset**: 3–7 days after ingestion
- **PT prolongation**: factor VII has shortest half-life → PT prolonged first, within 48–72 hours of exposure
- Sites of hemorrhage: thorax (most common), abdomen, joints (esp. large-breed dogs), subcutis, CNS
- **Decontamination threshold**: ingestions of >0.02 mg/kg — decontaminate and possibly treat
- **Warfarin**: <0.5 mg/kg unlikely to cause bleeding
- Decontamination: within 4 hours of ingestion; emesis standard; risks of AC (aspiration, hypernatremia) may outweigh benefits → relative safety of Vitamin K1 treatment preferred

**Vitamin K1 Dosing** (verbatim):
- 1.5–2.5 mg/kg q12h administered PO with a small fatty meal
- In small patients (puppies, kittens): injectable form can be administered orally
- **Duration by agent**:
  - Warfarin (Coumadin): 14 days
  - Bromadiolone: 21 days
  - All other ARs (brodifacoum, chlorophacinone, difethialone, diphacinone, pindone): 30 days
- Check PT 60–72 hours after last dose to ensure continued treatment not needed (also from Table 132.1)
- If actively bleeding or very prolonged PT: give plasma (fresh or fresh frozen) for immediately available clotting factors PLUS start vitamin K1
- Severe anemia: whole blood transfusion
- Severe dyspnea: thoracentesis; autotransfusion provides oxygen-carrying capacity but NOT coagulation factors

> ClinIQ: ✓ ACRM

---

### Factor Xa Antagonists (Apixaban/Eliquis, Rivaroxaban/Xarelto) (Ch 138)

- LD50 apixaban in dogs: >1500 mg/kg
- Spontaneous bleeding not seen in dogs even at 27.55 mg/kg
- Duration: ~24 hours
- May demonstrate prolonged PT and PTT

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — novel oral anticoagulant toxicosis)

---

### Allium spp. (Onions, Garlic, Leeks, Chives) (Ch 138)

- Toxic in raw, cooked, or dried form
- **Toxic doses in dogs**:
  - Raw onions: 15–30 g/kg causes hemolysis
  - Garlic: approximately 3× more potent than onions
- **Cats**: approximately 3× more sensitive to Allium spp. than dogs
- **Lab markers**: Heinz body anemia, hemolysis, hematuria (AKI secondary to hemolysis)
- Changes in hematocrit may be seen in as little as 12 hours; typically 2–5 days; peak effects around 5–7 days

> ClinIQ: ✓ ALLIUM (DIS-TOX-ALLIUM)

---

### Zinc (Ch 138)

- Most common source: US pennies minted after 1982 (copper-clad zinc core); also galvanized metal, nuts, bolts, screws
- Zinc oxide (diaper rash ointment, sunscreen) rarely causes hemolysis (causes self-limiting emesis); large amounts or chronic ingestion can cause hemolysis
- Mechanism: oxidative damage to RBC membrane → hemolysis; also AKI secondary to hemolysis
- **Lab markers**: hemolysis, hemoglobinuria, anemia; blood zinc level (requires royal blue stopper collection tube — rubber stoppers contain zinc)
- **Treatment**:
  - Induce emesis; radiographs to locate metallic objects (absence doesn't rule out zinc — may have dissolved)
  - Activated charcoal NOT effective for heavy metals
  - If emesis unsuccessful: endoscopy or surgery to remove metallic zinc
  - Proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole 0.7–1 mg/kg PO q12h) preoperatively reduces zinc solubilization
  - If hemolysis begun: IV fluid diuresis; sodium bicarbonate 2–3 mEq/kg over several hours in IV fluids (minimizes hemoglobin precipitation in renal tubules); transfusions for severe anemia
  - Hemolysis may still occur up to 72 hours after zinc removal
  - Chelation rarely needed (zinc levels drop rapidly after removal)

> ClinIQ: ✓ PROT-TOX-ZINC (DIS-TOX-ZN)

---

### Acetaminophen — Hematologic Component (Ch 138)

- **Methemoglobinemia threshold**:
  - Cats: 10 mg/kg
  - Dogs: 200 mg/kg
- PAP (para-aminophenol) metabolite → oxidizes hemoglobin to MetHgb
- MetHgb increases within 2–4 hours; Heinz body formation follows
- N-acetylcysteine decreases half-life of methemoglobin in cats from 10 to 5 hours
- **Treatment** (hematologic focus):
  - **N-acetylcysteine**: 140 mg/kg PO or IV loading dose, then 70 mg/kg PO q6h for 5–7 additional doses
  - **Ascorbic acid**: 20–30 mg/kg PO, IM, or IV q8h × 3 days (reduces MetHgb back to hemoglobin)
  - **Methylene blue**: 1–1.5 mg/kg slow IV (accelerates MetHgb reduction); **CONTROVERSIAL IN CATS** — can cause Heinz bodies and MetHgb in cats
  - Oxygen as needed

> ClinIQ: ✓ PARA (DIS-TOX-APAP)

---

### Methemoglobinemia — General (Ch 138)

Agents causing MetHgb: acetaminophen, local anesthetics (benzocaine, benzonatate, bupivacaine, dibucaine [most toxic], lidocaine, proparacaine), naphthalene mothballs, phenazopyridine (metabolized to acetaminophen, dogs >60 mg/kg), propylene glycol (chronic in cats — Heinz body anemia)

- Cats more sensitive to oxidative hemoglobin damage (8 free sulfhydryl groups on hemoglobin vs. 4 in dogs)
- Heinz bodies: translucent blebs at periphery of RBC on Giemsa stain; new methylene blue stain greatly enhances identification
- Blood appears dark brown; mucous membranes have "chocolate" or muddy cast

**General MetHgb treatment**:
- NAC loading 140 mg/kg then 70 mg/kg q6h × 5–7 doses
- Ascorbic acid 20–30 mg/kg PO, IM, IV q8h × 3 days
- Methylene blue 1–1.5 mg/kg slow IV (avoid in cats)
- Oxygen therapy

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — methemoglobinemia protocol — general; cat-specific lidocaine/local anesthetic MetHgb)

---

### Naphthalene Mothballs (Ch 138)

- 1–2 mothballs can cause methemoglobinemia and hemolysis (very toxic)
- Differentiation from paradichlorobenzene mothballs: naphthalene sinks in water but floats in saturated salt solution; paradichlorobenzene sinks in both
- Decontamination if recent ingestion (dissolves in stomach during first 1–2 hours): endoscopy or gastric lavage
- Treatment for toxicosis-induced hemolysis: as above for hemolysis/MetHgb

---

### Hemolysis — General Workup and Treatment (Ch 138)

- Serial hematocrits and blood smears for Heinz bodies for ~5 days after exposure (peak effects delayed)
- If hemolysis begun:
  - IV fluid diuresis to protect kidneys from hemoglobinuric nephropathy
  - Sodium bicarbonate 2–3 mEq/kg over several hours in IV fluids (minimize hemoglobin precipitation in renal tubules)
  - Transfusions (whole blood or packed RBCs) for severe anemia
- Owners: monitor for pale mucous membranes, weakness, hemoglobinuria

---

### Bone Marrow Suppression (Ch 138)

Agents: 5-fluorouracil, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, vinblastine, vincristine, chloramphenicol (dose-related, reversible), colchicine/Colchicum/Gloriosa (develops 1–2 weeks after exposure), estrogens (>1 mg/kg acute; signs up to 14 days; can be irreversible), methimazole (cats, chronic; bone marrow aplasia), phenylbutazone (may be irreversible)

- Treatment: decontamination in earliest stages; specific antidotes if available (leucovorin for methotrexate)
- Monitoring: CBC for 2 weeks or longer
- Filgrastim: 1–5 mcg/kg SQ daily if severe neutropenia
- RBC transfusions, darbepoetin (0.45 mcg/kg) or erythropoietin (100 U/kg SQ 3× weekly to start) for restarting marrow erythropoiesis

> ClinIQ: ✓ 5FU (partial — bone marrow component); ✗ MISSING — methotrexate, chloramphenicol, estrogen, phenylbutazone protocols
