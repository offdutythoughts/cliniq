### Ch 139 — Principles of Drug Disposition and Pharmacokinetics

### Key Pharmacokinetic Concepts (Ch 139)

**Bioavailability / Routes**
- Most oral delayed- or extended-release formulations designed for humans may not be effective or consistent in animals due to GI anatomy differences (may have immediate release or pass through without releasing drug).
- TD (transdermal) PLO compounded formulations: very few actually undergo transdermal absorption; most are ineffective. Exception: methimazole TD PLO in cats has some efficacy (variable vs. PO) — monitor T4.
- SC absorption can be decreased and erratic in dehydration, hypotension, hypothermia, obesity — prefer IM in those patients.

**Distribution — relevant tissue barriers**
- Blood-brain barrier (tight junctions + p-gp efflux) restricts poorly lipophilic drugs.
- Blood-prostate, blood-bronchus, blood-ocular barriers also limit drug penetration → need high-lipophilicity drugs for CNS/eye/prostate infections.
- Cefovecin: >95% protein-bound → persists for weeks after a single dose (low free-drug fraction available for tissue distribution).

**Species Metabolism Differences (critical for dosing)**
- **Cats deficient in UGT1A6 and UGT1A9** → decreased glucuronide conjugation → acetaminophen toxic even at subtherapeutic doses in cats (do NOT use acetaminophen in cats).
- Cats metabolize prednisone poorly (oral prednisolone AUC 4× higher than oral prednisone in cats → use prednisolone, not prednisone, in cats).
- Morphine: dogs → morphine-3-glucuronide (inactive); cats → morphine-3-sulfate (alternate pathway, clinically similar half-life).
- Tramadol: dogs → n-desmethyltramadol (inactive metabolite, minimal opioid effect); cats → O-desmethyltramadol (active, opioid effect).
- Dogs lack N-acetyltransferase → no NAPA formation from procainamide (class I antiarrhythmic effects only in dogs, not class III); increased sulfonamide hypersensitivity risk.

**Drug Interactions — CYP (Table 139.2)**

| Substrate | Inhibitor | Clinical Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Phenobarbital, pentobarbital, propofol, methadone | Chloramphenicol | Phenobarbital toxicosis; prolonged effects of propofol, pentobarbital, methadone |
| Theophylline | Enrofloxacin (and other fluoroquinolones) | Theophylline toxicosis |
| Cyclosporine | Ketoconazole | Increased cyclosporine effects or decreased cyclosporine dosage needed |
| Midazolam | Ketoconazole | Prolonged sedative effects of midazolam |
| Methadone | Fluconazole | Increased methadone oral bioavailability and duration of effects |

| Substrate | Inducer | Clinical Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Chloramphenicol, diazepam, theophylline, digoxin, levetiracetam, glucocorticoids | Phenobarbital | Decreased substrate efficacy |
| Phenobarbital | Phenobarbital (self-induction) | Decreasing plasma concentrations over time → decreased efficacy if dose not adjusted |
| Phenytoin | Phenytoin | Poor efficacy due to rapid elimination |

**Half-life clinical rules**
- Steady state reached after ~5 half-lives (97% of SS concentration).
- 5 half-lives also = time for ~97% elimination.
- Doubling dose extends duration by ~1 half-life (not double duration); doubles CMAX.
- Meloxicam t½ dogs ~24 h → monitor/hospitalize meloxicam toxicosis for 5–7 days.

**Drug clearance in renal disease**
- Renal elimination decreases near-proportionally with GFR decline.
- Amikacin elimination tracks GFR → monitor TDM before azotemia is apparent.
- Preferred strategy: choose hepatically eliminated drug (e.g., marbofloxacin over enrofloxacin in renal disease) or drug with higher therapeutic index (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate over enrofloxacin).

**Drug clearance in hepatic disease**
- Dosage adjustments not well characterized; no specific recommendations available.
- Low-bioavailability drugs at single PO doses may cause adverse effects in liver disease (less first-pass → higher systemic exposure).
- TDM available for some drugs: digoxin (adjust dose based on plasma concentrations).
- Compounded drugs: 13–25% of tested compounded drugs (Missouri State Board data 2006-2020) failed drug content testing; range of failures 3%–225% of stated content.

### PK-PD Decision Rules (Ch 140)

**Concentration-dependent antibiotics** (AUC:MIC ratio predicts efficacy):
- Fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides
- Administer as highest safe dose, **once daily** regardless of half-life.

**Time-dependent antibiotics** (T>MIC predicts efficacy):
- Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems), glycopeptides (vancomycin)
- Must maintain drug concentration above MIC for 40–50% of dosing interval.
- Critically ill / immunosuppressed patients may require >40–50% T>MIC → consider CRI.
- Short half-life drugs may require TID dosing (injectable cephalosporins, penicillins); or BID for highly susceptible bacteria with low MICs (cephalexin, amoxicillin-clavulanate).
- Longer half-life options: cefovecin, cefpodoxime → less frequent dosing.

**Bactericidal vs. bacteriostatic** — no longer considered clinically relevant.

**Tissue penetration**
- Pores/fenestrations in capillary endothelium are large enough for most drug molecules to pass from plasma to interstitial space. No "good tissue penetration" vs. "poor tissue penetration" categorization — avoid this error.
- Barriers requiring lipophilic drugs: CNS, eye, prostate, bronchial epithelium.
- Abscesses: poor blood supply limits drug access → surgical drainage is primary treatment; drug penetration by diffusion alone is limited.
- UTI (uncomplicated): high urine drug concentrations alone may suffice; **not** for complicated UTI, dilute urine (CKD), or involvement of deeper bladder layers/kidney/prostate.

**Note on specific doses:** Chapter 140 explicitly states dose regimens are "provided in other sources" (reference: Papich MG. *Papich Handbook of Veterinary Drugs*, 5th ed. Elsevier; 2021) and ISCAID guidelines (www.ISCAID.org). The chapter does not provide its own dose table.

### Ch 141 — Antifungal and Antiviral Therapy

### Antifungal Drugs — Drug Profiles and Doses

**Important note:** Chapter 141 explicitly states that doses for dermatophyte drugs (griseofulvin, azoles, terbinafine) are "provided in another reference" (Papich MG. *Papich Handbook of Veterinary Drugs*, 5th ed. Elsevier; 2021). Specific mg/kg doses for most systemic antifungals are not tabulated in this chapter.

#### Amphotericin B (Fungizone)
- Polyene macrolide — most active antifungal agent available.
- **Spectrum:** Blastomyces dermatitidis, Histoplasma, Cryptococcus, Coccidioides, Candida, Aspergillus, Leishmania.
- **Route:** IV only (cannot be given PO).
- **Key adverse effect — nephrotoxicity:** Early reversible nephrotoxicosis with each daily dose; **permanent nephrotoxicosis is related to total cumulative dose** — monitor renal function throughout; abandon if persistent azotemia.
- **Pre-medication:** IV fluid therapy before each dose; antihistamines, NSAIDs, antiemetics to manage acute reactions (vomiting, tremors, fever, anorexia).
- **Liposomal formulations (amphotericin B lipid complex):** Less nephrotoxic, allows higher cumulative doses; more expensive, less commonly used in veterinary patients.
- [Specific mg/kg doses — see Papich or disease-specific chapters]

#### Ketoconazole (Ch 141)
- Oldest systemic azole antifungal; **imidazole** group.
- **Spectrum:** Malassezia pachydermatis, systemic fungi, dermatophytes.
- **Advantages:** Low cost, familiarity.
- **Adverse effects:** Nausea, anorexia, vomiting (dose-related; decrease dose, divide doses, give with food); hepatotoxicosis (idiosyncratic, more likely at high doses — monitor ALT/ALP); inhibits cortisol and testosterone synthesis (exploited for hyperadrenocorticism management).
- **CYP interaction:** Potent CYP3A inhibitor → cyclosporine concentrations increased 2–3-fold when given concurrently. Also inhibits p-gp → affects CNS drug penetration and intestinal absorption of other drugs.
- [Specific mg/kg doses — see Papich or disease-specific chapters]

#### Itraconazole (Ch 141)
- Triazole; 5–100× more potent than ketoconazole.
- **Spectrum:** Systemic fungi, dermatophytes, Malassezia.
- **Formulations:** Capsules (Sporanox) and oral liquid solution (Itrafungol — veterinary formulation approved for cats, may be used in dogs).
- **CRITICAL: Compounded formulations are NOT bioequivalent** — bioequivalence studies in dogs and cats show compounded formulations are not sufficiently absorbed. Use only commercial formulations.
- **Dosing schedule (cats):** Long half-life allows once daily OR alternate-day dosing; commercial formulation (Itrafungol) can be administered on **alternate weeks** for dermatophyte treatment in cats.
- **Drug interactions:** Inhibits CYP450 and p-gp → may increase cyclosporine, digoxin, cisapride concentrations.
- **Adverse effects:** Anorexia, vomiting (especially at high doses); hepatopathy (less common than ketoconazole); monitor liver enzymes.
- **Food requirement:** Must be administered with food to optimize absorption (needs gastric acid and dietary fat for solubilization). Do NOT administer with antacids, H2-blockers, or PPIs (will decrease absorption).
- [Specific mg/kg doses — see Papich or disease-specific chapters]

#### Fluconazole (Ch 141)
- **More water-soluble** than ketoconazole/itraconazole → different PK; oral absorption less affected by food or antacids.
- **Spectrum:** Coccidioides spp. (good), Cryptococcus neoformans (good), dermatophytes and yeast infections (used in dogs); less active than itraconazole against Blastomyces and Histoplasma.
- **Drug interaction:** Inhibits methadone metabolism → increased oral methadone bioavailability and duration of effects (Table 139.2).
- [Specific mg/kg doses — see Papich or disease-specific chapters]

#### Voriconazole (Vfend)
- Triazole; important for disseminated aspergillosis in humans.
- **Cats:** Ocular and neurotoxicosis reported — use with caution.
- **Dogs:** Short half-life limits use.

#### Posaconazole (Noxafil)
- Newest azole; wider spectrum than itraconazole (adds activity against Mucorales: Mucor, Rhizopus).
- **Dogs:** Long half-life allows dosing **every 2–3 days**.
- **Cats:** Oral suspension produces effective concentrations.
- Adverse effects: Rare based on anecdotal accounts.
- [Specific mg/kg doses — see Papich or disease-specific chapters]

### Ch 142 — Antiparasitic Drugs

### Key Decision Rules (Ch 142)
- Always use labeled products at label doses when available.
- For concentration-dependent efficacy, underdosing risks resistance development.
- Monitor post-treatment efficacy with fecal exam at 10–14 days.
- **Multidrug-resistant hookworms** (Ancylostoma caninum, esp. greyhounds): treat with all three drug classes simultaneously.

### Multidrug-Resistant Hookworm Protocol (A. caninum; Ch 142)
- **Approach 1:** Oral pyrantel pamoate 5 mg/kg + febantel 25 mg/kg + praziquantel 5 mg/kg PO + topical moxidectin 2.5 mg/kg
- **Approach 2:** Oral fenbendazole 50 mg/kg daily × 3 days + oral pyrantel pamoate 5 mg/kg + topical moxidectin 2.5 mg/kg
- **If failure:** Emodepside 1 mg/kg PO (extra-label, with caution)
- Monitor efficacy with fecal egg count reduction test at 10–14 days post-treatment.

### Drug Resistance Notes (Ch 142)
- **ML (macrocyclic lactone) heartworm preventives:** Documented resistant Dirofilaria immitis isolates exist; compliance remains the most common cause of failure.
- **Dipylidium caninum:** Resistance to praziquantel and epsiprantel documented. Options: nitroscanate 50 mg/kg PO once; compounded pyrantel pamoate 5 mg/kg + praziquantel 5 mg/kg + oxantel pamoate 20 mg/kg PO once; nitazoxanide 100 mg/kg PO once.
- **Giardia:** Resistant strains in humans documented (5-nitroimidazoles, benzimidazoles); anecdotal resistance in veterinary species. Consider nitazoxanide or secnidazole as alternatives.

### NSAID Safety Rules (Ch 143)
- Adverse event rate: ~3% in dogs and cats.
- Most common adverse effects: GI, hepatic, renal.
- **Do NOT use in:** dehydrated, hypotensive, or renal/liver-compromised patients.
- **Do NOT combine:** two NSAIDs together, or NSAID + glucocorticoid concurrently → markedly increased GI adverse event risk.
- **Washout between NSAIDs:** 5–7 days standard; 10–14 days after aspirin (due to aspirin-triggered lipoxins and irreversible platelet inhibition).
- **Cats and NSAIDs:** Cats metabolize NSAIDs differently from dogs. Meloxicam and robenacoxib used for OA in cats; long-term safety unclear. Recent reviews suggest relatively safe in chronic renal disease when used carefully.
- **Renal monitoring:** In older dogs / CKD → evaluate renal and liver function at onset of therapy and monitor for adverse events. CKD → COX-2 overexpressed; NSAID-mediated inhibition negatively impacts renal function.

**Glucocorticoid potency (relative to cortisol = 1):**
- Dexamethasone: 25×
- Methylprednisolone: 5×
- Prednisone/prednisolone: 4×

**Biological half-lives:**
- Dexamethasone: 36–72 hours
- Methylprednisolone, prednisolone, prednisone: 12–36 hours

**Glucocorticoid anti-OA notes:**
- Evidence comparing glucocorticoids vs. NSAIDs for OA is lacking.
- GCs accelerate cartilage breakdown (unlike NSAIDs which do not appear to influence cartilage breakdown rate) → avoid for OA management.
- Long-term use leads to common systemic adverse effects.

### Anti-Nerve Growth Factor Monoclonal Antibody (Ch 143)
- **Frunevetmab** (Solensia): Felinized anti-NGF monoclonal antibody; FDA-approved for OA pain in cats (2022). Monthly SC injection.

### ⚠️ CRITICAL SAFETY WARNINGS (Ch 144)

**AZATHIOPRINE IN CATS — DO NOT USE**
- Cats have significantly lower erythrocyte TPMT activity than dogs or humans.
- AZA causes severe, potentially fatal myelosuppression in cats (progressive neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, or pancytopenia).
- **Azathioprine is CONTRAINDICATED in cats.**

### Glucocorticoids (Ch 144)

**Immunosuppressive use context:**
- Prednisone in dogs is efficiently converted to prednisolone by hepatic 11-betahydroxysteroid dehydrogenase → either prednisone or prednisolone can be used in dogs.
- **In cats:** Oral prednisolone AUC is 4× higher than oral prednisone → use **prednisolone** in cats (not prednisone).
- **Obese cats:** prednisolone AUC is 2× that of lean cats — **dose based on lean body weight** in obese cats.
- Cats are anecdotally less sensitive to GC effects than dogs (fewer, lower-affinity GC receptors in liver and skin).
- Dexamethasone: relative potency 25×; biological t½ 36–72 h; minimal mineralocorticoid activity.
- GC-induced diabetes in dogs: rare but reported.

### Azathioprine (Ch 144)

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| Initial dose (dogs) | 2 mg/kg q24h × 2–4 weeks |
| Maintenance (dogs) | Taper to 2 mg/kg q48h |
| Cats | **CONTRAINDICATED** |
| Onset of action | Lag time 2–6 weeks expected (mechanism-based; reduced proliferation of memory T-cells) |
| Adverse effects | Myelosuppression (8% dogs in first 2 months); hepatotoxicosis (15% dogs — subclinical ALT/ALP elevations, in first 2 months) |
| Duration of adverse effects | Generally reversible within 1–8 weeks of dose reduction or discontinuation; neutropenia may be persistent |
| Drug interaction | Allopurinol + AZA → increased TGN concentrations (xanthine oxidase inhibition) → reduce AZA dose significantly if combined |
| Monitoring | CBC (for myelosuppression), liver enzymes; baseline before starting, then q2 weeks for first 2 months |
| Persistence after discontinuation | Active metabolite depot in blood cells → effects may persist 3–6 weeks after discontinuation |

### Key Warnings Summary
1. **Azathioprine — FATAL IN CATS.** Never use in cats. Even a few days of treatment can cause fatal myelosuppression.
2. **Enrofloxacin in cats — max 5 mg/kg/day.** Higher doses cause retinal degeneration and blindness. (Referenced via drug interaction: enrofloxacin inhibits CYP → theophylline toxicosis; Chapter 139 Table 139.2.)
3. **Fluoroquinolones — avoid in young/growing animals.** Cartilage toxicity risk in juvenile patients.
4. **Amphotericin B — nephrotoxicity:** Monitor renal function before each dose; permanent toxicity related to cumulative dose; pre-treat with IV fluids; abandon if persistent azotemia.
5. **NSAID washout:** 5–7 days between NSAIDs; 10–14 days after aspirin. Never combine two NSAIDs or NSAID + GC.
6. **Meloxicam in cats — FDA boxed warning for long-term use.** Only approved as single SC injection in cats.
7. **Itraconazole compounded formulations — NOT bioequivalent.** Use commercial formulation only (Sporanox capsules or Itrafungol oral solution).
8. **Famciclovir compounded oral suspension — NOT effective** in cats.
9. **Prednisone vs. prednisolone in cats:** Use prednisolone; prednisone oral bioavailability is 4× lower in cats.
10. **Obese cats and prednisolone:** Dose based on lean body weight (AUC 2× higher in obese cats).
11. **Cyclosporine + ketoconazole/fluconazole:** 2–3× increase in cyclosporine concentration — intentionally used to allow 50–75% dose reduction.
12. **Allopurinol + azathioprine (dogs):** Xanthine oxidase inhibition → markedly increased TGN concentrations → reduce AZA dose significantly.
13. **Acetaminophen is TOXIC in cats** at any dose (deficiency of UGT1A6/1A9 glucuronidation).
