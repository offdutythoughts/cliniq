### Ch 169 — Immunologic and Hematologic Disease: Introduction and Drug Therapy
*(Archer TM)*

**Overview of immunosuppressive strategy**
- Goal: achieve disease remission → controlled taper → lowest effective dose.
- No combination of drugs has been shown more effective than sole glucocorticoid therapy in controlled RCTs; no second agent beyond GC has proven superior to another.
- Avoid three simultaneous immunosuppressants unless absolutely necessary — no evidence of superior efficacy, increased risk of secondary infection.

**Glucocorticoid therapy**
- Mainstay of initial immunosuppression: wide availability, low cost, rapid onset.
- IMHA dogs: if PCV drops ≥5% absolute in 24 h during first 7 days of sole GC therapy → add second agent (per ACVIM 2019 consensus).
- GC dose reductions: ≈25% if on monotherapy; may reduce 25–50% if a second drug is maintained.
- Taper: one drug at a time, ≈25% every 2–4 weeks; rechecks before each reduction.
- Total treatment duration for severe IM blood disorders: often 4–6 months or longer.

**Second-line immunosuppressants (dogs)**
- Cyclosporine A (CsA) — modified/ultramicronized preparation only (not Sandimmune); no myelosuppression; GI side-effects managed by freezing capsules, antiemetics, or administration with food; check liver enzymes every 2–3 months; response expected ≤1 week.
- Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) — not used with pre-existing GI disease; CBC every 2–3 weeks × 1 month, then every 2–3 months; response 1–2 weeks.
- Azathioprine (DOG ONLY — not safe in cats) — CBC + liver enzymes every 2 weeks × 2 months, then every 1–2 months; response 1–2 weeks; can cause mild poorly regenerative anemia (≠ myelosuppression).
- Leflunomide — CBC + liver enzymes every 2 weeks × 2 months, then every 1–2 months.
- Do not use azathioprine + mycophenolate together (same mechanism).

**Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of CsA**
- PK monitoring: consult lab (NCSU) for sample timing.
- PD monitoring: measures IL-2 production by T cells (MS University); enormous variation in immune suppression at same dose; a patient with low CsA dose but significant IL-2 suppression may develop secondary infections — unexpected case cited (3.1 mg/kg PO q12h).
- Indications for PD monitoring: 1) after week 1 to confirm suppression, 2) poor clinical response despite CsA, 3) dosage reductions (relapse concern), 4) unexpected secondary infection.

**Myelosuppression monitoring thresholds**
- ANC >1000/mcL, no clinical signs: antibiotics not required unless other risk factors.
- ANC <1000/mcL, no clinical signs: prophylactic antibiotics indicated.
- ANC <1000/mcL + clinical signs (fever): antibiotics + intensive monitoring for sepsis.

**Relapse management**
- On high-dose sole GC → add second agent.
- On full doses of two drugs → consider TDM.
- Mild relapse during taper → return to last effective dose.
- Severe/fulminant relapse → restart full induction doses; then double taper interval going forward.
- Refractory IMHA/ITP with continued relapses → splenectomy may be considered.

**Secondary infections**
- Monitor: lower urinary signs, skin changes, anorexia, general malaise.
- CsA + fungal infection → discontinue CsA first.
- Highest risk: three simultaneous IS drugs; patients with TDM-documented massive suppression.

### Ch 170 — Coagulation Testing
*(McMichael MA)*

**Preanalytical requirements**
- Correct anticoagulant: sodium citrate 3.2%, 1:9 ratio (not EDTA) for coagulation assays; K+EDTA for CBC/platelet count.
- Fill tubes immediately; invert 4–6× after filling; no heat/cooling; direct venipuncture into vacuum tube preferred.
- If central line: flush with heparinized saline → discard ≥3 mL before collection.
- Preanalytical errors account for up to 75% of lab errors in human labs.

**Key thresholds**
- Petechiation: platelet count <20,000/mcL (Ch 170 text; same value confirmed in Ch 175).
- Platelet estimate by smear: average platelet count per 5 oil-immersion fields × 15,000/mcL.
- ACT normal: dog 71 s (range 55–80 s); cat 66 s (range 55–85 s) using MAX-ACT tube (manual method).
- ACT prolonged by severe thrombocytopenia even with intact coagulation cascade.

**PT**
- Evaluates: extrinsic (FVII) + common pathway (FX, FV, prothrombin, fibrinogen).
- Prolonged PT + normal aPTT = FVII deficiency.
- Vitamin K deficiency: PT prolongs within 36–72 h (FVII shortest half-life 6.2 h); clinical bleeding awaits prothrombin deficiency.
- PT strongly correlated with rivaroxaban anti-Xa activity in dogs (better than aPTT or TEG).
- Anti-Xa activity still gold standard for DOAC monitoring.

**aPTT**
- Evaluates: contact (prekallikrein, FXII, FXI) + intrinsic (FVIII, FIX) + common pathways.
- Only factors NOT affecting aPTT: FVII and FXIII.
- Coagulation times prolonged when factor activity <30–50%.

**Factor half-lives** (critical for vitamin K deficiency timing)
- FII: 42 h; FVII: 6.2 h; FIX: 13.9 h; FX: 16.5 h.
- Clinical bleeding: typically 3–5 days after rodenticide ingestion.

**D-dimers**
- Specific for cross-linked fibrin breakdown (not fibrinogen or fibrin monomer).
- Half-life short → presence indicates recent fibrinolysis (~5 h).
- Elevated in: DIC, anticoagulant rodenticide intoxication, liver/renal disease, heart failure, neoplasia, internal hemorrhage, post-surgery, IMHA.
- Not all commercial human assays cross-react with animal D-dimers.

**Antithrombin (AT)**
- Reported as % of pooled normal species plasma (normal species-specific standard required).
- AT deficiency: decreased hepatic production, DIC consumption, renal/enteric loss.
- AT requires sulfated proteoglycans (endothelial heparan or pharmacological heparin) for inhibitory action.

**TEG/ROTEM (TE)**
- Hypercoagulable artifact from low hematocrit (anemia) — TE tracings appear hypercoagulable in IMHA, hemoabdomen, DIC.
- Erythrocytosis → hypocoagulable artifact.
- TE identifies hypercoagulability but results have NOT been predictive of thrombosis in dogs.
- Parameters: R = reaction time (start to 2 mm amplitude); K = clot kinetics (2–20 mm); α = alpha angle (slope 2–20 mm); MA = maximum amplitude.

**Diagnostic algorithm (bleeding)**
- Petechiae/mucosal bleeding → screen platelet count ± PT/aPTT.
- Platelet low + normal PT/aPTT → bone marrow, imaging, infectious screening.
- Platelet low + abnormal PT/aPTT → DIC, hemangiosarcoma, splenic/liver disease, factor deficiency, rodenticide.
- Platelet normal + ↑ PT + normal aPTT → rodenticide, early hepatic failure.
- Platelet normal + ↑ PT + ↑ aPTT → hepatic failure, DIC, rodenticide, factor screening.
- Platelet normal + normal PT/aPTT → PFA-100, vWF testing.

### Ch 171 — Hyper- and Hypocoagulable States
*(Blois SL)*

**Virchow's Triad in veterinary patients**
- Hypercoagulability: increased platelet/procoagulant activity, deficient anticoagulants, impaired fibrinolysis, vascular stasis, endothelial disruption/activation.

**Hypercoagulable diseases**

*IMHA*
- Most deaths occur shortly after diagnosis; up to 50% attributed to thromboembolism even with thromboprophylaxis.
- Mechanisms: increased platelet reactivity; increased TF on platelets/monocytes/endothelium; phosphatidylserine exposure from RBC membranes; procoagulant microparticles; imbalanced pro-/anticoagulant factors; systemic inflammation.
- Pulmonary thromboembolism most common; venous and arterial thrombi both occur.

*Protein-Losing Nephropathy (PLN)*
- High prevalence of hypercoagulability by TEG.
- Mechanisms: AT loss through glomerulus; increased platelet reactivity; hyperfibrinogenemia; decreased fibrinolysis.
- Hypoalbuminemia NOT associated with increased hypercoagulability risk in dogs (unlike humans).
- Protein-losing enteropathy (PLE): also hypercoagulable by TEG; thromboembolic complications reported but incidence relatively low.

*Neoplasia*
- Majority hypercoagulable; mechanism includes increased TF expression (cellular and soluble), platelet hyperactivity, systemic inflammation.
- Hypocoagulability seen with metastatic disease / advanced stage.

*Endocrinopathy*
- Hyperadrenocorticism (endogenous and exogenous GC) → hypercoagulable + thrombotic disease.
- Hypothyroidism and diabetes mellitus: thrombosis reported in small proportion.

*Inflammation/Sepsis*
- Acute pancreatitis, sepsis, SIRS, trauma, immune-mediated disease → hypercoagulable.
- Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs): promote clot formation and delay fibrinolysis in dogs.
- Severe inflammation → consumptive coagulopathy → DIC → hypocoagulable.

**Thromboprophylaxis indications and agents**
- Mandatory in IMHA and PLN unless contraindicated (e.g., platelet count <30,000/mcL).
- Consider in other conditions where hypercoagulability or thrombosis risk factors detected.
- Clopidogrel: superior platelet inhibition vs low-dose aspirin in dogs.
- Antiplatelet agents, LMWH, unfractionated heparin, direct oral Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban) all have anticoagulant activity in dogs and cats; large prospective trials lacking.
- Antiplatelet/anticoagulants do NOT dissolve existing thrombi — they reduce thrombogenesis.

**Vitamin K deficiency (hypocoagulable)**
- Most common: anticoagulant rodenticide (vitamin K1 antagonism of epoxide reductase).
- Less common: decreased intestinal synthesis, decreased absorption, cholestasis.
- Factors affected: II, VII, IX, X; anticoagulant proteins C, S.
- Factor half-lives (see Ch 170): FVII shortest (6.2 h) → PT prolongs first; then aPTT/ACT.
- PIVKA levels increase 12–24 h after ingestion (not specific).
- Treatment: Vitamin K1 (NOT K3 — slower onset, Heinz body risk).

**Vitamin K1 dosing**
- Initial: 2.5–5 mg/kg SC or PO; then 6–12 h later maintenance 2.5–5 mg/kg PO divided into 2–3 doses/day.
- Cats with liver disease: 0.5–1.5 mg/kg SC q12h.
- PT check 36–48 h after discontinuing therapy to confirm adequate duration.
- PT typically normalizes 14–36 h after starting VK1 therapy.
- IV vitamin K1 → anaphylaxis risk.

**Hepatic disease (hypocoagulable)**
- Chronic hepatopathies, portosystemic shunts, extrahepatic biliary obstruction: generally normo- to hypercoagulable on TE.
- Advanced chronic hepatopathies: hypocoagulable.
- Acute hepatopathy: normo- or hypocoagulable.
- Cats with cholestatic disease (VK-replete): hyper- or normocoagulable; hypocoagulability in synthetic failure.

**DIC**
- Tissue factor = primary mediator.
- Stages: Initial (compensated/hypercoagulable) → Progressive (overt/hypocoagulable).
- Lab findings: thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/aPTT, elevated D-dimers, hypofibrinogenemia, decreased AT, schistocytes.
- No single test has high sensitivity or specificity; diagnostic algorithms needed.
- Prognosis: poor regardless of underlying disorder; higher mortality with hypocoagulability and multiple hemostatic abnormalities.
- Plasma transfusion: 10–20 mL/kg IV initial dose dogs; 6 mL/kg cats; prophylactic plasma transfusion NOT routinely recommended in DIC.
- Heparin in hypercoagulable DIC: controversial; heparin potentiates AT (which is already depleted in DIC); avoid in hypocoagulable phase.

**Acquired anticoagulants**
- IgG antibodies that inhibit coagulation factors or increase clearance.
- Anti-phospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant): prolongs aPTT; paradoxically associated with THROMBOSIS.
- Plasma mixing test: supports diagnosis of acquired anticoagulant.

**Hemophilia A and B**
- Hemophilia A (FVIII deficiency) more common than B (FIX deficiency); both in dogs and cats.
- X-linked recessive: males most commonly affected.
- Maine Coon cats: suspected inherited FI and FIX deficiency.
- Diagnosis: normal PT, prolonged aPTT/ACT → measure FVIII:C or FIX:C.
- Normal factor activity: 50–150%.
- Classification: Mild 6–20%; Moderate 2–5%; Marked/Severe <2%.
- Severity correlation with clinical signs not consistent in dogs.
- Desmopressin: transiently increases FVIII in people; does NOT appear to work in dogs.
- Repeated transfusions → inhibitor development (factor VIII or IX inhibitors).

**Factor XII deficiency (cats)**
- Common hereditary trait in domestic cats (two high-frequency F12 mutations).
- Prolonged aPTT and ACT, but NO clinical bleeding (in vivo coagulation depends on TF/FVII).
- Does NOT require treatment.
- Concurrent hemophilia A or B can cause hemorrhage.

**Congenital vitamin K-dependent factor deficiency**
- Reported in Devon Rex cats; responsive to vitamin K1.

**Scott Syndrome**
- Defect of procoagulant phosphatidylserine externalization on platelet surface.
- Hemorrhage; coagulopathy (not thrombocytopenia). Covered more in Ch 175.

**Hyperfibrinolysis (Greyhounds)**
- Delayed post-surgical/traumatic hemorrhage.
- Aminocaproic acid 500 mg per 30 kg dog PO q8h, beginning post-operatively.

**Plasma products**
- FFP: all coagulation factors including labile V and VIII; initial dose 10–20 mL/kg IV dogs; 6 mL/kg cats.
- Cryoprecipitate: FVIII, FXIII, vWF, fibrinogen, fibronectin; 50–80% levels of FFP in smaller volume.
- Cryoprecipitate-poor plasma: adequate for most factors except cryoprecipitate content.

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIC (DIS-BD-DIC) — enrichment: heparin use in hypercoagulable phase, TAT marker. ✓ Haemophilia A/B — factor classification thresholds (mild 6–20%, moderate 2–5%, severe <2%), desmopressin ineffective in dogs. ✓ Factor XII deficiency — mechanism and no-treatment rationale. ✓ Anticoag Rodenticide — dosing and timing of VK1 therapy. ✓ Vit K deficiency — hepatic disease dosing (cats 0.5–1.5 mg/kg SC q12h). ✓ IMHA (DIS-BD-IMHA) — up to 50% of deaths from thromboembolism even with prophylaxis. ✗ MISSING: Primary polycythemia vera / hypercoagulable workup (covered Ch 173) as distinct entity beyond hyperviscosity.

### Ch 172 — Nonregenerative Anemia
*(Winzelberg Olson S)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — nonregenerative anemia work-up)

**Definition**
- Anemia = Hct/Hb/RBC below reference range; anemia is most frequently characterized by Hct in clinical practice.
- Nonregenerative anemia: reticulocyte count <60,000/mcL (dogs) or <50,000/mcL (cats) using aggregate reticulocytes (automated).
- Regenerative anemia may appear nonregenerative in first 3–7 days (pre-regenerative phase).
- Anemia in approximately 4% of feline and up to 31% of canine patients >8 years.
- Nonregenerative anemia most common form in both dogs and cats.

**Reticulocyte production index (RPI) in cats**
- Formula: (Ret% × [Hct/37]) / ([-0.05] × Hct + 3.25).
- Cutoff: 0.35 (manual counting), 0.59 (automated) — high sensitivity and specificity for regenerative status.

**Reticulocyte indices for iron deficiency (dogs)**
- Low reticulocyte Hb ≤26 g/dL; low CHretic ≤20.1 pg; low RET-He <20.9 pg.
- High % reticulocytes with low Hb (≥74.2%) and low CHretic (≥50.7%) → suspicious for iron-deficient erythropoiesis.
- Iron saturation (serum Fe/TIBC) <20% → suggestive of iron deficiency.

**Iron-deficiency vs. anemia of inflammation**
- Both: low serum iron, low reticulocyte indices.
- Iron deficiency: low/normal TIBC; low ferritin (unless concurrent hepatic disease).
- Anemia of inflammation: normal-to-increased TIBC (acute), low TIBC (chronic); increased ferritin; mechanism = hepcidin elevation → ferroportin degradation → iron trapping.

**Up to one-third of dogs and >50% of cats with IMHA may be nonregenerative at diagnosis.**

**Differential diagnosis (comprehensive — Table 172.1)**
- Primary medullary: aplastic anemia, MDS, myeloproliferative syndromes, primary myelofibrosis, dysmyelopoiesis, congenital dyserythropoiesis (English Springer Spaniel), inherited cobalamin malabsorption (Giant Schnauzer, Border Collie, Beagle, Australian Shepherd), myelophthisis (leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, bone marrow necrosis), NRIMHA/PIMA, pure red cell aplasia.
- Secondary medullary (infectious): FeLV (cats), FIV (cats), parvovirus, Ehrlichia, Bartonella (dogs), sepsis.
- Drug reactions: antimicrobials, chemo drugs, estrogens (dogs), phenobarbital (dogs), recombinant human EPO, copper deficiency secondary to chelation therapy; aflatoxin.
- Neoplasia: Sertoli cell tumor (dogs), lymphoma, histiocytic disease.
- Congenital: osteosclerosis from pyruvate kinase deficiency (dogs), cobalamin malabsorption (dogs), folate deficiency (cats), copper deficiency (dogs).
- Immune: NRIMHA/PIMA, pure red cell aplasia.
- Extramedullary: renal (AKI, CKD, maldevelopment), endocrine (hypoadrenocorticism, hypothyroidism), GI/malabsorptive (cobalamin deficiency), hepatic, pancreatitis, neoplasia, iron deficiency (GI bleeding, hematuria, ectoparasites).

**Pure red cell aplasia vs. aplastic anemia**
- Pure red cell aplasia: normocytic normochromic anemia only; bone marrow = erythroid hypoplasia/aplasia, normal granulo- and megakaryopoiesis.
- Aplastic anemia: bicytopenia/pancytopenia; bone marrow = marked reduction/absence of all hematopoietic tissue, replaced by fat.
- PIMA survival at 12 months: 43%.

**Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs)**
- Indicated when PCV ≤20% (moderate-severe anemia) and other methods insufficient.
- Anti-EPO antibody risk (pure red cell aplasia): epoetin alfa/beta → up to 25–30% of dogs and cats; darbepoetin → 6–8% of dogs and cats; median onset 14.5 weeks in cats.
- If anti-EPO antibody aplasia develops → discontinue ESA; consider immunosuppression.
- Concurrent monthly parenteral iron supplementation recommended with ESAs.

**Darbepoetin dosing**
- Cats: 1 mcg/kg SQ once weekly until target PCV attained; then every 2–3 weeks; target PCV 25–35%.
- Dogs: 0.5–0.8 mcg/kg SQ once weekly initially; reduce interval up to q21 days; target PCV >30%.

**Epoetin dosing**
- Dogs and cats: 100 units/kg SQ 3× weekly; reduce to 2× weekly maintenance once target PCV attained.

**Iron supplementation**
- Oral: ferrous sulfate 100–300 mg/dog PO q24h (20–60 mg elemental Fe); 50–100 mg/cat PO q24h (10–20 mg elemental Fe). Ferrous gluconate: 16.25 mg/kg/day in cats.
- Parenteral: iron dextran 10–20 mg/kg IM dogs (max 300 mg); 10 mg/kg IM cats. Anaphylaxis risk IV.

**Immunosuppressive therapy for medullary causes**
- Glucocorticoids: first-line for PIMA/pure red cell aplasia/nonregenerative IMHA.
- Cyclophosphamide: 50 mg/m2 PO q24h × 4 days, repeated weekly (for pure red cell aplasia not responding to GC alone).
- Azathioprine: 2 mg/kg PO q24h × 5 days then q48h — dogs with pure red cell aplasia and NRIMHA. NOT in cats.
- Cyclosporine: 4–12 mg/kg PO q24h (dogs with NRIMHA); 10 mg/kg PO q24h median (cats with pure red cell aplasia).
- Mycophenolate mofetil: 10–15 mg/kg PO q12h (dogs with aplastic anemia, MDS, NRIMHA).
- Menatetrenone (vitamin K2 analog): 2 mg/kg for feline MDS; 1.4 mg/kg/day median in dogs.

### Ch 173 — Erythrocytosis and Primary Polycythemia
*(LeVine DN, Hohenhaus A)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — primary polycythemia vera / erythrocytosis work-up)

**Classification**
- Relative erythrocytosis (RE): volume contraction (vomiting, diarrhea, diuretics); splenic contraction (dogs, horses).
- Absolute erythrocytosis (AE): primary (PE) or secondary (SE).
- Primary erythrocytosis (PE): EPO-independent; usually acquired somatic mutation (analogous to human polycythemia vera); JAK2 V617F found in 1/5 tested dogs; most companion animals with PE lack splenomegaly/leukocytosis/thrombocytosis seen in human PV.
- Secondary erythrocytosis (SE): increased EPO.
  - Appropriate SE: hypoxemia (right-to-left cardiac shunts — rPDA, Tetralogy of Fallot; pulmonary disease; congenital methemoglobinemia from CYB5R deficiency).
  - Inappropriate SE: non-hypoxemic EPO excess (renal tumors most common — renal cell carcinoma, pyelonephritis; other tumors — nasal fibrosarcoma, schwannoma, cecal leiomyosarcoma, hemangiosarcoma; endocrinopathies — hyperadrenocorticism, hyperthyroidism, acromegaly).

**Signalment for PE**
- Middle-aged to older dogs and cats; no sex predilection; some series show increased male cats.

**Clinical signs of AE**
- Hyperviscosity/hypervolemia: neurologic signs (seizures, altered mentation, twitching, ataxia, blindness); tortuous scleral/retinal vessels; polyuria/polydipsia (vasopressin impairment); vomiting/diarrhea (GI hypoperfusion).
- Cyanotic mucous membranes: appropriate SE from right-to-left shunts.
- Differential cyanosis (caudal body only): rPDA.
- Brown mucous membranes: hereditary methemoglobinemia.
- Hyperemic mucous membranes: PE and all other SE.

**Clinicopathology**
- Hypoglycemia: in vitro glucose consumption by excess erythrocytes; glucometer under-reads with erythrocytosis.
- Cats with PE: may have elevated creatine kinase (ischemic myopathy).

**Diagnostic algorithm**
1. Exclude RE (rehydration, fluid response, breed-specific reference intervals — sighthounds, Norwegian Forest Cat, Siberian, Holy Birman).
2. If AE confirmed: arterial blood gas (PaO2 <80 mmHg or SaO2 <95% = hypoxemia → appropriate SE).
3. Cardiac/pulmonary evaluation: thoracic radiographs, echo, contrast echo (bubble study for right-to-left shunts).
4. If normoxic: abdominal imaging (CT or ultrasound for masses/renal lesions), minimum database.
5. Serum EPO: elevated = strongly suggestive of SE (specific but not sensitive); low/normal EPO can occur in SE.
6. JAK2 V617F testing (dogs via NC State): confirms PV if positive; negative does not exclude PE.
7. Bone marrow: non-specific erythroid hyperplasia in PE and SE; not recommended unless myeloproliferative neoplasm suspected.

**Treatment — phlebotomy**
- Mainstay of PE and uncorrectable SE.
- 10–20 mL/kg with or without crystalloid replacement; serial phlebotomies.
- Target HCT in humans <45%; in cats, PCV <50% may be unnecessary.
- Mean phlebotomy interval: 6.5 weeks (cats).
- Hirudotherapy (medicinal leeching): 5–10 mL per leech + oozing 24–48 h post-detachment; successfully used in cats with PE.

**Cytoreductive therapy — hydroxyurea (HU)**
- Indicated if phlebotomy too frequent or not tolerated.
- Dose strategies: 50 mg/kg PO q48h; 30 mg/kg PO q24h × 7 days → 15 mg/kg PO q24–48h.
- Cats: lower daily doses; do not exceed 100 mg/kg and not more frequently than every 5 days.
- Side-effects: reversible bone marrow suppression; Heinz bodies (cats); macrocytosis; alopecia; nail sloughing; methemoglobinemia (blocks methemoglobin recycling).

**JAK inhibitors**
- Ruxolitinib (JAK1/2): used in HU-resistant human PV; not first-line due to long-term uncertainty.
- Oclacitinib (Apoquel): JAK1 inhibitor with some JAK2 activity; possible utility in canine PE with documented JAK2 mutations.

**Prognosis**
- Cats with PE: survival >17 months with treatment; majority good quality of life.
- Dogs with PE: 1–33 months with phlebotomy, HU, or both.

### Ch 174 — Immune-Mediated Hemolytic Anemia and Other Regenerative Anemias
*(Weingart C, Kohn B)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ IMHA (DIS-BD-IMHA) — extensive enrichment opportunities below.

**Classification of hemolytic anemias**
- Extravascular (predominant): phagocytosis by mononuclear phagocytic system (spleen, liver, bone marrow).
- Intravascular (less common): membrane attack complex or fragmentation → hemoglobinemia/hemoglobinuria → ghost cells.

**IMHA — Signalment**
- Non-associative (primary) IMHA: dogs of all ages, median 5–7 years; breeds: Cocker Spaniels, Irish Setters, Dachshunds, Poodles, Springer Spaniels, Bearded Collies, Old English Sheepdogs; somewhat more common in females.
- Cats: no sex or breed predisposition; 12/19 cats in one study were <3 years of age.
- Up to 1/3 of dogs with IMHA have concurrent ITP (Evans' syndrome).
- Up to 30% of dogs (50% cats) may be nonregenerative at initial presentation.

**Antibody mechanisms**
- Autoagglutinins (IgM or IgG at high levels): RBC agglutination in 0.9% saline.
- Hemolysins (IgM, IgG): activate complement → membrane attack complex → intravascular hemolysis.
- Incomplete warm antibodies (IgG, less often IgM + complement): most common; agglutinate only with Coombs' reagent; extravascular hemolysis (spleen main site).
- Cold antibodies (4°C): rare; cold season → cyanosis/necrosis of extremities.
- IgG-loaded RBCs: destroyed by Fc-receptor macrophages without complement.

**Laboratory findings — IMHA diagnostic criteria (ACVIM 2019)**
- Diagnosis requires ≥2 signs of immune-mediated RBC destruction AND ≥1 sign of hemolysis.
  - OR ≥1 sign of immune-mediated RBC destruction + ≥1 sign of hemolysis if no other cause identified.
- **Signs of immune-mediated destruction**: spherocytosis (dogs), agglutination (unwashed RBCs), positive DAT/flow cytometry, persistent agglutination after washing.
- **Signs of hemolysis**: hyperbilirubinemia, hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria, ghost cells.

**Saline agglutination test (SAT)**
- Mix 1 drop RBCs with 4 drops 0.9% saline.
- Positive SAT in IMHA: dogs 78–100%; cats 65–100%.
- Wash RBCs 3× in 0.9% saline at 21–37°C to confirm (excludes rouleaux, nonspecific binding).
- Persistent agglutination after washing: rare without IMHA (4–10% dogs; 21% cats) → almost pathognomonic.

**Spherocytes**
- Present in ~2/3 of dogs with IMHA.
- >5 spherocytes/100× oil immersion field: supportive of IMHA.
- 3–4 spherocytes/100× oil immersion field also consistent if no other hemolysis cause.
- Cats: spherocytes difficult to identify (lack central pallor).

**Direct Coombs test (DAT)**
- Sensitivity: 61–82% dogs; 82% cats.
- Specificity: 94–100% dogs; 95–100% cats.
- False negatives: technical errors, low antibody concentration, unfavorable Ag-Ab ratio, pre-treatment with GC.
- Even after days of IS therapy, DAT can still be positive.
- Do not transfuse before collecting sample for DAT.

**Other laboratory findings**
- WBC up to 100 × 10⁹/L in dogs (cytokine storm); monocytosis.
- Marked hyperbilirubinemia and bilirubinuria (common dogs; mild in chronic cats).
- CRP: severely elevated in dogs.
- Hypokalemia: renal tubular acidosis.
- ANA: rarely positive (suggests SLE).
- Coagulation: hypercoagulability (50%) and DIC (51–80% of dogs) on initial or progressive evaluation.

**Transfusion thresholds**
- Dogs: PCV approximately <15–18%.
- Cats: PCV <12–15%.
- Use RBCs as fresh as possible (not older than 7–10 days); older stored blood associated with higher mortality.
- Blood type and crossmatch before transfusion.

**Immunosuppressive treatment**

*Induction — Dogs*
- **Prednisolone 1 mg/kg PO q12h or 50–60 mg/m²/day** in dogs >25 kg.
- Alternatively, methylprednisolone sodium succinate 10 mg/kg IV once, then oral.
- If oral not possible: dexamethasone 0.2–0.4 mg/kg IV q24h initially.
- Response expected within 2–3 weeks.
- Second agent (MMF, CsA, azathioprine, leflunomide) in severe/refractory cases — see Ch 169 for doses.
- **MMF: 8–10 [–12] mg/kg PO q12h** (also stated as induction option).
- **CsA: 5 mg/kg PO q12h** (maintenance/second line).
- **Azathioprine: 2 mg/kg [50 mg/m²] PO q24h initially; reduce to q48h after 2–3 weeks**.
- **Leflunomide: 2 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- Neutrophil/platelet counts: check every 1–2 weeks on cytostatic drugs.

*Induction — Cats*
- **Prednisolone 1.5–2 mg/kg PO q12h × ~3 days** (or methylprednisolone 10 mg/kg IV once, then oral).
- Then reduce to 1 mg/kg PO q12h.
- Second agents for cats: **chlorambucil 0.2 mg/kg PO q24h initially, then 0.1 mg/kg PO q24h**; CsA 7 mg/kg PO q24h; MMF 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h; leflunomide 2 mg/kg PO q24h.
- Dexamethasone 0.15–0.25 mg/kg PO q24h for relapse in cats.

*Maintenance/tapering*
- Dogs: if PCV stable (>30% for 2 weeks) → reduce prednisolone ~25% every 3 weeks.
- If second IS drug used: reduce prednisolone 25% first; then taper second drug every 3–4 weeks.
- Total duration: usually 6–8 months before discontinuation.
- GI protection at high doses: omeprazole 1 mg/kg PO q12h + sucralfate 20–30 mg/kg PO q12h.
- Urinalysis every 8–12 weeks (UTI risk).

*Thromboprophylaxis (dogs — REQUIRED)*
- Indication: all dogs during initial treatment EXCEPT when platelet count <30,000/mcL.
- **Clopidogrel: initially 10 mg/kg, then 1–4 mg/kg PO q24h** (preferred, superior platelet inhibition).
- **Acetylsalicylic acid: 0.5 mg/kg PO q24h** if clopidogrel not available.
- **Unfractionated heparin: 150–300 U/kg SC q6h, OR 100 U/kg IV bolus + 900 U/kg q24h**.
- **Dalteparin (LMWH): 150–175 U/kg SC q8h**.
- **Enoxaparin (LMWH): 0.8–1 mg/kg SC q6–8h**. Individual dose adjustment via anti-Xa if available.
- **Rivaroxaban (factor Xa inhibitor): 1–2 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- Discontinue thromboprophylaxis when dog in remission (Hct + CRP normal) with no other risk factors.

*Human IVIG*
- **0.5–1 g/kg IV once** in non-responders; routine use not recommended.
- No superior efficacy demonstrated in randomized trial vs. vincristine for acute adjunctive management.

*Splenectomy*
- Refractory cases; controversial.

**Prognosis — dogs (non-associative IMHA)**
- Fatality rate: 24–52%.
- Most IMHA deaths within first 1–2 weeks; 6-month overall survival: 73%.
- Relapse rate: up to 33%; may require lifelong medication.
- Long-term: 22.9% relapse in 61 dogs at median 517 days (range 94–3972 days).

**Negative prognostic markers (dogs)**
- Autoagglutination, intravascular hemolysis, persistently low reticulocyte count, leukocytosis, left shift, thrombocytopenia, high serum bilirubin and BUN, elevated liver enzymes, hypoalbuminemia, hyperlactatemia, prolonged PT/aPTT.

**Prognosis — cats (non-associative IMHA)**
- Mortality rate: 23.5–38%.
- Recurrence rate: 31% (up to 40 weeks post-diagnosis in one study).
- Negative prognostic factors: older age, high serum bilirubin.

**Evans' syndrome (concurrent IMHA + ITP) in dogs**
- One study: 76% died/euthanized within 30 days (poor prognosis).
- Another study (platelet count ≤15,000/mcL definition): 25% mortality (similar to either alone — DIC exclusion may explain discrepancy).

**Pyruvate kinase (PK) deficiency — cats**
- 7.4% of Somali/Abyssinian cats in German study.
- Also: European/Domestic Shorthair, Bengal, Singapura, Maine Coon, Norwegian Forest.
- R isoenzyme absent → energy deficiency → shortened RBC lifespan → chronic hemolysis.
- Hct range in 25 affected cats: 13–44% (median 29%); reticulocyte counts 5,000–936,000/mcL (median 98,000).
- Splenomegaly common; 6/25 cats died at median 4.1 years.
- DNA test available; no specific therapy (splenectomy for temporary stabilization).

**PK deficiency — dogs**
- Basenji, Beagle, West Highland White Terrier, Dachshund, others.
- Progressive myelofibrosis and osteosclerosis ~1 year of age; hepatic failure from iron overload.
- PK activity may be normal/elevated in dogs (M2 isoenzyme persists in dogs unlike cats).
- DNA test required for definitive diagnosis; die age 1–8 years.

**PFK deficiency**
- English Springer Spaniel, Cocker Spaniel, Wachtelhund, Whippet, mixed breeds.
- Low intracellular 2,3-DPG → high Hb-O2 affinity → increased alkaline fragility.
- Hemolytic crises triggered by exertion, panting, barking, heat (hyperventilation-induced alkalosis).
- DNA test available.

**Cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency (methemoglobin reductase)**
- Dogs: Chihuahua, Borzoi, English Setter, Corgi, Pomeranian, Poodle mix.
- Cats: Domestic Shorthair.
- Met-Hb >10%: blood brown on filter paper (vs. normal venous blood turning red).
- Cyanosis at Met-Hb ≥10%; clinical signs at ≥40%; ataxia/lethargy/dyspnea/stupor/coma >60%.
- Dogs: Met-Hb 13–41%; cats 44–52%.
- Hereditary deficiency usually does not require treatment.

**Heinz body anemia**
- Cats more susceptible (8–10 reactive sulfhydryl groups/Hb vs 2–4 in others; depleted glutathione; feline spleen has large sinusoidal openings and cannot remove Heinz-body RBCs efficiently).
- Heinz bodies in up to 5% RBCs in healthy cats (normal finding).
- Eccentrocytes = dominant marker of oxidative stress in DOGS.
- N-acetylcysteine: 140 mg/kg IV initially, then 70 mg/kg IV q6h.
- SAMe: 20–40 mg/kg PO q12h 1 h before feeding.

**Acetaminophen toxicity thresholds**
- Cats: doses as low as 10 mg/kg may cause clinical signs.
- Dogs: clinical signs expected at high doses ~200 mg/kg.
- Cats: methemoglobinemia 2–4 h; hemolytic anemia 2–3 days after ingestion.

**Zinc intoxication**
- Coins: radiographic detection; confirm by plasma zinc measurement.

**Hypophosphatemia-induced hemolysis**
- Phosphate <0.8 mmol/L (<2.5 mg/dL) → hemolysis, myopathy, cardiac/neurologic dysfunction.
- Causes: DM ketoacidosis treatment, refeeding syndrome, oral phosphate binders.
- Treatment: 0.01–0.03 mg/kg/h IV constant-rate infusion in anorectic/vomiting patients.

**Splenic torsion**
- Large breeds (Great Danes); intravascular hemolysis → hemoabdomen.

**Blood loss thresholds**
- >20–30% circulating blood volume lost → cardiovascular signs.
- Full blood volume: dogs 80–90 mL/kg; cats 50–60 mL/kg.
- Initial bolus for acute blood loss: 1/4 blood volume (20–22 mL/kg dogs; 12.5–15 mL/kg cats) over 15–30 min.
- Transfusion indicated if blood loss >30 mL/kg or Hct drops to <20–25%.

### Ch 175 — Immune Thrombocytopenia, von Willebrand Disease, and Other Platelet Disorders
*(Callan MB, Catalfamo JL)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ ITP (DIS-BD-IMTP) — enrichment below. ✓ vWD (DIS-BD-VWD) — enrichment. ✓ Thrombocytopenia — ✓ Thrombocytopathia.

**Primary hemostasis overview**
- Platelet count 200–500 million/mcL; lifespan 6–10 days.
- Petechiae/ecchymoses/epistaxis: most common with platelet count <30,000/mcL.
- vWD: rarely causes petechiae; ecchymoses may follow trauma.

**Primary ITP — Pathophysiology**
- Complex: platelet destruction (anti-GPIIb/IIIa antibodies predominant) + impaired megakaryocyte and platelet production (cell-mediated).
- Reduced Treg cells observed in pilot study — potential causal role.
- Primary ITP in 871 dogs: only 5.6% of thrombocytopenic cases; ITP is most common cause of SEVERE thrombocytopenia.
- Infectious agents implicated in secondary ITP: Anaplasma spp., Babesia spp., Ehrlichia spp., Leptospira spp., Leishmania infantum; drugs (sulfonamides, cephalosporins most common — 5–7 days sensitization period; rapid thrombocytopenia on re-exposure).

**ITP diagnostic criteria**
- Diagnosis presumptive — always by exclusion.
- Severe thrombocytopenia ≤30,000/mcL typical; in 34 dogs with primary ITP: median platelet count 9,500/mcL (range 0–40,000/mcL).
- DOGiBAT score: standardized bleeding scoring (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = severe) at 9 anatomic sites; inversely correlates with platelet count; directly correlates with transfusion requirements and hospitalization duration.
- Platelet count does NOT consistently predict bleeding risk or mortality.
- Platelet-associated immunoglobulin (PAIg) by flow cytometry: variably sensitive (29.4–100% direct PAIgG); nonspecific.

**ITP treatment — dogs**
- Glucocorticoids: mainstay.
- Adjunctive: vincristine (first-line adjunct) OR human IVIG (hIVIG).
  - **Vincristine: 0.02 mg/kg IV once** — no difference vs hIVIG in randomized trial; vincristine preferred (lower cost, easy administration).
  - **hIVIG: 0.5 g/kg IV once** — can shorten severe thrombocytopenia.
  - Vincristine + cyclosporine concurrently → increased neutropenia risk (odds ratio 12.97); CsA inhibits vincristine metabolism.
  - Most dogs respond to GC within 7 days; second IS agent not always necessary.
- Second IS agents (retrospective evidence only — no controlled trials): azathioprine, cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, leflunomide.
  - **Mycophenolate monotherapy** (when GC contraindicated): median dose 8.5 mg/kg PO q12h; 5/5 dogs achieved complete remission; median follow-up 16 months.
- Duration of treatment: 4–6 months tapering.

**Platelet transfusions for ITP**
- Uncommonly indicated (transfused platelets rapidly destroyed).
- Exception: uncontrolled/life-threatening bleeding (CNS or pulmonary hemorrhage).
- Fresh whole blood: most readily available source.
- Lyophilized (StablePlate Rx) and cryopreserved canine platelets: commercially available; low adverse reaction rates.

**ITP prognosis — dogs**
- 89.6% survived to hospital discharge; 86.6% achieved remission (platelet >150,000/mcL).
- Relapse: 31% at 15–1239 days (median 79 days); 11/14 relapses within 1 year.
- 1-year mortality (all causes): 19.4%; from ITP specifically: 11.9%.
- Concurrent IMHA + ITP (Evans' syndrome): variable prognosis (see Ch 174).

**ITP — cats**
- Primary ITP rare: 3% of 194 thrombocytopenic cats; mean platelet count 19,000/mcL (range 5–40,000).
- Secondary ITP from FeLV, FIV, FIP, lymphoma.
- Mortality: 15% (13 cats in two series).
- Oral prednisolone effective in only 5/9 cats; some required dexamethasone, cyclosporine, chlorambucil.
- 3 of 4 surviving cats in one series required long-term IS therapy; frequent relapses.

**von Willebrand Disease**

*Classification*
- Type 1: low plasma vWF, full multimer array, mild-moderate bleeding.
- Type 2: variable plasma vWF, absent high-molecular-weight multimers (German Shorthaired/Wirehaired Pointers), moderate-severe bleeding.
- Type 3: complete absence of plasma vWF (Dutch Kooiker, Scottish Terrier, Shetland Sheepdog), severe bleeding.

*Diagnosis*
- Normal platelet count, PT, aPTT + primary hemostatic bleeding → evaluate for vWD.
- BMBT >4 minutes + platelet count >100,000/mcL + PCV >30%: suggestive of vWD, thrombopathia, or vasculopathy.
- Plasma vWF:Ag (ELISA, Cornell University Comparative Coagulation): normal 70–180%; borderline 50–69%; abnormal 0–49%.
- Bleeding unlikely if vWF:Ag >30%; type 1 vWD dogs typically <35%.
- PFA-100 (collagen/ADP cartridge): 95.7% sensitivity, 100% specificity in dogs for type 1 vWD <35%.
- Type 2 diagnosis: vWF:collagen binding activity (CBA) ELISA (detects absence of high MW multimers).
- DNA testing (breeds listed in Table 175.1): most Doberman, Corgi, Kerry Blue Terrier, Irish Setter, Papillon, Poodle, WHW Terrier, etc. share same c.7437 G>A mutation (type 1).
- Do NOT screen during illness or pregnancy (vWF:Ag increases — false normalization).

*Treatment*
- **DDAVP (desmopressin): 1 mcg/kg SC** (nasal spray formulation), 30 min before surgery; not more than once daily (tachyphylaxis and hyponatremia risk).
  - In Dobermans with type 1 vWD: mean plasma vWF:Ag increased from 10% to 17% at 1 h.
- **Cryoprecipitate (CRYO): 1 unit/10 kg** (1 unit = prepared from 200–250 mL FFP) — product of choice for bleeding in vWD.
- **FFP: 10–15 mL/kg** — alternative if CRYO unavailable.
- Half-life of plasma vWF ≈12 h → CRYO or FFP may need q8–12h for severe bleeding.
- Compatible PRBCs should be available when vWD dogs undergo surgery.

**Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome (AVWS)**
- Structural/functional alterations in vWF from lymphoproliferative, myeloproliferative, cardiovascular, autoimmune, or other disorders.
- Documented in Cavalier King Charles Spaniels and other dogs with myxomatous mitral valve disease and subaortic stenosis (decreased vWF:Ag and loss of high MW multimers).
- Consider in new-onset bleeding with laboratory findings suggesting vWD.

**Hereditary thrombopathias (Table 175.2)**

| Disorder | Defect | Breed | Testing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glanzmann thrombasthenia | GPIIb-IIIa absent/deficient | Otterhound, Great Pyrénées | DNA (Auburn, PawPrint, VetGen, Animal Genetics, HealthGene) |

**Acquired platelet dysfunction**
- Renal disease, hepatobiliary disease, paraproteinemia.
- Drugs with anti-platelet effects: hydroxyethyl starch, NSAIDs, cephalosporins.
- Therapeutic anti-platelet drugs: aspirin (irreversible COX inhibitor) and clopidogrel (P2Y12 receptor antagonist).

### Ch 176 — Nonneoplastic White Blood Cell Disorders and Pancytopenia
*(Johns JL)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Immune-Mediated Neutropenia (DIS-IMNP) — enrichment below.

**Immune-Mediated Neutropenia (IMN)**

*Presentation and associations*
- May occur with IMHA, ITP, polyarthritis.
- Drug associations: cephalosporins, phenobarbital, primidone, ivermectin, methimazole; one case with hypoadrenocorticism.
- Younger animals at higher risk: median age at diagnosis 5 years; 4-fold increased risk if <4 years.
- Clinical signs: lethargy, persistent fever, recurrent/persistent skin infections or abscesses, reactive lymphadenopathy, GI signs, coughing. Some subclinically affected.

*Hematologic abnormalities*
- Absolute neutrophil count significantly lower in IMN than other neutropenic disorders.
- Cutoff for defining IMN: <1500 neutrophils/mcL on at least 2 sequential CBCs (one study).
- 60% of dogs: ANC <500/mcL; 91%: ANC <1000/mcL.
- Band neutrophil counts often low or zero (contrasts with sepsis/overwhelming demand).
- Degenerative left shift (immature > segmented) suggests peripheral demand rather than IMN.
- Bone marrow: myeloid hyperplasia in 23/35 dogs; myeloid hypoplasia in 10; "left shift" in maturation (decreased late-stage neutrophilic cells); immune attack on myeloid precursors demonstrated by IFA for IgG/C3.

*Diagnosis*
- Definitive: species-specific anti-neutrophil antibodies (not established in veterinary medicine).
- Flow cytometry: variable sensitivity in dogs; direct testing impaired by severe neutropenia, cell fragility; indirect flow cytometry or leukoagglutination on banked serum preferred.
- Presumptive diagnosis: after exclusion of other causes + response to immunosuppressive therapy.

*Treatment*
- Corticosteroids: mainstay.
- Adjunctive: azathioprine (delay until initial response to avoid confusion with myelosuppression) OR cyclosporine (initial adjunct option).
- Taper: variable 1–6 months.
- Outcomes: 63% (17/27) required continued corticosteroids at 6 months; 63% (12/19) at 1 year.
- Relapse: 34% became neutropenic during/after taper; refractory in 4/5 of relapsed dogs despite adjunct therapy.

**Pancytopenia**
- Definition: non-regenerative anemia + neutropenia + thrombocytopenia.
- Causes: multiple concurrent disorders (sepsis + anemia of inflammation + platelet consumption) OR bone marrow production impairment.

**Differential diagnosis for pancytopenia by bone marrow disease (Box 176.1)**

*Responsive (hypercellular/normocellular — ineffective hematopoiesis)*
- Immune-mediated destruction of hemic precursors of multiple lineages.
- Primary or secondary MDS; secondary dysmyelopoiesis.
- Medications: cephalosporins, phenobarbital/primidone, vincristine.
- Ehrlichia canis infection.

*Non-responsive (hypocellular/acellular — general marrow suppression)*
- Infectious: parvovirus, fungal, Cytauxzoon felis, E. canis.
- Toxins/medications/hormones: estrogens, azathioprine, griseofulvin, phenylbutazone, cephalosporins, meclofenamic acid, TMS + fenbendazole, albendazole, quinidine, chloramphenicol, captopril.
- Chemotherapy/radiation.
- Immune-mediated suppression of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells.
- Neoplastic marrow infiltration: leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, histiocytic sarcoma.
- Non-neoplastic myelophthisis: osteopetrosis, myelofibrosis.
- Myelonecrosis.

**Primary MDS classification (modified FAB scheme for small animals)**
- MDS-EB (excess blasts): most common in dogs; <20% myeloblasts; dysplasia all 3 lineages; worst prognosis; in cats 31–65% of primary MDS; FeLV-associated in cats.
- MDS-RC (refractory cytopenia): older dogs; nonregenerative anemia primary; bone marrow hypercellular (erythroid hyperplasia) or normocellular; erythroid dysplasia; insidious onset.
- MDS-RCMD (refractory cytopenia with multilineage dysplasia): dysplasia ≥2 lineages; <5% myeloblasts.
- >20% myeloblasts = acute myeloid leukemia (WHO criterion); >30% erythroblasts required for erythroid leukemia.

**Secondary dysmyelopoiesis (non-clonal)**
- Most common causes in dogs: ITP, IMHA, pure red cell aplasia, lymphoma.
- Cats: IMHA, ITP, pure red cell aplasia, lymphoma, FeLV, FIV, glomerulonephritis, FIP.

**Treatment of pancytopenic disorders**
- Eliminate underlying cause where possible.
- Cytotoxic agents: cyclophosphamide, cytosine arabinoside, prednisone, cyclosporine, daunorubicin, vincristine, hydroxycarbamide, cytarabine.
- Supportive: transfusion, growth factors (EPO, G-CSF).

**Infectious disorders causing leukocyte abnormalities**
- Parvovirus: neutropenia in some but not all; leukopenia in <50% on presentation; 92% dogs and 84% cats had acellular bone marrow; survival predicted by total leukocyte count >4500/mcL and lymphocyte count >1000/mcL.
- Ehrlichia canis (acute): neutropenia/leukopenia; morulae in lymphocytes, monocytes, macrophages; chronic = panhypocellularity.
- A. phagocytophilum: lymphopenia, neutropenia, eosinopenia, monocytosis; morulae in peripheral neutrophils.
- FeLV: neutropenia in ~50% of cats with FeLV-related illness.

### Ch 177 — Immune-Mediated Polyarthritis and Other Polyarthropathies
*(Stone M)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — IMPA)

**Classification of polyarthritis (Box 177.1)**
- Infectious: rickettsial, bacterial, Mycoplasma, L-form bacteria, fungi, viruses (cats).
- Reactive (secondary to distant immunogenic stimulus): non-joint infectious focus; non-joint noninfectious inflammatory focus; drugs; distant neoplasia; post-vaccinal.
- Primary immune-mediated polyarthropathy:
  - Non-erosive: SLE; breed-associated (Shar-Pei, Akita); idiopathic IMPA.
  - Erosive: breed-associated (Greyhound, Border Collie); idiopathic (rheumatoid arthritis — primarily bone lysis; periosteal proliferative polyarthritis in cats).

**Typical features of idiopathic IMPA**
- Multiple joints, especially carpi and tarsi.
- Fever of unknown origin.
- Neutrophilic inflammation in synovial fluid (>10% neutrophils).
- Neutrophilic polyarthritis that is culture-negative.

**Signalment — idiopathic IMPA**
- Age 3–7 years most common (range 6 months to 12 years).
- No sex predilection; all breeds.
- Rare in cats (young to middle-aged).

**Breed-associated syndromes**
- Shar-Pei (familial Shar-Pei fever/FSF): 12–48 h recurrent bouts of fever + hock inflammation (swollen hock syndrome); more frequent in early years, decrease with age; HA overexpressed in skin; colchicine 0.025–0.03 mg/kg (maximum 0.6 mg/dog) PO q12h recommended to reduce AA amyloidosis risk.
- Akita: 8 puppies <8 months with severe polyarthropathy; traced to common ancestor; 2/8 responsive to IS therapy.

**Diagnostic workup**
- Tick-borne agents: try doxycycline/minocycline (response within 72 h → rickettsial); negative serology in acute disease does not exclude.
- Arthrocentesis: ≥4 joints (both carpi + both tarsi minimum); <0.2 mL common; slides sufficient.
- Synovial fluid cytology: mononuclear <10% neutrophils = degenerative; immune-mediated/infectious >10% neutrophils.
- Labs: mild nonregenerative anemia and mild leukocytosis common; thrombocytopenia → tick-borne or concurrent ITP; marked liver enzyme elevation → evaluate for hepatopathy as distant stimulus.
- ANA and RF may be evaluated; RF elevated in any inflammatory disease.
- Proteinuria: monitor; corticosteroids cause proteinuria (mean UPC from 0.29 ± 0.10 to max 1.27 ± 1.02 after 28 days of prednisone 2.2 mg/kg PO q12h).

**Treatment — dogs**
- Prednisone monotherapy: 1–2 mg/kg PO q24h until remission (resolution of clinical signs + lab changes over ≥2 weeks); then taper 50% every 4 weeks; minimum 4 months total.
- Combination prednisone + mycophenolate: prednisone 1 mg/kg PO q12–24h + **mycophenolate 10 mg/kg PO q12h**; taper prednisolone 25–50% every 2 weeks, maintain MMF; goal: discontinue GC, maintain on MMF alone; if remission on MMF for 2 months → reduce 50%; after further 2 months → discontinue.
- Relapse after MMF discontinuation → reinduction with short prednisone course; then MMF 10 mg/kg q12–24h for life.
- If MMF insufficient → leflunomide 2–3 mg/kg PO q24h or cyclosporine 5 mg/kg q12h with GC; taper to monotherapy.
- NSAID washout 7 days before starting corticosteroids (avoid concurrent use — high GI ulceration risk).

**Treatment — cats**
- Some cats require alternate corticosteroid (prednisolone, methylprednisolone, triamcinolone, dexamethasone).
- Chlorambucil: 15 mg/m2 PO q24h (often 4 mg/cat/day) × 4 days, repeated every 3 weeks; OR 2 mg total per cat every 2–3 days.
- Taper chlorambucil before prednisolone.
- Methotrexate for erosive disease: 7.5 mg PO once weekly + leflunomide 10 mg PO q24h → improve in 7/12 cats; maintenance 2.5 mg methotrexate/week + 10 mg leflunomide every 3–4 days.

**Prognosis**
- 90% show rapid response to IS therapy.
- 10% fail — progressive joint damage.
- Many relapse; some require lifelong IS therapy.

**Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)**
- Rare; erosive joint disease; average age at diagnosis 5 years in dogs; ~1/3 have systemic signs.
- RF present in ~70% dogs with clinically diagnosed RA.
- Prognosis: guarded; relentlessly progressive; most patients experience joint deterioration.
- Feline RA: very rare; two forms — primarily bone lysis (RA) and periosteal proliferative.

**Septic arthritis features**
- Single large joint (stifle, hip, shoulder, elbow) most common.
- Prior surgery greatly increases likelihood of bacterial etiology.
- Blood culture medium for synovial fluid before submission (increases sensitivity).

### Ch 178 — Immune-Mediated Dermatologic Diseases
*(Bizikova P)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — pemphigus foliaceus, immune-mediated skin diseases)

**Pemphigus Foliaceus (PF)** — most common autoimmune skin disease dogs and cats
- Autoantibodies disrupt desmosomal adhesion (desmocollin-1 in dogs; target unknown in cats) → acantholysis → subcorneal pustules.
- Median age 5–6 years.
- Distribution: nasal planum, dorsal muzzle, periocular, concave pinnae, pawpads (dogs); nipple area and claw folds frequently affected in cats.
- Systemic signs (lethargy, anorexia, fever) in up to 50%.
- Insecticide-triggered PF: Promeris Duo, Certifect, Vectra 3D; begins at application site.
- Histopathology: neutrophilic (or mixed neutrophilic-eosinophilic) subcorneal pustules with acantholysis.
- Diagnosis: skin lesion type/distribution + exclusion of infectious causes + supportive cytology/histopathology.
- Biopsy: entire pustule or active erosion margin.

**Pemphigus Vulgaris (PV)** — rare
- Antibodies against desmoglein 3 → deep suprabasal acantholysis → vesicles → deep erosions and ulcers.
- Affects mucosae AND haired skin; systemic signs common.

**Paraneoplastic Pemphigus (PNP)** — very rare
- Resembles PV + erythema multiforme (suprabasal acantholysis + single-cell apoptosis).
- Acute, rapidly progressive.
- Management: identify and eliminate primary neoplasm.

**Autoimmune Subepidermal Blistering Skin Diseases (AISBDs)**
- Three main: mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP), epidermolysis bullosa acquisita (EBA), bullous pemphigoid (BP).
- Autoimmune attack on dermo-epidermal junction → dermo-epidermal separation → tense vesicles/bullae → deep erosions/ulcers.
- EBA: acute onset; mucosae AND haired skin; pawpad sloughing; systemic signs; Great Danes overrepresented; often in young dogs.
- BP: older adult dogs; haired skin ± mild mucosal lesions; usually no systemic signs.
- MMP: exclusively mucosae/mucocutaneous junctions; usually no systemic signs.
- Definitive AISBD subtyping requires IF techniques (not widely available in veterinary medicine).
- EBA treatment: colchicine (drug of choice; not with oral cyclosporine).

**Erythema Multiforme (EM), SJS, TEN**
- EM minor: epidermal detachment <10% BSA; 0–1 mucosa; usually no systemic signs.
- EM major: epidermal detachment <10% BSA; >1 mucosa; often systemically ill.
- SJS: erythroderma >50% BSA; epidermal detachment <10%; mucosal involvement variable.
- TEN: epidermal detachment >30% BSA.
- SJS/TEN overlap: 10–30% epidermal detachment.
- Most common drug triggers: NSAIDs (carprofen), antibiotics (sulfonamides, amoxicillin, cephalosporins, penicillin), phenobarbital.
- SJS/TEN: dermatological emergency; high mortality from sepsis, AKI, hypoalbuminemia, hypothermia.

**Uveodermatological Syndrome (UDS)**
- Resembles Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada syndrome in humans.
- Lymphocyte-mediated attack of melanocytes → cutaneous/mucosal depigmentation ± leukotrichia.
- Uveitis frequently precedes skin lesions.
- Predisposed breeds: Akita, Samoyed, Siberian Husky; males 2× more commonly affected.

**Symmetric Lupoid Onychitis (SLO)**
- Lymphocyte-mediated attack on nail bed epithelium.
- Nail pain, sloughing → regrown nails short, misshapen, dry, brittle, crumbly.
- Predisposed: Gordon Setters, German Shepherds.
- Majority of nails affected (useful clinical clue).

**Juvenile Cellulitis (JC)**
- Puppies 3 weeks to 5 months (occasionally adults); unknown etiology.
- Predisposed: Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers, Dachshunds, Gordon Setters, Cocker Spaniels, Goldendoodles, Labradoodles.
- Prodromal: fever, lethargy, depression, anorexia, severe submandibular lymphadenopathy.
- Treatment: predniso(lo)ne 2 mg/kg/day for 2–4 weeks sufficient.

**Treatment principles for immune-mediated skin diseases**
- Systemic immunosuppression cornerstone.
- Initial phase: glucocorticoids at immunosuppressive doses ± non-steroidal IS (azathioprine, MMF, CsA, colchicine, doxycycline/niacinamide).
- Taper GC first (25–50% every 2–4 weeks); goal transition to non-steroidal within 3 months.
- PF/PV: high-dose GC pulse therapy for initial phase (non-genomic effects on keratinocyte adhesion).
- MMP, BP, facial DLE, MCLE maintenance: doxycycline 2–5 mg/kg PO q12–24h + niacinamide (250 mg if <10 kg; 500 mg if >10 kg) ± topical GC/tacrolimus.
- CsA effective for: facial and generalized DLE, MCLE, VCLE, EM, SJS, TEN, alopecia areata, pseudopelade, sebaceous adenitis, non-thymoma exfoliative dermatitis, UDS. NOT with colchicine (interaction).
- High-dose glucocorticoid pulse therapy not to be confused with standard dosing.

### Ch 179 — Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
*(Banovic F)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — SLE)

**Pathogenesis**
- Imbalance between cellular waste disposal (apoptotic material) and clearance → innate inflammatory response via nucleic acid recognition receptors → type I interferon (IFN) → B-cell differentiation, loss of tolerance, autoantibody production.
- Key pathways: lymphocyte signaling; IFN signaling (nucleic acid sensing); clearance of immune complexes.
- Canine SLE: genetic predisposition; linked to DLA A7 allele and decreased serum IgA in one colony.
- NSDTRs (Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retrievers): SLE-related IMRD (GWAS-identified 11 gene regions, mostly T-cell activation).

**Environmental/drug triggers**
- UV light: activates keratinocytes, increases apoptosis, upregulates type I IFN → UV-induced flares in dogs.
- Propylthiouracil-induced ANA + IMHA + thrombocytopenia (cats).
- Hydralazine: ANA induction in dogs.
- FeLV/FIV: SLE-like disease in cats; serum ANA can occur early in FeLV infection.

**Clinical signs (Table 179.1)**
- Dogs (n=302): nonerosive polyarthritis 78%; fever 68%; renal disorders 55%; dermatologic lesions 46%; lymphadenopathy/splenomegaly 38%; leukopenia 18%; hemolytic anemia 15%; thrombocytopenia 13%; myositis 6%; CNS disorders 5%; neuropathy 2%.
- Cats (n=25): dermatologic lesions 60%; fever 52%; renal disorders 40%; nonerosive polyarthritis 36%; hemolytic anemia 24%; CNS disorders 24%.

**Diagnostic criteria for SLE (Table 179.2 — veterinary adaptation of human criteria)**
Requires ≥2 separate manifestations of multisystemic autoimmune disease + positive ANA titer, without evidence of infectious disease/drugs/vaccines:
1. ANA — abnormal titer (absence of drugs/infection/neoplasia known to cause positive ANA).
2. Mucocutaneous lesions — depigmentation, erythema, erosions, ulcerations, crusts/scaling including oral ulcers, with biopsy consistent with SLE.
3. Arthritis — nonerosive, nonseptic, ≥2 peripheral joints.
4. Renal disorders — glomerulonephritis or persistent proteinuria (no UTI).
5. Anemia and/or thrombocytopenia — IMHA/ITP (no offending drugs).
6. Leukopenia — low total WBC.
7. Polymyositis or myocarditis.
8. Serositis — nonseptic cavity effusion (abdominal, pleural, pericardial).
9. Neurologic disorders — seizures (absence of other cause).
10. Antiphospholipid antibodies — prolonged aPTT not correcting with 1:1 mixing (no heparin/FDPs).

**ANA testing**
- Substrates: mouse/rat liver, HeLa, Vero, or HEp-2 cells.
- ANA can be positive in healthy dogs (low prevalence) and dogs with chronic inflammatory, infectious (tick-borne, Bartonella), neoplastic, or immune-mediated diseases other than SLE.
- ANA-negative SLE cases exist.
- No universally accepted cutoff or protocol in veterinary medicine.
- ANA against anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm: clinically valuable in human SLE; variable results in canine SLE.

**Exclusion of mimics**
- Leishmaniasis: fever, lymphadenopathy, skin disease, polyarthritis, IMHA/ITP, PLN, high ANA titers.
- Tick-borne diseases: doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q24h up to 1 week before concluding immune-mediated disease.
- Cats: FeLV and FIV testing.

**Treatment**
- Sun avoidance (UV-aggravated).
- Prednisone/prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day (dogs); prednisolone or dexamethasone preferred (cats).
- Adjunct IS (Ch 169):
  - **Azathioprine (dogs only): 2.2 mg/kg PO** — hepatotoxic, NOT in cats.
  - **Cyclosporine (dogs and cats): 5 mg/kg PO q12–24h**.
  - **Mycophenolate mofetil (dogs and cats): 10–20 mg/kg IV or PO q12–24h**.
  - **Chlorambucil (cats): 2 mg PO every 48–72h**.
- Levamisole: 2–5 mg/kg (max 150 mg) PO every 48 h (one controlled study showing sustained remission after prednisone discontinuation vs. prednisone alone); serious side-effects (agranulocytosis, excited behavior, vomiting, bradycardia) — use with caution.
- Hydroxychloroquine (5–10 mg/kg PO q24h): successfully used for canine CLE; warrants further investigation for SLE.
- Oclacitinib: reversed canine CLE lesions (0.6 mg/kg PO q12h); not yet used for SLE.
- Oral JAK inhibitors: under investigation for human SLE.
- Full doses until complete remission (resolution of all clinicopathological abnormalities) → slow taper.
- Monitor: CBC, biochemistry, urinalysis, serum ANA every 1–3 months until resolution; then as needed.
- Renal function: monitor for life.

### Ch 180 — Immunodeficiencies
*(Fogle J)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — primary immunodeficiencies)

**Clinical features suggesting immunodeficiency**
- Infections with normally non-pathogenic organisms.
- Unusually severe/persistent infections.
- Inordinate number of different infections.
- Recurrence of infections that were subclinical earlier in life.
- Infections difficult to treat.
- Repeated infection with pyogenic bacteria → phagocytic/complement/antibody defect.
- Recurrent viral infections → adaptive immunity defect.
- Recurrent fungal infections → T-cell ± antibody defect.

**Innate immune system defects**

*Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency (LAD)*
- LAD1: CD18 deficiency; ITGB2 gene mutation; autosomal recessive.
  - Irish Setters (missense ITGB2 mutation); mixed breed dogs; Domestic Shorthair cat (different ITGB2 mutation).
  - Die within first or second year of life from severe infection.
  - Diagnosis: flow cytometry showing absent/reduced neutrophil CD18/CD11b; genetic test available for Irish Setters.
- LAD3: FERMT3 (KINDLIN3) gene defect; normal CD18/CD11b expression; poor beta-1,2,3 integrin function.
  - German Shepherd Dog and mixed breed; recurrent infections + bleeding from thrombocytopathia.

*Cyclical Neutropenia (Gray Collie Syndrome)*
- AP3B1 gene mutation (autosomal recessive) → defective lysosomal membrane protein trafficking.
- Gray haircoat, reduced pigmentation (melanocyte dysfunction).
- Cyclical neutropenia + thrombocytopenia every 10–14 days.
- Defective neutrophil elastase and myeloperoxidase function.
- Clinical signs post-waning maternal antibody; recurrent respiratory/GI infections, gingivitis, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy.
- Rarely live past 3 years.
- Treatment: recombinant G-CSF (resolves neutropenic episodes; not curative).

*Canine Trapped Neutrophil Syndrome*
- VPS13B gene mutations (autosomal recessive) in Border Collies.
- Profound neutrophil accumulation in bone marrow; very few in peripheral circulation.
- May have craniofacial developmental abnormalities, stunted growth.
- Rarely live past 6–12 months.

*Chédiak-Higashi Syndrome*
- LYST gene defect; autosomal recessive; blue smoke Persian cats.
- Abnormally large secretory granules; poor neutrophil chemotaxis and killing; thrombocyte dysfunction.
- G-CSF therapy helps.

*Pelger-Huët (PH) Anomaly*
- Inherited neutrophil defect; hyposegmented nuclei (mistaken for bands).
- Usually subclinical neutrophil dysfunction.
- Dogs and cats (many breeds including Australian Shepherd, Basenji, Foxhound, Cocker Spaniel).

*Complement C3 Deficiency*
- Autosomal recessive in Brittany Spaniels.
- Poor opsonization → impaired phagocytosis; bacterial infections (pneumonia, sepsis, pyometra).

**Adaptive immune system defects**

*IgA Deficiency*
- Most commonly reported immune deficiency in dogs; most difficult to definitively diagnose.
- Breeds: Shar-Peis, Beagles, Cocker Spaniels, Dalmatians, Akitas, German Shepherds, Irish Wolfhounds, Rottweilers, Weimaraners, Dachshunds, Bull Terriers.
- Clinical signs: infections at mucosal surfaces and skin (pyoderma, otitis, demodicosis, bronchitis, pneumonia, GI dysbiosis).
- Diagnosis: measure BOTH serum IgA AND mucosal IgA (feces or saliva) at ≥2 time points.
- Decreased serum only: suspicious; decreased both at multiple time points: strongly indicative.
- Normal IgA concentration does not exclude diagnosis (IgA quality may differ).

*Common Variable Immunodeficiency (CVID)*
- Consistently documented in dogs with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia.
- Breeds: Miniature Dachshunds, Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, other purebreds and mixed breeds, Miniature Poodles, Pomeranians.
- Impaired T-cell function + decreased serum IgG and IgA; most also have low IgM (exception: CKCSs can have increased IgM).

*T-Cell Deficiency*
- Thymic aplasia in Birman kittens; FOXN1 gene loss-of-function mutation → hairless with thymic aplasia ("nude" syndrome equivalent).

*SCID (Autosomal Recessive) — Jack Russell Terriers*
- DNA-PKcs defect → prevents V(D)J recombination → complete lack of functional TCR and BCR development.
- Severe lymphopenia, profound lymphoid tissue hypoplasia, agammaglobulinemia, death.

*X-linked SCID*
- Basset Hounds (deletional mutation) and Cardigan Welsh Corgis (insertional mutation): dysfunctional IL-2 receptor gamma.
- Without IL-2Rγ → T-cell maturation defective; B-cell class switching impaired → marked lymphoid hypoplasia.
- Clinical signs post-waning maternal antibody; death from opportunistic infection.

### Ch 181 — Vasculitides
*(Swann J)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Vasculitis (DIS-BD-VASC) — enrichment below. ✓ CRGV/Alabama Rot — specific details.

**Classification and pathophysiology**
- Vasculitis = leukocytic infiltration of vessel wall → endothelial/smooth muscle necrosis.
- Fibrinoid change (eosinophilic hyaline material in vessel wall) = diagnostic marker.
- Leukocytoclastic vasculitis: nuclear fragmentation of neutrophils in vessel wall (apoptosis/necroptosis) — not associated with specific clinical phenotype.
- Neutrophilic infiltrates most common in dermis; less commonly lymphoplasmacytic, eosinophilic, granulomatous.

**Causes**
- ~75% of dogs with any vasculitis and ~50% with cutaneous vasculitis have a concurrent causative problem.
- Primary IMV syndromes are rare; most common = idiopathic cutaneous vasculitis (small dermal vessels).
- ANCA (anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibodies) detected in Beagles with polyarteritis in research settings; also elevated non-specifically in dogs with inflammatory bowel disease and IMHA.
- Concurrent diseases/exposures (Table 181.1): Leishmania spp., R. rickettsii, B. gibsoni, Bartonella henselae, Toxoplasma gondii, D. immitis, A. vasorum, FIP; lymphoproliferative disease, SLE, IMHA, reactive histiocytosis; itraconazole, sulfonamides, NSAIDs (meloxicam, carprofen, ibuprofen), human albumin infusion, rabies vaccination, ivermectin (oral), rattlesnake envenomation; grapes/raisins.

**Breed-specific vasculitis syndromes**
- Jack Russell Terriers: idiopathic cutaneous vasculitis.
- German Shepherd Dogs: familial cutaneous vasculopathy.
- Juvenile Chinese Shar-Peis: acute febrile neutrophilic vasculitis.
- Steroid-responsive meningitis arteritis (SRMA): Ch 242.
- Proliferative arteritis of nasal philtrum: Giant Schnauzers, Saint Bernards.
- Nasal planum vasculitis: Scottish Terriers.

**CRGV (Cutaneous and Renal Glomerular Vasculopathy)**
- First described in US Greyhounds (1980s); emerging UK disease.
- Microscopic intravascular thrombi in dermal and glomerular vessels + inflammatory damage.
- Initiating event possibly vasculitis disrupting anti-thrombotic endothelium; cause (toxin? pathogen?) unidentified.
- Clinical presentation: one or more cutaneous ulcerated lesions on limbs → rapid onset azotemia and oligoanuric AKI within 48 h.

**Clinical signs of vasculitis**
- Edematous swelling: erythematous/warm (unlike edema from oncotic pressure changes) — localized peripherally or intracavitary effusions.
- Purpura: does NOT blanch on diascopy.
- Well-demarcated painful ulceration: ischemia/infarction in poorly vascularized regions (pinna tips, nasal planum, digital pads, tail, flanks/limbs, oral mucosa).
- ANCA-associated systemic vasculitis: anemia + thrombocytopenia (concurrent antibody responses or microvascular damage).
- Systemic signs: fever, lethargy, decreased appetite, acute phase response.

**Diagnosis**
- Definitive: histopathological evaluation only; lesions change rapidly (timing of biopsy critical).
- Complete workup: drug/toxin exposure history, infectious disease testing, thoracic/abdominal imaging.
- Diet trial (novel protein or hypoallergenic) for cutaneous vasculitis (possible dietary hypersensitivity).

**Treatment**
- Directed at underlying cause first.
- Idiopathic IMV: prednisone/prednisolone immunosuppressive starting dose — **dogs: 2 mg/kg or 40 mg/m2; cats: 4 mg/kg** (Ch 144).
- Additional IS (azathioprine, cyclosporine, chlorambucil, MMF) if GC insufficient or to limit side-effects.
- **Pentoxifylline: 15 mg/kg PO q8–12h** (dogs and cats) — decreases leukocyte chemotaxis/degranulation, inhibits TNF-α, decreases blood viscosity. No published evidence of efficacy for cutaneous/systemic vasculitis.
- Oxytetracycline + niacinamide (Ch 178): purported immunomodulatory effects; no published evidence.

**Prognosis**
- Infectious vasculitis: resolves with appropriate treatment.
- Immune-complex vasculitis in immune-mediated diseases: often resolves with IS.
- Idiopathic cutaneous vasculitis: may be cured or managed.
- Systemic IMV or CNS vasculitis: usually progressive and terminal.

### Ch 182 — Diseases of the Spleen
*(Donovan TA, Slade DJ)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — splenic disease / masses)

**Splenic functions**
- Immunological response to blood-borne antigens.
- Removal of aged/defective blood cells.
- Blood filtration (foreign particles, infectious agents).
- Blood reservoir (dog stores ~1/3 blood volume during sleep).
- Hematopoiesis (fetus and some adult disease states).
- Post-splenectomy: increased susceptibility to hemotropic mycoplasmosis, eperythrozoonosis, babesiosis.

**Prevalence of splenic lesions**
- 1–3% of canine surgical accessions; benign nodular hyperplasia most common (19–24%); up to 48% in one radiation-exposed colony.
- "Double two-thirds rule" (2/3 of splenomegaly cases = neoplasia; 2/3 of splenic neoplasia = HSA) — subject to considerable refinement.
- HSA prevalence in splenic masses: 9–74% depending on study design.
- Dogs without hemoperitoneum: benign in 70%.
- Dogs with hemoperitoneum: malignancy in 68–87%.
- Non-traumatic splenic rupture: malignancy in 67–76% of cases.

**Factors associated with malignancy**
- Hemoperitoneum, older age, larger breed/body weight, lower splenic mass to volume ratio, presence of thoracic metastasis, anemia, thrombocytopenia.
- Dogs <27.8 kg significantly less likely to have HSA.
- Miniature Schnauzers, Dachshunds, Beagles: overrepresented in small breed nodular lesions.

**Clinical signs**
- Non-specific: lethargy, inappetence, vomiting, anorexia, weight loss, weakness/collapse.
- Active bleeding: pallor, tachycardia, tachypnea, weak pulses, abdominal fluid wave.
- Cardiac arrhythmias.
- Polyuria/polydipsia (acute splenic disease — mechanism unknown; possibly psychogenic or splenic stretch receptors).

**Clinicopathology**
- No pathognomonic findings.
- Anemia: non-regenerative (anemia of inflammation) or regenerative (blood loss, IMHA).
- Nucleated RBCs: can appear with altered splenic function.
- Howell-Jolly bodies: decreased splenic function or post-splenectomy.
- RBC morphologies with splenic disease: acanthocytes, elliptocytes, schistocytes, spherocytes.
- Post-splenectomy: transient thrombocytosis and leukocytosis (weeks); absent physiologic erythrocytosis from splenic contraction.

**Imaging**
- Ultrasound: modality of choice; describes echotexture, size, contour, blood flow; few pathognomonic patterns (cytology/biopsy required).
- Normal cat spleen: mean thickness 8.2 mm (SD 1.4 mm); width 26.7 mm (SD 4.4 mm) on transverse images.
- Splenic torsion on ultrasound: markedly enlarged, mottled/lacy parenchyma, abnormal position; hyperechoic hilar perivenous triangle highly associated with torsion.
- CT: best for dogs >25 kg; contrast studies help differentiate malignant from benign; "whirl sign" + strongly hyperattenuating center (pre-contrast) in torsion.

**Fine-needle aspiration (cytology)**
- Helpful for: metastatic neoplasia, diffuse lymphoma, extramedullary hematopoiesis.
- Negative predictive value: 51% — poor; significant false-negative rate.
- Positive predictive value for neoplasia: 97%.
- Aspiration of blood-filled/cystic structures: low yield.
- Generally safe; lower hemorrhage risk than liver aspiration (capsule present).
- Non-aspiration (pin-cushion) technique may reduce blood contamination.
- Contraindication: significant thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopathia, recent thromboprophylactics.

**Histopathology / biopsy**
- Total splenectomy preferred over partial or needle core biopsy.
- Sample 5–10 wedge sections from junction of mass and surrounding spleen (viable tissue); avoid necrotic center.
- Sample firm, white-to-tan tissue; avoid friable red tissue.

**Causes of splenomegaly (Table 182.3)**
- Uniform (congested): barbiturate anesthesia/euthanasia, GDV, splenic torsion, acute hemolytic anemia (includes IMHA), acute septicemia.
- Uniform (meaty): infiltrative neoplasia (lymphoma, histiocytic sarcoma, MCT, leukemia, myeloproliferative disease), diffuse lymphoid hyperplasia, splenitis, chronic hemolytic anemia, EMH, amyloid/lysosomal storage diseases.
- Nodular (hemorrhagic): HSA, infarction (acute), hematoma, nodular hyperplasia.
- Nodular (non-hemorrhagic): neoplasia, infarction (subacute-chronic), splenitis, nodular hyperplasia.

**Differential for splenic lesions by ultrasound echogenicity**
- Hyperechoic nodular: myelolipomas (strongly hyperechoic, non-cavitated), calcified hematomas; also benign nodular hyperplasia, EMH, granuloma, hematoma, infection, neoplasia.
- Hypoechoic nodular/Swiss-cheese pattern: lymphoma, histiocytic sarcoma, EMH, infarct.
- Mixed echogenicity "target-like": metastasis (primary consideration); also benign nodular hyperplasia, EMH, hematoma, infection, neoplasia.
- Anechoic: abscess, splenic pseudocysts, cyst.
- Masses with heterogeneous echotexture + anechoic cavities: hematoma, HSA; also solid forms of HSA and other neoplasms.

**Benign splenic nodular hyperplasia**
- Four types: lymphoid, stromal, hematopoietic, complex; no difference in biological behavior.
- Lymphoid nodular hyperplasia most common; must be differentiated from marginal zone lymphoma and mantle cell lymphoma.
- Splenic myelolipomas: strongly hyperechoic; benign; adipocytes + hematopoietic cells; uncertain process (neoplastic, metaplastic, or hamartomatous).

**Splenic infarction**
- Associated with: neoplasia, corticosteroid excess, immune-mediated disorders, endocrinopathy (DM), coagulopathies, splenic hematoma, cardiac/liver/renal disease, sepsis, vasculitis.
- Splenic vein thrombosis: most common disease of the splenic vein; neoplasia most common cause (lymphoma most common neoplasm).
- Dogs with splenic infarction: may have higher post-operative mortality.

**Splenic torsion**
- Breeds: German Shepherd Dogs, Great Danes, English Bulldogs.
- Previous GDV episodes proposed as risk factor (controversial — one large study showed no association with subsequent GDV after splenectomy).
- Severe thrombocytopenia (21–23 k/mcL) described in 2 dogs with splenic torsion — resolved post-splenectomy.
- Treatment: surgical splenectomy; favorable short- and long-term prognosis.

**Splenitis causes (Box 182.1)**
- Viral: FIV, FIP, FeLV, systemic calicivirus (cats); infectious canine hepatitis (dogs).
- Rickettsial/Mycoplasma: ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis, hemotropic mycoplasmosis, Q fever, RMSF.
- Bacterial: bacteremia, bartonellosis, brucellosis, Lyme, melioidosis, salmonellosis, tularemia, mycobacteria, nocardiosis, yersinia.
- Fungal: blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, sporotrichosis; opportunistic infections.
- Protozoal: babesiosis, cytauxzoonosis (cats), hepatozoonosis, leishmaniasis, toxoplasmosis, trypanosomiasis.

**Hypersplenism**
- Definition: decreased RBCs/WBCs/platelets in blood + histologic features of diffuse hematopoiesis (myeloid metaplasia), vascular thrombosis with splenic infarction, reactive/proliferative cellular reticular meshwork with excess macrophages.
- Erythrophagocytosis present in 92% of 65 cases.
- Coombs testing positive in only 21% of tested dogs.
- 70% of dogs died in 1-year post-operative interval.
- Giant cells on histology: indicative of poor prognosis.
- Treatment: splenectomy (most common) when secondary cause cannot be treated.

**PBLEB (Polyclonal B-cell Lymphocytosis in English Bulldogs)**
- Newly recognized syndrome; potentially pre-malignant.
- 84 cases: CD21+ lymphocytosis; 70% polyclonal/restricted polyclonal Ig gene rearrangements; splenomegaly/splenic masses 57%; hyperglobulinemia 65%; median age 6.8 years; males overrepresented 74%.

**Extramedullary Hematopoiesis (EMH)**
- Low-level myeloid, erythroid, platelet precursors normal in spleen.
- Clinical significance: inflammatory disease, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, hemorrhage, myeloproliferative/lymphoproliferative disorders, chemotherapy recovery, neoplasia.

**Splenosystemic shunts (cats)**
- Splenosystemic shunts arising from splenic vein to renal vein/caudal vena cava.
- 90% single shunts; 91% spayed females.
- 42% of 33 cats had hepatopathy.
- "Spaghetti sign" on radiographs: more evident on VD projections.

**Hemangiosarcoma (splenic)**
- Most common splenic malignancy in dogs; discussed in detail in Ch 325.
- Hemoabdomen associated with HSA in 68–87% of acute non-traumatic hemoperitoneum.
- HSA prevalence in hematoma formation: lymphoid nodules present in 27% of hematomas vs. 0% in 120 HSA cases — distinguishing feature.
