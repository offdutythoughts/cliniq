### Ch 183 — Does This Dog or Cat Have an Infectious Disease?

**Clinical reasoning scaffold** (problem-oriented approach):
- Thrombocytopenia: decreased production (ehrlichiosis, FeLV), sequestration (vasculitis, splenomegaly), consumption (DIC), immune-mediated destruction (rickettsioses, ehrlichia, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Bartonella, leishmaniosis).
- Lymphocytosis in dogs: signals ehrlichiosis (Ch 193), babesiosis, hepatozoonosis, leishmaniosis, trypanosomiasis, A. platys. Monoclonal or polyclonal hyperglobulinemia + lymphocytosis → rule out ehrlichiosis before lymphoid neoplasia.
- Hyperglobulinemia (polyclonal): ehrlichiosis, babesiosis, leishmaniosis, trypanosomiasis, schistosomiasis, FIV, FIP, bartonellosis, fungal infections.
- Hyponatremia in dogs: associated with RMSF (Ch 193) and leptospirosis (Ch 192) via vasculitis/ADH release.
- Pancytopenia: parvovirus, panleukopenia, ehrlichiosis, histoplasmosis, leishmaniosis.
- Hypercalcemia + granulomatous disease: aspergillosis, coccidioidomycosis, histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, pythiosis, schistosomiasis (Heterobilharzia), nocardiosis (cats), mycobacterial infections.
- "Immune panels" (ANA, RF, pANCA): can be positive in vector-borne infections — of limited value before infection ruled out.

**Diagnostic testing principles:**
- PCR (direct): best in acute phase; negative does not rule out infection.
- IgM antibody: detectable 5–7 days post-exposure; IgG follows.
- Paired serology (fourfold rise): required for definitive exposure confirmation in many diseases.
- Combining PCR + serology increases sensitivity for vector-borne pathogens (Figure 183.2).

### Ch 184 — Laboratory Diagnosis of Infectious Disease

**Key diagnostic methods:**
- Acid-fast stain: Ziehl-Neelsen (heated) or Kinyoun (cold/preferred). Mycobacteria = AFB positive; Nocardia = partially acid-fast; Actinomyces = acid-fast negative.
- Gram stain: 5% discrepancy rate vs culture; 58% of discrepancies are false negatives on Gram stain; 42% false positives.
- MALDI-TOFms: species-level identification in 15–20 minutes from colony; 95.3% agreement with phenotypic/biochemical methods; superior for mycoplasma differentiation.
- Blood culture volumes (pediatric reference for dogs/cats): <3.9 kg: 1 mL; 4–7.9 kg: 3 mL; 8–13.9 kg: 6 mL; 14–18.9 kg: 12 mL; 19–25.9 kg: 16 mL; 26–39.9 kg: 20 mL.
- Antigen assays available: Dirofilaria immitis, Cryptococcus, Blastomyces (serum + urine), Histoplasma (serum + urine), Aspergillus (serum + urine), FeLV. Giardia, Cryptosporidium antigen in feces.
- PCR: quantitative (qPCR) allows monitoring treatment response. Specimen integrity critical — collect before antimicrobials where possible.
- Brucella screening: RSAT → confirm positives with 2ME-RSAT or AGIDcpa (Ch 189).

### Ch 185 — Companion Animal Vaccinations

**Canine core vaccines** (CDV, CPV-2, CAV-2 MLV; rabies):
- Puppies: start 6–8 weeks, every 2–4 weeks until ≥16 weeks, then dose at 6 months. Single dose at ≥26 weeks for any missed primary window.
- Adults: one dose MLV sufficient for immunological protection if no prior vaccination.
- Revaccination: no more frequently than every 3 years for core MLV. Rabies: 1-year or 3-year licensed DOI per local law.
- Duration of immunity (DOI): CDV, CPV, CAV can be 9 years or longer; sometimes lifelong.

**Canine non-core vaccines:**
- Leptospirosis: 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart starting at 8 weeks; annual booster. Quadrivalent (Icterohaemorrhagiae, Canicola, Grippotyphosa, Pomona/Bratislava) preferred over bivalent in endemic areas.
- Bordetella/CPiV: IN or oral preferred over parenteral. Single IN/oral dose from 3–4 weeks (IN) or 8 weeks (oral). Annual booster.
- Lyme (Borrelia burgdorferi): 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart starting 9–12 weeks; annual booster before tick season. Multiple product types (bacterins, recombinant OspA subunit, chimeric crLyme). Controversial — does NOT reliably prevent Lyme nephritis.
- CIV: 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart from 6 weeks; annual booster. Licensed for H3N8 and/or H3N2.
- Leishmania infantum (CVL vaccine): follow manufacturer protocol; annual booster. Does not confer sterile immunity — combined with topical insecticide.

**Feline core vaccines** (FPV, FHV-1, FCV, rabies):
- FPV revaccination: triennial minimum. FHV-1 and FCV: triennial for low-risk cats; annually for high-risk cats.
- Adult cats new to vaccination: 2 doses even with MLV (limited response to FHV-1/FCV component).

**Feline non-core:**
- FeLV: WSAVA/ABCD = non-core; AAFP = core for <1 year old. Test antigen-negative before vaccination. Annual booster (high-risk) or every 2–3 years (at-risk).
- FIV vaccine: not available USA/Canada/Europe; available Australia/NZ. Cross-protection uncertain; vaccines cause positive serology.
- Chlamydia felis: 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart from 8–9 weeks; annual booster.
- Bordetella: single IN dose from 4 weeks; annual booster.

**NOT recommended:** Feline FIP vaccine, CCoV vaccine (dogs), Giardia vaccine (dogs), dermatophytosis vaccines.

**FISS (feline injection-site sarcoma):** Avoid interscapular region. Use lateral abdomen, distal limbs, or tail. Non-adjuvanted/recombinant vaccines preferred.

**Serologic testing:** Point-of-care kits for CDV, CPV, CAV (dogs); FPV (cats). Seropositivity correlates with protection for core vaccines; absence does not guarantee susceptibility. Retest annually in senior animals.

### Ch 186 — Hospital-Associated (Nosocomial) Infections

- HAIs occur in all veterinary facilities; 82% of teaching hospitals reported outbreaks within 5 years.
- Main nosocomial pathogens: methicillin-resistant Staphylococci (MRSA, MRSP), Salmonella, CIV, CaPiV, canine papillomavirus, dermatophytes.
- Important syndromes: SSIs, invasive device infections, respiratory disease, diarrhea, opportunistic infections.
- Zoonotic risk to staff documented; multidrug-resistant bacteria (MRSA, ESBL Enterobacterales) are increasing concerns.
- Surveillance: passive (routine records, antibiograms) preferred; active screening for targeted high-risk scenarios. Syndromic surveillance (cough, fever, diarrhea) aids early detection.
- C. difficile spores: alcohol-based hand sanitizer insufficient — use soap and water. Bleach 1:10 (after removing organic material) for C. difficile outbreak control.
- Environmental culture: not routinely recommended; reserve for specific outbreak investigations with clear action plan.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — Nosocomial infections as a topic; content is framework-level, not disease-specific.

### Ch 187 — Lyme Disease

**Organism / Vector / Geography:**
- Causative: Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto (North America), Bb sensu lato (Eurasia).
- Vector: Ixodes spp. ticks (I. scapularis, I. pacificus in North America; I. ricinus in Europe).
- Transmission: requires ≥53 hours tick attachment (time for OspA downregulation). Transmission possible from larvae → nymphs → adults transstadially; not transovarial.
- Geography: Primarily NE, mid-Atlantic, upper Midwest USA; expanding via migratory birds. 70–90% seroprevalence in healthy dogs in some endemic areas.
- Coinfection vectors: same Ixodes ticks carry A. phagocytophilum, Borrelia miyamotoi, Babesia microti, Bartonella spp., Powassan virus.

**Clinical signs (dogs):**
- Lyme arthritis (LA): <5% of Lyme(+) dogs; oligo/polyarthritis, hot/painful joints, fever, hyporexia; signs 2–5 months after bite; self-limiting 4 days in young puppies, minimal in adults. Rarely: cutaneous rash, neurologic, cardiac.
- Lyme nephritis (LN): <2% of Lyme(+) dogs. ICGN with PLN. Breeds at higher risk: Labrador and Golden Retrievers. Signs: proteinuria → hypoalbuminemia → thromboembolism, edema, uremia. No single early serologic test predictive of LN risk.

**Diagnostics:**
- SNAP 4Dx Plus (IDEXX): qualitative C6 peptide antibody (natural exposure only; not vaccinal), plus heartworm antigen, E. canis/ewingii, A. phagocytophilum, A. platys antibodies.
- Lyme C6 Quant (IDEXX): quantitative. Post-treatment goal: ≥50% decline in C6 Quant level (originally >100) by 3–6 months. Qualitative C6 positive may persist years post-treatment — not an indication of active infection.
- AccuPlex4 (Antech): qualitative, may detect seroconversion ~1 week earlier for Lyme and Anaplasma vs SNAP 4Dx in one study; less repeatable/specific in other study.
- OspA antibodies: primarily vaccinates. OspC: rises 2–3 weeks post-natural exposure, declines 3–5 months later. OspF: rises 6–8 weeks, persists long-term, possibly wanes post-treatment.
- Whole-cell IFA/ELISA: cross-react with spirochetes and vaccine antigens; IgM/IgG titers unhelpful in dogs due to long incubation.
- PCR: limited utility — Bb does not circulate in blood. PCR on skin biopsy at tick site.

**Treatment:**
- Lyme arthritis: doxycycline 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h for 4 weeks (best protocol unknown); or minocycline. Clinical improvement in 1–2 days; if no response, reconsider diagnosis.
- Lyme nephritis: doxycycline for 3–6 months (or until C6 Quant waned); standard PLN treatment (renal diet, RAASi, omega-3 FA, antithrombotics, antihypertensives as needed). Immunosuppression (mycophenolate, pulse glucocorticoids, cyclosporine) if ICGN confirmed by biopsy.
- Nonclinical nonproteinuric dogs: treatment not recommended. Monitor proteinuria 3–4 times year 1, twice yearly thereafter.
- Carrier clearance: most cleared with 1 month antibiotics; 10–15% remain carriers despite full course.

**Tick prevention:** Products killing ticks early after attachment preferred: permethrin topicals, amitraz/pyrethroid collars, isoxazolines (NexGard, Simparica, Bravecto). Sarolaner: 68% kill at 8h, 98% at 12h, 99% at 24h.

### Ch 188 — Mycobacterial Infections, Actinomycosis, Nocardiosis

#### MYCOBACTERIAL INFECTIONS

**Organism categories:**
- Tuberculous (MTB complex): M. bovis (cat > dog; cattle reservoir; raw food diet risk), M. tuberculosis (dog; human source), M. microti (cat; vole reservoir in UK).
- Slowly growing mycobacteria (SGM): MAC (M. avium-intracellulare) — most common systemic SGM in cats/dogs. Miniature Schnauzer (CARD9 mutation), Basset Hound, Somali, Siamese, Abyssinian cats predisposed. Also M. kansasii, M. ulcerans (Buruli ulcer; Australia/Africa).
- Rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM): M. fortuitum, M. smegmatis, M. chelonae-abscessus — subcutaneous/cutaneous/panniculitis in cats, less in dogs; often immunocompetent, obese females.
- Leproid: M. lepraemurium (feline leprosy; global), Candidatus M. lepraefelis (Aus/NZ), Candidatus M. tarwinense (SE Australia); CLGS (Canine Leproid Granuloma Syndrome; Aus/Brazil) = short-haired large-breed dogs, head/pinnae nodules.

**Clinical signs:**
- Cats: SQ masses with draining tracts, lymphadenomegaly, systemic signs (respiratory, ocular, neurologic). RGM: panniculitis of ventral abdomen and inguinal area.
- Dogs: nonspecific (lethargy, anorexia, weight loss, GI, respiratory). SGM often rapidly fatal.

**Diagnosis:**
- Acid-fast staining of tissue: high numbers of organisms with MAC/SGM; RGM often no visible AFB on cytology.
- Feline IGRA (interferon-gamma release assay): commercially available for MTB complex in cats.
- Gold standard: 16S rRNA or hsp65 gene sequencing. MALDI-TOF increasingly available.
- SGM culture: 4–12 weeks; fastidious species cannot be cultured.

**Treatment (Table 188.2):**

*SGM in cats (3 months combination then 3–4 months dual):*
- Rifampicin 10–15 mg/kg PO q24h + fluoroquinolone (pradofloxacin 5 mg/kg, marbofloxacin 2.75–5.5 mg/kg, or moxifloxacin 10 mg/kg PO q24h) + clarithromycin 62.5 mg/cat PO q12h or azithromycin 10–15 mg/kg PO q24h.
- Continue 2 months beyond resolution of clinical and radiographic signs.

*SGM in dogs:*
- Rifampicin 10–15 mg/kg (max 600 mg/day) PO q24h + enrofloxacin 10 mg/kg IV/IM/SC/PO q24h + clarithromycin 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h or azithromycin 5–15 mg/kg PO q24h.
- Susceptibility testing required; dogs with systemic SGM: grave prognosis.

*RGM in cats:*
- Gentamicin 5–8 mg/kg IV/IM/SC q24h (perioperative) or amikacin 10 mg/kg + pradofloxacin 5 mg/kg PO q24h + clarithromycin 62.5 mg/cat PO q12h. Surgical debulking often required. High recurrence.

*CLGS (dogs):* Often spontaneous remission; refractory: surgical resection + long-term dual antimicrobials.

**Zoonosis:** M. tuberculosis and M. bovis transmit from pets to immunocompromised humans. Raw food diets linked to M. bovis clusters in cats.

#### ACTINOMYCOSIS

**Organism:** Facultatively anaerobic Gram-positive branching filamentous rods; non-acid-fast. Commensal of oral cavity; opportunistic after trauma/aspiration.

**Signalment:** Young male large-breed hunting/working dogs (pyothorax, abscess). Young male cats (pyothorax after bite wounds or aspiration).

**Clinical signs:** Dogs: pyothorax/pneumonia post-plant material penetration; SQ abscesses. Cats: pyothorax (most common), nasofacial, intra-abdominal, CNS infections. "Sulfur granules" in exudate.

**Diagnosis:** Non-acid-fast Gram-positive filamentous rods on cytology; 16S rRNA gene sequencing preferred for definitive ID.

**Treatment (Table 188.2):** Surgery + long-term (weeks to months) high-dose penicillins:
- Ampicillin 20–40 mg/kg IV/IM/SC q6–8h (dogs) or 20 mg/kg IV/IM/SC q8–12h (cats); or amoxicillin 22–30 mg/kg q8h.
- Alternative: clindamycin 10–15 mg/kg IV/PO q12h.
- Cats with pyothorax: drainage + antimicrobials (thoracotomy less often needed than in dogs).
- CNS actinomycosis: all reported cases fatal.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Actinomycosis)

#### NOCARDIOSIS

**Organism:** Gram-positive, variably partially acid-fast, aerobic saprophytic soil organism. Distinct from Actinomyces (which is commensal, not a soil organism).

**Signalment:** Immunosuppressed dogs (more commonly CNS/thoracic dissemination), cats (often cutaneous in immunocompetent; disseminated with immunosuppression).

**Clinical signs:** Cutaneous/SQ abscesses with draining tracts, mycetomas; thoracic disease (pneumonia, pyothorax); disseminated with CNS. Nocardia spp. isolated from osteomyelitis, cystitis, hepatitis cases.

**Diagnosis:** Gram-positive thin filamentous organisms; partially acid-fast (unreliable in direct specimens). Culture (extended incubation); MALDI-TOF or 16S rRNA sequencing for species ID.

**Treatment (Table 188.2):**
- First-line: Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole: dogs 15 mg/kg PO q12h; cats 30 mg/kg PO q24h.
- Amikacin: 10–15 mg/kg IV/IM/SC q24h (dogs); 10 mg/kg q24h (cats).
- Severe/disseminated: imipenem-cilastatin 5 mg/kg slow IV q8h ± cefotaxime 50 mg/kg IV q12h.
- Duration: 6–12 months based on culture/susceptibility testing.
- Prognosis: disseminated (especially CNS) = grave; cutaneous in medically immunosuppressed dogs may respond if immunosuppression discontinued.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Nocardiosis)

### Ch 189 — Brucellosis

**Organism / Transmission:**
- Brucella canis: Gram-negative aerobic coccobacillus with ROUGH colonial morphology (lacks "O" polysaccharide side chain of smooth Brucella spp.).
- Transmission: oronasal and venereal routes, blood/milk, transplacental. Infective oral dose: 10⁶ CFU; infective conjunctival dose: 10⁴ CFU.
- Bacteremia: develops 1–4 weeks post-infection; persists 6–64 months. Semen shedding highest weeks 3–11, decreases after week 12. Post-abortion vaginal discharge: highly infective ≥500 doses/mL, persists 4–6 weeks.
- Global distribution; highest prevalence (>20%) in Brazil, Asia, Eastern Europe. Southeastern USA 6–20%.

**Clinical signs:**
- Fever is UNCOMMON (rough LPS has low endotoxic activity).
- Most infected animals show no clinical signs.
- Reproductive: early embryonic death and resorption (infection ≤20 days post-conception); abortion at day 45–59 gestation; orchitis, epididymitis, scrotal edema/dermatitis, testicular atrophy.
- Non-reproductive: lymphadenomegaly, diskospondylitis, osteomyelitis, polyarthritis, meningoencephalitis, pyogranulomatous dermatitis, ocular lesions.

**Diagnostics:**
- Testing cascade required — no single test definitive.
- RSAT: sensitive screening from week 12 post-infection; NOT specific (cross-reacts with Staph, Strep, Bordetella, Pseudomonas, Moraxella, other Gram-negatives). Perform in-clinic.
- 2ME-RSAT: improved specificity over standard RSAT.
- LFIA: initial study showed sensitivity 98.6%, specificity 100%.
- Confirmatory tests after positive screen: AGID, ELISA (cytoplasmic antigens > cell wall antigens for specificity), or PCR.
- Culture: definitive but slow (7 days), biohazard risk (BSL-2); may lack sensitivity in chronic infection (organism in lymph nodes/spleen/reproductive tissue after 4–5 years).
- PCR: on blood, semen, vaginal swabs, urine, aborted fetus. Semen + vaginal swab PCR: more sensitive than 2ME-RSAT. Any PCR positive (viable or not) = confirmed infection.
- Antibody kinetics: 3–12 weeks post-infection for antibody development. Dogs may alternate between seropositive and seronegative.

**Treatment:**
- Euthanasia often recommended (treatment rarely curative; zoonotic risk).
- Combination therapy: 3-month course of doxycycline + gentamicin + rifampin (Makloski 2011 protocol); or enrofloxacin monotherapy (Wanke et al.).
- All treated dogs must be spayed/neutered and isolated indefinitely. Retest every 4–6 months.
- Kennel response: quarantine ≥3 months, stop all breeding, monthly testing, remove positives.

**Zoonosis:** ZOONOTIC. Low pathogenicity in humans but causes flu-like illness; underreported. Highest risk: children, elderly, pregnant women, immunocompromised. Reportable in many US states.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Brucellosis)

### Ch 190 — Tetanus and Botulism

#### TETANUS

**Organism / Pathogenesis:**
- Clostridium tetani: Gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming rod. Spores ubiquitous in moist fertile soil.
- Tetanospasmin → retrograde axonal transport to CNS → cleaves synaptobrevin (SNARE protein) → prevents release of glycine and GABA from inhibitory interneurons → disinhibited motor neuron firing → spastic paralysis.
- Incubation: 5–12 days (up to 3 weeks) after wound inoculation.

**Clinical signs (Ch 190 severity classification):**
- Class I: facial signs only (erect ears, furrowed brow, risus sardonicus, enophthalmos, third eyelid protrusion).
- Class II: generalized rigidity or dysphagia ± class I.
- Class III: recumbency or seizures ± class I or II.
- Class IV: autonomic dysfunction (tachycardia/bradycardia, hypertension, dysuria, constipation) ± class I–III.
- Mortality: up to 50% in dogs; 100% survival in class I/II; 58% in class III/IV.
- Cats: usually localized tetanus; recover well; carpal flexion pattern typical.
- Serum creatine kinase often elevated (1599–18,405 U/L) in >50% of dogs.

**Diagnosis:** Primarily clinical. CBC may reflect infection; serum biochemistry and CSF normal. Culture from wound often fails. EMG: prolonged discharges after needle insertion; doublet motor unit discharges.

**Treatment:**
- Environment: dark, quiet; cotton wool in ear canals; minimal handling.
- Antitoxin (equine): 100–1000 U/kg (maximum 20,000 U/kg) IV, SC, or IM. Test dose first (0.1–0.2 mL of 1:10,000 solution intradermal). Neutralizes only unbound toxin — benefit decreasing with disease progression; no significant survival benefit proven.
- Antimicrobials (kill vegetative organisms): Metronidazole 7–10 mg/kg PO or IV q8–12h for 10 days (SUPERIOR to penicillin G — achieves bactericidal concentrations in anaerobic tissue). Alternatively: clindamycin 10 mg/kg PO/IV/IM q8–12h or doxycycline 5–10 mg/kg PO/IV q12h.
- Sedation: Diazepam 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q8h (dogs; max 10 mg); cats 0.25–0.5 mg/kg (max 5 mg); IV infusion 0.1–1 mg/kg/hr; or clorazepate (dogs: 0.5–1 mg/kg q8h; cats: 0.2–0.5 mg/kg q12–24h).
- Phenobarbital: 1–4 mg/kg PO or IV q12h or IM q6h.
- Chlorpromazine (drug of choice for hyperexcitability): 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IM/IV/PO q6–12h.
- Acepromazine (substitute): 0.005–0.05 mg/kg IV q2h as needed (max 3 mg/dog).
- Propofol infusion: for tonic-clonic seizure activity.
- Magnesium sulfate (adjunct): 70 mg/kg over 30 minutes then 100 mg/kg/day CRI; oral magnesium aspartate 12 mg/kg q12h for 14 days once feasible.
- Recovery: 3–5 months for axonal regrowth. REM sleep behavior disorder in up to 50% of recovering dogs (self-resolves within 6 months).

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Tetanus)

#### BOTULISM

**Organism / Pathogenesis:**
- Clostridium botulinum: Gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming rods. Toxin types A–G; dogs and cats primarily type C.
- Ingestion of preformed toxin in carrion/rancid food → toxin absorbed from small intestine → binds presynaptic peripheral nerve terminals → modifies SNARE proteins → prevents ACh exocytosis at NMJ → flaccid paralysis.

**Clinical signs:** Afebrile, acute-onset progressive flaccid tetraparesis/plegia. Clinical signs within 12 hours of ingestion. Normal pain perception and consciousness. LMN signs (decreased/absent reflexes and muscle tone). Cranial nerve deficits in severe cases. Cholinergic signs (mydriasis, KCS, urinary retention, constipation, HR changes). Respiratory failure = death.

**Diagnosis:** Clinical (history of carrion exposure; multiple animals affected). Definitive: demonstration of botulinum toxin in blood or intestinal contents early in disease. EMG: reduced compound motor action potential amplitude; normal NCV.

**Treatment:** Supportive care (padding, turning, bladder management). Antitoxin (type C, if available): effective only if early; test dose first. Recovery: 2–3 weeks; muscle weakness may persist up to 1 year.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Botulism)

### Ch 191 — Bartonellosis

**Organism / Vector:**
- Bartonella spp.: Gram-negative bacteria; >14 species detected in dogs/cats.
- Cats: natural reservoir for B. henselae (2 serotypes) and B. clarridgeiae, B. koehlerae.
- Dogs: reservoir for B. vinsonii subsp. berkhoffii (4 genotypes) and B. rochalimae.
- Vector: cat flea (Ctenocephalides felis) primary vector. Ticks (Ixodes ricinus, Rhipicephalus sanguineus) likely but not confirmed as vectors for dogs.
- Prevalence: cats 5–40%; dogs 1–20%; 18–20% of clinically healthy dogs infected.
- Relapsing bacteremia cycles: ~5-day intervals.

**Clinical manifestations:**

*Dogs (CANINE BARTONELLOSIS):*
- Endocarditis (aortic valve most common; 19–28% of canine IE); congestive heart failure.
- Physical exam: heart murmur (89%), lameness (43%), respiratory signs (28%), collapse/weakness (17%), fever.
- Lab: proteinuria (40%), anemia (38%), leukocytosis (36%), thrombocytopenia (34%), elevated liver enzymes (32%), hyperbilirubinemia (30%), hyperglobulinemia (26%), azotemia (21%).
- Other: myocarditis, arrhythmias, PLN (glomerular), polyarthritis, neurologic signs (21%), meningoencephalitis, pyogranulomatous disease, dermatitis/panniculitis, peliosis hepatis.

*Cats (FELINE BARTONELLOSIS):*
- Most cats subclinically bacteremic.
- Sick cats: fever, lymphadenopathy, lethargy, uveitis, endocarditis, myocarditis, reproductive failure.
- Lab: anemia, eosinophilia, mature neutrophilia, thrombocytopenia, hyperglobulinemia.
- B. henselae IgG titer >1:512: supports endocarditis diagnosis in dogs.

**Diagnostics:**
- Enrichment PCR ("BAPGM ePCR"): most sensitive — culture in Bartonella alpha-Proteobacteria growth medium then PCR. Available Galaxy Diagnostics, Research Triangle Park, NC.
- Standard PCR (EDTA blood): best for cats with high bacteremia or endocarditis. Low sensitivity in dogs with non-endocarditis syndromes (<10 organisms/mcL).
- "Triple draw": 3 separate blood draws on consecutive days to account for relapsing bacteremia.
- Serology: high titers (>1:512) support endocarditis; overestimates infection in cats, underestimates in dogs. >50% of seropositive dogs are PCR-negative.
- Culture: gold standard; 4–8 weeks; poor yield from dog blood except with endocarditis.
- Non-blood specimens (effusion, synovial fluid, tissue): often higher sensitivity than blood for non-endocarditis disease.

**Treatment (Tables 191.2 and 191.3):**

*Canine bartonellosis (treat only if clinically ill):*
- Long-term combination therapy — monotherapy doxycycline or azithromycin no longer recommended.
- Bacteremia: **Doxycycline 5 mg/kg PO q12h or 10 mg/kg PO q24h + enrofloxacin 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h for 6 weeks**.
- CNS infection: **Doxycycline 5 mg/kg PO q12h or 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 6 weeks + rifampin 5 mg/kg PO q12h for 4–6 weeks**; or azithromycin 10 mg/kg PO q24h/7 days then every other day for 6–12 weeks + rifampin 5 mg/kg PO q12h for 4–6 weeks.
- Endocarditis/hospitalization: **Doxycycline 5 mg/kg PO q12h or 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 6 weeks + amikacin 20–30 mg/kg IV/IM/SC q24h (inpatient only; monitor renal function)**.
- Monitor with serology + enrichment PCR at 2 and 6 weeks post-therapy.

*Feline bartonellosis (treat only if clinically ill):*
- Bacteremia: **Doxycycline 5 mg/kg PO q12h or 10 mg/kg PO q24h + pradofloxacin 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 4–6 weeks**.
- Endocarditis: **Marbofloxacin 5.5 mg/kg PO q24h for 6 weeks + azithromycin 10 mg/kg PO q24h/7 days then every other day for 6–12 weeks**.
- Osteomyelitis/polyarthritis: **Amoxicillin-clavulanate 62.5 mg/cat PO q12h for 4 weeks + azithromycin 10 mg/kg PO q48h for 12 weeks**.
- Pradofloxacin superior to enrofloxacin for Bartonella in cats.

- Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction: start second antibiotic 5–7 days after first. Treat with short-course prednisone (0.5–1 mg/kg PO q24h dogs; prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg PO q24h cats) if reaction occurs.
- Do not treat healthy PCR/culture-positive cats — risk of resistance development.

**Zoonosis:** Cat scratch disease in humans (B. henselae; papule + regional lymphadenopathy). Serious risk to immunocompromised. No documented dog-to-human transmission via bite/scratch (Bartonella DNA in dog saliva). Occupation hazard for veterinary staff.

### Ch 192 — Leptospirosis

**Organism / Epidemiology:**
- Leptospira spp.: thin, helically coiled spirochetes with hooked ends; >250 pathogenic serovars in 24 serogroups. Genus divided: P1 (pathogenic), P2 (intermediates), S1/S2 (saprophytic).
- Reservoir: small rodents (primary); also wildlife and domestic animals including dogs and cats.
- Route: contact with urine of infected animal via mucous membranes or broken skin; contaminated soil or water.
- Incidental hosts develop acute, severe, potentially lethal disease; reservoir hosts = subclinical.
- Formerly: Canicola and Icterohaemorrhagiae serogroups predominated. Now: Australis, Grippotyphosa, Pomona, Autumnalis, Sejroe also important.
- Seasonal: peak in warmer, wetter months. Risk factors: outdoor water access, wildlife exposure, herding/hound/working breeds, male sex (some studies).

**Pathogenic mechanisms:**
- Leptospiremia: cleared by acquired immune response after 1 week; persist in "immune-privileged" sites (renal tubules, uvea).
- AKI: acute interstitial nephritis + tubular necrosis; hypokalemic non-oliguric AKI via tubular Na+-K+ ATPase inhibition; acquired vasopressin resistance → hyposthenuria.
- Hepatic: cholestatic hepatitis; hepatocellular necrosis; periportal edema; Kupffer cell proliferation; hyperbilirubinemia from bile canaliculi disruption.
- LPHS (Leptospiral Pulmonary Hemorrhage Syndrome): intra-alveolar hemorrhage WITHOUT significant vasculitis; major cause of mortality; multifactorial pathogenesis. Emerging complication.

**Clinical signs:**
- Most common: anorexia, vomiting, lethargy, abdominal pain, diarrhea, jaundice, dehydration, musculoskeletal pain, fever or hypothermia, dyspnea.
- Oliguria/anuria: ~30% of dogs with acute leptospirosis in referral population.
- In one Swiss cohort (n=298): renal involvement 99.6%, pulmonary 76.7%, hepatic 26%, DIC 18.2%.
- Other: ventricular tachyarrhythmias, intussusception, ocular signs, skin calcifications.
- Cats: usually subclinical shedders; interstitial nephritis most common histopathologic finding.

**Lab findings:**
- CBC: mild-moderate anemia (50%); neutrophilic leukocytosis with left shift, lymphopenia, monocytosis; thrombocytopenia (common, multifactorial).
- Chemistry: azotemia (most); ALP and bilirubin elevations more frequent than ALT. Hypokalemia (renal/GI wasting + Na+-K+ ATPase inhibition). Hyponatremia, hypochloremia. Hyperbilirubinemia in icteric form.
- Urinalysis: isosthenuria (majority) or hyposthenuria; glucosuria; hematuria, pyuria, granular casts; proteinuria (glomerular + tubular); organisms NOT visible on light microscopy.

**Diagnostics:**
- MAT (microscopic agglutination test): gold standard; acute + convalescent 1–2 weeks apart. Fourfold rise (or seroconversion from negative to ≥1:800 to 1+ serovars) = highly suggestive. Single titer ≥1:800 in unvaccinated dog with consistent signs + bivalent vaccine = likely leptospirosis.
- Post-vaccinal titers: can reach 1:6400+; majority negative by week 15 but may persist up to 12 months in small percentage.
- POC ELISA/lateral flow assays: useful early (before MAT antibody rise); same limitations regarding early infection and vaccination.
- PCR on blood (first week) then urine (thereafter); test both simultaneously as exact timing unknown. PCR does NOT yield false positives post-vaccination.
- PCR targets: lipL32 gene (pathogenic-specific) or 23S rDNA.
- Algorithm: suspect → collect blood + urine PCR + initiate treatment → submit MAT → if PCR positive = acute; if negative → pursue MAT +/- retest at 7–14 days.

**Treatment:**
- **Doxycycline: 5 mg/kg PO q12h or 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 14 days** (clears leptospires from blood AND kidneys — superior to penicillin in vivo for renal clearance).
- If doxycycline not tolerated initially (GI effects): penicillin G, ampicillin, or amoxicillin IV until doxycycline tolerated. Penicillin clears leptospiremia but NOT reliable for renal clearance.
- Macrolides (azithromycin) and 3rd-gen cephalosporins: alternative in humans; evaluated in animal models.
- Contact household dogs: treat with doxycycline for 2 weeks.
- LPHS: supportive (avoid stress/overhydration/systemic hypertension); O₂ ± mechanical ventilation in severe cases. Immunosuppression data limited.
- Long-term: ~50% of survivors have impaired renal function >1 year post-discharge; monitor long-term.

**Vaccination:**
- Bivalent: partial/no immunity to heterologous serogroups.
- Quadrivalent (USA since 2001; various European formulations): covers Icterohaemorrhagiae + Canicola + Grippotyphosa + Pomona or Australis/Bratislava. Significantly reduces odds of leptospirosis (Swiss case-control study).
- Annual vaccination recommended (12-month protection demonstrated).

**Zoonosis:** ZOONOTIC. Dog-to-human transmission possible but exact risk unknown. Dogs should urinate away from heavily frequented areas until course complete. Owners: wear gloves, wash hands, disinfect urine spills.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Leptospirosis (DIS-INFECT-LEPTO)

### Ch 193 — Ehrlichiosis, Anaplasmosis, Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever, Neorickettsiosis

#### EHRLICHIA CANIS (Canine Monocytic Ehrlichiosis, CME)

**Organism / Vector / Geography:**
- Obligate intracellular; infects monocytes; forms morulae. Vector: Rhipicephalus sanguineus (brown dog tick); transmitted transstadially, not transovarially.
- Worldwide; most frequent in southern US. Can be transported by subclinically infected dogs to non-endemic regions.
- Transmission possible within hours of tick attachment.

**Clinical phases:**
- Acute (8–20 days post-tick): lethargy, fever, inappetence, weight loss, lymphadenomegaly, splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia (1–4 weeks post-infection), mild leukopenia, anemia, ocular/nasal discharge, peripheral edema, petechiae. May spontaneously recover in 2–4 weeks.
- Subclinical: months to years; mild persistent thrombocytopenia; organism sequesters in spleen.
- Chronic: lethargy, inappetence, bleeding tendencies, pallor, fever, weight loss, lymphadenomegaly, splenomegaly, anterior uveitis, retinal hemorrhage/detachment, PU/PD, edema. Pancytopenia (hypoplastic bone marrow) in severe chronic form.
- Atypical lymphocytes on CBC; lymphocytosis, bone marrow plasmacytosis, monoclonal or polyclonal hyperglobulinemia → rule out E. canis when lymphoreticular neoplasia suspected.
- Hypoalbuminemia, elevated ALT/ALP; protein-losing nephropathy from immune-complex glomerulonephritis.

**Diagnostics:**
- Morulae in monocytes: diagnostic if seen but insensitive (found in 2/19 dogs with chronic CME in one study). Cannot distinguish E. canis from E. chaffeensis morphologically.
- IFA: antibodies detectable 7–28 days post-infection. Chronic CME: titers often >1:600,000. May remain elevated years post-treatment. Cross-reactivity: E. ewingii, E. chaffeensis, minor cross with A. phagocytophilum.
- SNAP 4Dx Plus (IDEXX): POC ELISA for E. canis and E. ewingii combined (one spot). Sensitivity 97.8%, specificity 92.3% for E. canis.
- AccuPlex (Antech): detects E. canis antibodies (also Anaplasma, Borrelia, D. immitis).
- PCR: on blood, lymph node aspirates, splenic aspirates, or bone marrow (bone marrow sensitivity as low as 25%). Best for acute illness (first week, before seroconversion). Not suitable alone for blood donor screening.

**Treatment:**
- **Doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 28 days**. Shorter courses (e.g., 16 days) may be effective for acute CME.
- Clinical improvement: 24–48 hours. Platelet counts improve/normalize within 2 weeks.
- Hyperglobulinemia: may take months to resolve.
- Titers decline 6–9 months post-treatment; some maintain high titers for years — base retreat decisions on platelet counts and globulin trends.
- Refractory thrombocytopenia: add short course (up to 1 week) immunosuppressive glucocorticoids.
- Chloramphenicol, imidocarb dipropionate, enrofloxacin: variable success; E. canis has intrinsic gyrase-mediated resistance to fluoroquinolones — enrofloxacin NOT recommended.
- Severe chronic disease: may not respond; cytopenias may persist months.

**Zoonosis:** E. canis DNA detected in humans with monocytic ehrlichiosis (Venezuela). Handle ticks, blood, tissue with precautions.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Ehrlichiosis (DIS-INFECT-EHRLICH + DIS-BD-EHRL CME)

#### EHRLICHIA EWINGII

- Granulocytic ehrlichiosis in dogs and people. Vector: Amblyomma americanum. Reservoir: white-tailed deer. South-central and southeastern USA primarily.
- Infects and forms morulae in GRANULOCYTES.
- Clinical: fever, lethargy, vomiting, diarrhea, neutrophilic polyarthritis, neurologic signs.
- Lab: anemia, leukopenia or leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, hyperglobulinemia, elevated ALP.
- Diagnosis: granulocytic morulae on blood smear; SNAP 4Dx Plus (sensitivity 96.5%, specificity 93.9% vs plate ELISA); E. ewingii-specific PCR is only confirmatory method.
- Treatment: doxycycline 2–4 weeks → rapid clinical improvement. Persistent subclinical infection documented.

#### ANAPLASMA PHAGOCYTOPHILUM (Granulocytic Anaplasmosis)

**Organism / Vector / Geography:**
- Infects and forms morulae in NEUTROPHILS (rarely eosinophils). Vector: Ixodes spp. ticks (I. scapularis in NE/upper Midwest USA, I. pacificus on west coast, I. ricinus in Europe). Requires 24–48 hours tick attachment. Reservoir: small mammals.
- North America (NE, upper Midwest most common), Europe (including UK), Asia. Labrador and Golden Retrievers account for 50% of cases (outdoor activity, not breed predisposition).
- Disease self-limiting in dogs — chronic disease (>30 days) not described.

**Clinical signs:** Often subclinical. When symptomatic (1–2 week incubation): fever, lethargy, inappetence, scleral injection, lameness, stiffness. Lymphadenomegaly, splenomegaly, neutrophilic polyarthritis. PU/PD, vomiting, diarrhea, CNS signs less common.

**Lab:** Thrombocytopenia (>80% of infected dogs). Less common: lymphopenia, eosinopenia, mild nonregenerative anemia. Mild hypoalbuminemia, elevated ALT and ALP.

**Diagnosis:**
- Morulae in neutrophils in endemic area: highly suggestive; found in 7–37% of circulating neutrophils. Cannot distinguish A. phagocytophilum from E. ewingii morulae.
- SNAP 4Dx Plus: sensitivity 92%, specificity 89% vs IFA. Many acutely ill dogs seronegative at presentation (insufficient time for antibody response).
- Fourfold titer rise required to confirm (previous subclinical exposure within 8–10 months causes detectable titers without active disease).
- PCR on whole blood: differentiates A. phagocytophilum from E. ewingii when morulae are seen in granulocytes.
- Cross-reactivity with A. platys and possibly Ehrlichia spp. on serology.

**Treatment:** **Doxycycline 5 mg/kg PO q12h for 2 weeks.** Signs abate within 12–48 hours. Tick control for prevention.

**Public health:** Causes human granulocytic anaplasmosis (febrile, rarely fatal). Dogs sentinel for human infection.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Anaplasmosis — A. phagocytophilum)

#### ANAPLASMA PLATYS (Canine Thrombocytic Anaplasmosis / Infectious Cyclic Thrombocytopenia)

- Infects platelets; forms morulae in platelets (visible on blood smear) and megakaryocytes in bone marrow.
- Vector: likely R. sanguineus (unconfirmed). South America, Australia, Asia, Africa, Europe, North America.
- Cyclic thrombocytopenia (1–2 week incubation, then spontaneous recovery of platelet count within days). Morulae visible in platelets during thrombocytopenic period.
- USA: mostly mild or subclinical. Europe/South America: more severe (fever, splenomegaly, hemorrhage). Coinfections common.
- Serology: cross-reacts with A. phagocytophilum (non-specific). A. platys-specific PCR available.
- Treatment: same as granulocytic anaplasmosis. Persistent human infection documented.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Infectious Cyclic Thrombocytopenia (Anaplasma platys)

#### NEORICKETTSIA HELMINTHOECA (Salmon Poisoning Disease, SPD)

**Organism / Life cycle / Geography:**
- Neorickettsia helminthoeca (family Anaplasmataceae): infects mononuclear cells.
- Vector/cycle: Nanophyetus salmincola (trematode) → miracidia → Oxytrema silicula snails → cercariae → metacercariae in salmonid (and some non-salmonid) fish kidneys/muscle → dogs eat infected fish → adult flukes in intestine → rickettsia infects dog's lymphoid tissue.
- Endemic: western slopes of Cascade Mountains, N California to S Vancouver Island. Disease can occur elsewhere via transport of infected fish.
- Cats: not susceptible to SPD (flukes mature but rickettsia not pathogenic in cats).

**Clinical signs (after 5–33 day incubation):**
- Anorexia, fever up to 42°C (107.6°F), lethargy, weight loss, vomiting, hemorrhagic diarrhea, marked lymphadenomegaly.
- Lab: neutrophilia ± left shift, lymphopenia, monocytosis; thrombocytopenia (~90%); hyponatremia, hypokalemia, elevated liver enzymes, hypoalbuminemia ± hypocholesterolemia and hypoglobulinemia.

**Diagnosis:**
- Fecal sedimentation or sugar flotation for N. salmincola ova (operculated eggs, 5–8 days post-ingestion). Combination flotation + sedimentation = 93% sensitivity. Ova may be shed months after recovery.
- N. salmincola ova in only 0.2% of >1800 routine fecal flotations (high specificity when found in compatible clinical setting).
- Cytology of lymph node aspirate: Neorickettsia organisms within macrophages + marked histiocytic inflammation.
- PCR on lymphoid tissue available.

**Treatment:** Doxycycline (parenteral if vomiting) + supportive care (fluids, blood products, antiemetics). Praziquantel to treat helminth infection. Prevention: feed only cooked or frozen (≥24 hours) fish.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Salmon Poisoning Disease / Neorickettsiosis)

#### ROCKY MOUNTAIN SPOTTED FEVER (RMSF)

**Organism / Vector / Geography:**
- Rickettsia rickettsii (family Rickettsiaceae). Infects and replicates in vascular endothelial cells.
- Vectors: Dermacentor variabilis (American dog tick; east of Rockies), D. andersoni (Rocky Mountain wood tick; Rocky Mountain states). Rhipicephalus sanguineus in Arizona. Amblyomma spp. in Central/South America.
- Transmitted transstadially AND transovarially. Reactivation period 4–24 hours after tick attachment before transmission.
- Incubation: 2–14 days (mean 7). Geographic: south-central and southeastern USA primarily; Central and South America (Brazilian spotted fever).
- Exposure to dogs = risk factor for human RMSF.

**Clinical signs (dogs and humans):**
- Fever, lethargy, anorexia, lymphadenomegaly.
- Edema/erythema of extremities (lips, muzzle, scrotum, penile sheath, pinnae, ventral abdomen) → potential necrosis/gangrene in severe human cases.
- Ocular: conjunctivitis, mucopurulent discharge, scleral injection, uveitis, hyphema, iridal/retinal hemorrhage.
- Petechiae/ecchymoses, epistaxis, melena, hematuria.
- Neurologic: ataxia, tremors, vestibular signs, hyperesthesia, opisthotonus, seizures (up to 80% of dogs).
- Respiratory: interstitial pattern (pulmonary edema).
- Cardiovascular: arrhythmias (myocarditis), AKI.
- Lab: leukocytosis ± left shift, anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes and CK, hypoalbuminemia, electrolyte disturbances, azotemia and hyperbilirubinemia in severe cases. CSF: elevated protein (<100 mg/dL usually), neutrophils and mononuclear cells.
- Human mortality: 2–10% generally; up to 22% in children (Mexico). Median time onset to death = 8 days.

**Diagnosis:**
- Serology: microimmunofluorescence (reference), ELISA. Fourfold rise in IgG titer (acute + convalescent) OR single titer >1:1024 with compatible clinical signs = diagnostic. Antibodies not detectable until 7–10 days after onset → serology limited for early diagnosis.
- Wide cross-reactivity among pathogenic and non-pathogenic SFG rickettsiae.
- Direct immunofluorescence or immunoperoxidase on skin biopsy: specificity and sensitivity ~75%.
- PCR on blood or tissue; low circulating numbers = poor sensitivity. PCR + immunohistochemistry on skin biopsy improves confirmation.
- Biosafety level 3 agent; cultivation requires special facilities.

**Treatment:**
- **Doxycycline: clinical response within 12–24 hours.** Other tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, and fluoroquinolones also effective. Continue ≥7 days. Parenteral if vomiting or neurologic signs.
- Do NOT delay treatment awaiting diagnostic results — treatment delays correlate with increased severity and mortality.
- Supportive: IV fluids/blood products for shock. CAUTION: avoid over-hydration (pulmonary/cerebral edema risk with crystalloids).
- Natural infection provides solid immunity; reinfection not documented.

**Zoonosis:** Dogs as sentinels. Precautions when removing ticks from dogs (gloves; wash hands; fine-tip forceps).

> ClinIQ: ✓ RMSF (DIS-INFECT-RMSF)

#### FELINE RICKETTSIAL DISEASE

- E. canis-like organisms detected in cats; clinical signs include polyarthritis, cytopenias. Cats rarely seropositive by IFA for E. canis.
- Cats with monocytic ehrlichiae: **doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q24h for minimum 28 days.**
- A. phagocytophilum in cats: morulae in granulocytes; lethargy, anorexia, fever, pallor, epistaxis, lameness, lymphadenopathy; doxycycline effective.

### Ch 194 — Hemotropic Mycoplasmas

**Species and pathogenicity:**

| Species | Usual host | Prevalence range (median) | Pathogenicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mycoplasma haemofelis | Cat | 0.4–27.0% (5.1%) | Acute hemolytic anemia; associated with IMHA |
| 'Ca. M. haemominutum' | Cat | 4.4–46.7% (15.5%) | Mild erythrocyte parameter drops; anemia if concurrent disease/immunosuppression |
| 'Ca. M. turicensis' | Cat | 0–26.0% (1.9%) | Occasional anemia; usually concurrent disease needed |
| Mycoplasma haemocanis | Dog | 0–48.6% (6.85%) | Hemolytic anemia mainly in splenectomized/immunosuppressed dogs |
| 'Ca. M. haematoparvum' | Dog | 0–33.3% (1.5%) | Anemia only if concurrent disease or immunosuppression |

**Pathogenesis:**
- Organisms live on erythrocyte surface membrane (wall-less bacteria).
- M. haemofelis: most pathogenic feline species; acute extravascular hemolytic anemia; antigenic variation explains fluctuating bacteremia; Coombs' positive but antibodies appear AFTER anemia (secondary, not primary cause — antibiotics + supportive care resolve without glucocorticoids).
- M. haemocanis: causes clinical disease primarily in splenectomized dogs; latent infections reactivated by splenectomy.
- Risk factors in cats: male sex, older age, non-pedigree, outdoor access, FIV positive.
- Canine risk factors: tick exposure, fighting (blood transmission), R. sanguineus geographic distribution.
- Transmission: cat bites (blood inoculation), possibly fleas/ticks, vertical (M. haemocanis likely), blood transfusion (screen donors).

**Clinical signs:** Lethargy, pallor, weakness. Inappetence, dehydration, weight loss, intermittent fever. Splenomegaly in cats (not dogs, usually already splenectomized). Tachycardia, tachypnea, murmur in severe anemia. Icterus rare.

**Lab:** Regenerative macrocytic hypochromic anemia (not always reticulocytosis initially). Positive Coombs' ± autoagglutination (cold agglutinins). Occasionally hyperproteinemia, hyperbilirubinemia, elevated ALT.

**Diagnosis:**
- Cytology: poor sensitivity (0–37.5%); cannot differentiate species. M. turicensis never visualized. Specificity with board-certified pathologist 84–98% when organisms seen.
- **PCR: gold standard — excellent sensitivity and specificity.** Differentiate species. qPCR allows quantitative monitoring of response. Always interpret positive in context (carrier state common for 'Ca. M. haemominutum').
- Duplex qPCR with host housekeeping gene prevents false negatives.

**Treatment (Table 194.2):**

| Drug | Dose | Route | Interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Doxycycline | 5 mg/kg | PO | q12h | First-line (10 mg/kg q24h GI side effects) |
| Doxycycline | 10 mg/kg | PO | q24h | Single daily dosing; esophagitis risk in cats (follow with food/water) |
| Marbofloxacin | 2–5.5 mg/kg | PO | q24h | Second-line; effective M. haemofelis; sequential after doxy for persistent infection |
| Pradofloxacin | 3–4.5 mg/kg | PO | q24h | May clear M. haemofelis better than doxy |
| Enrofloxacin | 5 mg/kg | PO | q24h | Effective but rare irreversible retinal toxicity in cats — avoid |

- First episode M. haemofelis or M. haemocanis with rapid favorable response: 2 weeks doxycycline may be adequate.
- Complex cases: 2–4 weeks doxycycline, monitor by qPCR.
- Persistent M. haemofelis after full doxycycline course: **4 weeks doxycycline followed by 2 weeks marbofloxacin** (Novacco 2018 protocol — clears bacteremia).
- Immunosuppressive glucocorticoids NOT recommended — can reactivate hemoplasma infection.

**Prevention:** Screen blood donors by PCR. Indoor housing (reduces vector exposure). Ectoparasite prevention for tick/flea vectors.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Hemotropic Mycoplasma / Hemoplasmosis / Haemobartonella)

### Ch 195 — Enteric Bacterial Diseases

**General approach:**
- Most bacterial enteritis self-limiting; antimicrobials not routinely indicated.
- Antimicrobials indicated for: SIRS/sepsis, extra-GI infections, immunocompromised patients (parenteral route preferred), neonates/juveniles with severe disease.
- All major enteropathogens have zoonotic potential. Raw meat diets = significant risk factor.

#### CAMPYLOBACTER SPP.

**Species in dogs/cats:** C. upsaliensis (most common in dogs), C. jejuni, C. helveticus (cats).
- Prevalence in healthy animals: 0–87% dogs, 0–75% cats (colonization). Higher in immature and intensively housed animals.
- Transmission: fecal-oral direct or indirect; survive weeks in feces.
- Pathogenicity: CDT (cytolethal distending toxin); invasive. Co-infections with parvovirus, coronavirus, Clostridium common.

**Clinical signs:** Usually subclinical. Immature/predisposed animals: mild-moderate diarrhea (watery to hemorrhagic/mucoid), tenesmus, lethargy, dehydration, anorexia, ± fever, vomiting. Extra-GI: cholangiohepatitis, cholecystitis. Association with IBD (especially neutrophilic IBD in cats — C. coli; FISH on endoscopic biopsies).

**Diagnosis:** Culture on modified charcoal cefoperazone deoxycholate (CCDA) agar; microaerophilic at 37°C (C. jejuni also thermophilic — 42°C preferred). MALDI-TOF MS for identification. Direct PCR on feces (genus-level or subspecies-level; improved sensitivity). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing lacking standardized veterinary breakpoints.

**Treatment (where indicated):**
- Erythromycin 10–15 mg/kg PO q8h; or
- Tylosin 6–16 mg/kg PO q12h; or
- Azithromycin 5–10 mg/kg PO q24h; or
- Enrofloxacin 5 mg/kg PO q24h — for 5–7 days.

**Zoonosis:** Contact with immature and diarrheic pets significant risk factor for C. jejuni/coli. Identical subtypes documented in pets and their owners.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Campylobacteriosis)

#### SALMONELLA SPP.

- Salmonella enterica (facultative anaerobic, motile Gram-negative rods; 6 subspecies; serotyping by O, K, H antigens).
- Prevalence in healthy pet dogs/cats: generally 0–3.6% (dogs), 0–4% (cats). Higher in juveniles, sheltered animals, stray dogs, working dogs.
- Risk factors: RMBD, kenneling, co-infections (e.g., parvovirus).
- Fecal shedding persists 3–6 weeks after resolution.

**Clinical signs:** Majority subclinical. Some: acute self-limiting malaise, anorexia, fever, vomiting, watery-mucoid ± hemorrhagic diarrhea. Endotoxemia and bacteremia (esp. young/immunocompromised) → DIC. Extra-GI: biliary tract, spleen, kidneys, heart, uterus, meninges, joints, lungs.

**Diagnosis:** Selective enrichment then culture (MacConkey, XLD, brilliant green agar); MALDI-TOF MS for ID. PCR after overnight enrichment (more sensitive). Intermittent shedding: collect at 2–3 week intervals until 3 consecutive negatives.

**Treatment:** Avoid antimicrobials routinely (promotes carrier state, antimicrobial resistance). Multidrug resistance in 43% of dog/cat isolates. When indicated (severe/systemic): trimethoprim-sulfonamides 15 mg/kg PO q12h or enrofloxacin 5 mg/kg PO q24h (good phagocytic penetration). Base on susceptibility.

**Zoonosis:** Confirmed risk factor: contact with pets, pet foods, pet treats.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Salmonellosis)

#### CLOSTRIDIOIDES (CLOSTRIDIUM) DIFFICILE

- Gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming bacillus. Spores resistant to most disinfectants (including alcohol-based hand sanitizers); killed by bleach (1:10) or accelerated hydrogen peroxide.
- Colonization prevalence: up to 58% healthy dogs, 21% healthy cats; higher in juveniles and hospitalized animals.
- Clinical CDI: hospital outbreaks documented. Usually subclinical or secondary pathogen.
- Toxins: tcdA (toxin A), tcdB (toxin B), binary toxin CDT.

**Diagnosis:** Culture + toxin gene PCR (tcdA and tcdB) — preferred. Human ELISA immunoassays for toxin A/B not validated in dogs/cats. Negative result rules out; positive with toxigenic genes but not antigen confirms.

**Treatment:** Supportive + dietary modification + probiotics. Metronidazole 10–15 mg/kg PO q12h where indicated; resistance in 21% of canine C. difficile isolates. Fecal microbiota transplant shown superior to metronidazole for acute diarrhea in one study.

**Zoonosis:** Identical ribotypes to human CDI detected in pets; zoonotic risk considered minimal but non-zero.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: CDI / Clostridium difficile enteritis)

#### CLOSTRIDIUM PERFRINGENS

- Ubiquitous, anaerobic, Gram-positive, spore-forming; 7 toxinotypes (A–G). Type A most common.
- Cultured in 84% healthy dogs and 91% diarrheic dogs (isolation alone has no diagnostic value).
- Acute hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome (AHDS): C. perfringens type A + netE/netF toxin genes (by real-time PCR) identified as primary pathogen in dogs with AHDS. Novel pore-forming toxins (NetE, NetF) are key virulence factors.
- Triggers for overgrowth: diet change, antimicrobial therapy, co-infections, concurrent disease.

**Diagnosis:** Detection of toxin genes by PCR — cpa (always present), cpe, netE, netF. Quantitative PCR cpa gene: cutoff >300,000 copies significant. CPE human immunoassays lack specificity in dogs.

**Treatment:** Supportive care sufficient for most. Probiotics may help microbiome recovery. Antimicrobials for AHDS with sepsis: metronidazole 10–15 mg/kg PO q12h, tylosin 6–16 mg/kg PO q12h, or ampicillin 10–20 mg/kg PO/IV q8h.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Clostridial enteritis / AHDS)

#### ESCHERICHIA COLI

- Pathotypes: EPEC, ETEC, EHEC/VTEC, EIEC, EAEC, AIEC.
- Granulomatous colitis (Boxer dog colitis): associated with AIEC; large intestinal diarrhea, hematochezia, weight loss; PAS-positive macrophages on endoscopic biopsy + FISH + culture on colonic biopsy. Treatment: enrofloxacin 5 mg/kg PO q12h for 6–8 weeks (achieve remission in most).

**Zoonosis:** Within-household sharing of E. coli strains confirmed. Pet ownership associated with ~7× increased risk for ESBL E. coli colonization.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Granulomatous colitis / E. coli enteritis)

### Ch 196 — Enteric Protozoan Diseases

**Major pathogens:** Giardia duodenalis, Tritrichomonas foetus (blagburni), Cystoisospora spp., Cryptosporidium spp. Also (uncommon): Toxoplasma gondii (GI phase), Neospora caninum, Balantidium coli, Entamoeba histolytica, Hammondia spp., Sarcocystis spp.

#### GENERAL PRINCIPLES

**Transmission:** Fecal-oral (most). Coccidians: sporulated oocysts; Giardia: cysts (ingestion of cysts or contaminated water/food); Tritrichomonas: trophozoites (no cyst stage); T. gondii/N. caninum: oocysts (and tissue cysts in intermediate hosts).

**Clinical:** Most enteric protozoa cause diarrhea primarily in young, immunosuppressed, or overcrowded animals. Treat only if diarrheic (not healthy carriers for Giardia, Cryptosporidium, Cystoisospora).

**Diagnosis (CAPC guidelines):**
- Sick animal: direct smear + centrifugal fecal flotation (zinc sulfate preferred for Giardia) + specific fecal ELISA.
- Flotation: at least 3 specimens over ~1 week before ruling out Giardia.
- Direct FA assay: higher sensitivity/specificity than flotation, Ziehl-Neelsen, or ELISA for Giardia and Cryptosporidium.
- PCR: most sensitive for T. foetus; also differentiates Toxoplasma from Neospora and Hammondia.
- Wet mount: motile trophozoites of Giardia (falling leaf at 100×) vs. Tritrichomonas (tear-drop, undulating membrane, vigorous motility); sensitivity ≤14% for T. foetus. Giardia ELISA can differentiate from Tritrichomonas.

#### GIARDIA SPP.

- G. duodenalis: most common; prevalence higher in dog park dogs but no association with diarrhea; host-specific assemblages C/D (dogs) and F (cats).
- Trophozoites in duodenum-ileum (dogs) and jejunum-ileum (cats).
- Diarrhea: mucoid, pale, soft, foul-smelling; steatorrhea possible. Not enteroinvasive — blood uncommon without coinfection.

**Treatment (CAPC recommendations):**
- Metronidazole monotherapy: 10–25 mg/kg PO q12h for 5–8 days. If combined with fenbendazole: 25 mg/kg PO q12h for 5 days.
- Fenbendazole monotherapy: 50 mg/kg PO q24h for 3–5 days. Combined with metro: 50 mg/kg PO q24h for 5 days.
- Secnidazole: 30 mg/kg PO single dose (cats); single or 2 doses (dogs, 2 weeks apart).
- Drontal Plus: febantel 25 mg/kg + praziquantel 5 mg/kg + pyrantel 5 mg/kg PO q24h for 3 days (dogs); 56.6/11.3/11.3 mg/kg for 5 days (cats).
- Nitazoxanide (NTZ): 100 mg/animal PO q12h for 3–4 days. GI irritant; vomiting common; not effective without functional immune response.
- CAPC does NOT recommend treating Giardia-positive animals without diarrhea. Do NOT use ELISA fecal antigen test to assess treatment response after diarrhea resolves.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Giardiasis)

#### TRITRICHOMONAS FOETUS (Blagburni)

- Cats primarily (young, purebred; catteries with history of recurrent diarrhea). Co-infections with Cystoisospora and Giardia increase clinical potential.
- Clinical: waxing and waning large bowel diarrhea; semi-formed feces with mucus ± fresh blood; tenesmus. Good body condition (only large intestine affected). Trophozoites survive in moist feces 7 days.
- Long-lasting, generally self-limiting infection (months to years).

**Treatment:**
- **Ronidazole 30 mg/kg PO q24h for 14 days** (only recognized effective treatment). Drug of choice — reserve for T. foetus only (do not use for Giardia alone due to resistance risk). Feces consistency improves within days; normalizes by 2 weeks. Neurotoxicosis reported in cats — caution.
- PCR to confirm efficacy. If PCR negative but diarrhea persists → consider other causes.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Tritrichomoniasis)

#### CRYPTOSPORIDIUM SPP.

- C. canis (dogs), C. felis (cats) are host-adapted species. C. parvum more zoonotic potential.
- Prepatent: 2–14 days (dog pups), 2–11 days (kittens). Patent: up to 33 days (dogs), 25 days (kittens).
- Most infections subclinical; clinical disease in immunosuppressed, preexisting intestinal disease, or chronic diarrhea cases.
- Clinical: small bowel diarrhea (high volume, low frequency, significant weight loss). Fresh blood/tenesmus in some chronic cases.
- Extracytoplasmic but intracellular location explains resistance to pharmacotherapy.

**Treatment (Table 196.2):**
- Azithromycin: dogs 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h for 5–7 days; cats 7–15 mg/kg PO q12h for 5–7 days.
- Nitazoxanide: 25 mg total PO q12h for 5 days (dogs/cats combined).
- Paromomycin: 125–165 mg/kg PO q12–24h for 5 days. CAUTION: do NOT use with bloody diarrhea (systemic absorption → renal toxicosis + ototoxicosis). 4/32 cats given paromomycin for T. foetus developed AKI; 3 became deaf.
- Tylosin: 10–15 mg/kg PO q8–12h for 21 days.
- None consistently eliminate infection; addressing immunosuppression is critical.
- Combination (azithromycin + paromomycin ± NTZ): used in human medicine.
- Addressing immunosuppression = mainstay for severe infections.

#### CYSTOISOSPORA SPP. (Isospora)

**Dogs:** C. canis (prepatent 9–11 days), C. ohioensis (6–7 days).
**Cats:** C. felis (prepatent 7–11 days), C. rivolta (4–7 days).
- Clinical: diarrhea, weight loss, dehydration; vomiting, anorexia, depression in severe cases; most common in young/debilitated/immunosuppressed.

**Treatment (Table 196.2):**
- Sulfadimethoxine 50–60 mg/kg PO q24h for 5–20 days (only FDA-approved drug for coccidiosis).
- Trimethoprim/sulfonamide 15–30 mg/kg PO q12–24h for 5 days.
- Ponazuril: 20 mg/kg PO q24h for 3 days; or 50 mg/kg PO q24h for 1 day (effective and safe in kittens/puppies).
- Toltrazuril 10–30 mg/kg PO q24h for 1–3 days; diclazuril 25 mg/kg PO q24h for 1 day (cats).

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Coccidiosis / Cystoisosporosis)

**Public health:** G. duodenalis, Cryptosporidium, T. gondii, B. coli, E. histolytica — zoonotic potential. Immunosuppressed owners: avoid contact with animal feces.

### Ch 197 — Systemic Protozoal Diseases

#### BABESIA

**Species and geography:**

*Large Babesia (2.4 × 5 microns):*
- Babesia canis: Europe/Asia; Dermacentor ticks. Most disease-causing.
- Babesia vogeli: Americas, southern Europe, Africa; R. sanguineus. Least pathogenic — US dogs often subclinical.
- Babesia rossi: sub-Saharan Africa only; H. leachi/H. elliptica. Most severe.

*Small Babesia (1–2.5 microns):*
- Babesia gibsoni: Asia origin; H. tick in Asia; in US — direct transmission via dog bites, vertical, blood transfusion (up to 55% prevalence in kenneled Pit Bulls in Alabama). Associated with fighting dogs.
- Babesia conradae: California and other southern US; coyote fighting.
- Babesia vulpes: North America.

**Clinical signs:** Subclinical to fulminant. B. vogeli (US): often subclinical unless immunosuppressed. B. canis, B. rossi, B. conradae, B. gibsoni: anemia, fever, pallor, icterus, splenomegaly. B. rossi/B. canis severe: neurologic signs, rhabdomyolysis, AKI from erythrocyte adherence and immune glomerulonephritis. DIC/ITP.

**Lab:** Regenerative anemia (PCV may normalize over 2–3 months with B. gibsoni). Severe thrombocytopenia early; mild thrombocytopenia can persist months. Coombs' positive. Hyperbilirubinemia; hypoalbuminemia; elevated liver enzymes.

**Diagnosis:**
- Wright's/Giemsa stain blood smear: intraerythrocytic organism (signet shape; paired pear-shape forms in large Babesia). Insensitive after initial parasitemia.
- PCR: most sensitive and specific; species-specific assays for B. canis, B. conradae, B. gibsoni, B. vulpes, B. vogeli; sequencing for unnamed species. Genus-wide assays available.
- Serology (IFA or ELISA): for B. vogeli and B. gibsoni; limited cross-reactivity among species (B. conradae not reliably detected). Rising titers over 2–3 weeks confirm recent infection.

**Treatment:**
- B. conradae, B. gibsoni, B. vulpes: **Azithromycin 10 mg/kg PO q24h + atovaquone 13.3 mg/kg PO q8h for 10 days.** Relapses occur. Reserve for clinically ill dogs only (treatment selects resistant strains).
- B. vogeli, B. canis, B. rossi: **Imidocarb dipropionate 5–6.6 mg/kg IM or SQ, twice 14 days apart (or 7.5 mg/kg once).**
- B. rossi and relapsing B. gibsoni (suspected atovaquone resistance): **Diminazene aceturate 3.5 mg/kg IM once ± clindamycin 25 mg/kg PO q12h.**
- Dogs can remain persistently infected even after treatment.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Babesiosis (DIS-BD-BABS)

#### CYTAUXZOON

**Organism / Pathogenesis:**
- Cytauxzoon felis: tick-borne protozoan of domestic cats. Vector: Amblyomma americanum (primary US vector). Geographic: southeastern, mid-Atlantic, Midwest US. Season: May–September. Reservoir: North American bobcat (subclinical).
- Two-phase: schizogonous phase (mononuclear cells in spleen/lung/liver/lymph nodes → vascular occlusion → shock, DIC) then intra-erythrocytic phase (hemolytic anemia).

**Clinical signs:** Peracute, rapidly fatal; ~90% mortality untreated. Anorexia, lethargy, fever → hypothermia. Dehydration, icterus, pale mucous membranes. Pancytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, elevated liver enzymes, hypoalbuminemia; hyperglycemia possible. Coagulopathy with prolonged clotting times.

**Diagnosis:** Cytologic examination of blood smear for piroplasms (intra-erythrocytic) + large schizonts in mononuclear cells. PCR: preferred diagnostic. Serology: not typically helpful (too short incubation).

**Treatment:**
- **Atovaquone + azithromycin: ~60% survival** (vs. 26% with imidocarb dipropionate — significantly superior).
- Intensive care: IV fluids, nutritional support, antiprotozoal medications.
- Dosing not specified for atovaquone/azithromycin in cats in text; extrapolation from Babesia protocol.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Cytauxzoonosis)

#### LEISHMANIA

**Organism / Vector / Geography:**
- >30 species; visceral leishmaniosis caused by L. infantum (dogs; primary reservoir in Old World).
- Vectors: Phlebotomus sandflies (Old World); Lutzomyia sandflies (New World).
- Most North American cases: imported from Europe or Latin America. Visceral disease reported in US dogs with no travel history (foxhounds via direct transmission). Blood transfusion transmission documented.
- Reportable in US. Treatment is controversial (rarely curative; risk of antiprotozoal resistance selection).

**Pathogenesis:** Promastigotes (sand fly injects) → macrophages → amastigotes (replicate) → disseminate to reticuloendothelial system if cell-mediated immunity fails. Strong T-cell response = elimination; weak CMI + strong humoral IgG = clinical disease.

**Clinical signs:** Alopecia, scaling, nodules/papules (cutaneous); fever, muscle wasting, splenomegaly, immune-mediated polyarthritis, anterior uveitis, glomerulonephritis; non-regenerative anemia, lymphopenia; hyperglobulinemia (mono- or polyclonal gammopathy).

**Diagnosis:** PCR preferred (excellent sensitivity). Cytology/histopathology (direct organism identification). Serology: many dogs remain seropositive after clearance — challenging interpretation.

**Treatment:** Dual therapy allopurinol + meglumine antimonial (pentavalent antimonial) for weeks to months. Miltefosine (FDA-approved for human leishmaniasis): clinical trials suggest reasonable option for dogs. Artesunate (FDA-approved antimalarial): randomized field trial positive results.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Leishmaniasis (DIS-INFECT-LEISHM)

#### HEPATOZOON

**Species:**
- Hepatozoon canis: Europe, Asia, Africa, South America; vector: R. sanguineus. Less severe.
- Hepatozoon americanum: USA; vector: Amblyomma maculatum (Gulf Coast tick). Infection by ingestion of infected ticks or infected mammalian prey. H. americanum: encysted in skeletal muscle → pyogranulomatous myositis; profound periosteal proliferation on radiographs (especially proximal appendicular skeleton).

**Clinical signs:** Wasting disease; fever, apparent pain/lameness, mucopurulent ocular discharge. Lab: nonregenerative anemia, marked leukocytosis (H. americanum hallmark — profound neutrophilia), hypoglycemia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated ALP. Osteoproliferative lesions on radiographs.

**Diagnosis:**
- Blood smear: gamonts within granulocytes (insensitive; low parasitemia in H. americanum). [See Figure 197.1B]
- ELISA: sensitive and specific for both H. americanum and H. canis.
- PCR on whole blood/buffy coat: most sensitive; differentiates species.

**Treatment:** Difficult; relapsing infections. **Decoquinate 10–20 mg/kg PO q12h** achieves prolonged remission (best evidence). Potentiated sulfonamides + clindamycin + pyrimethamine: some response but frequent relapse. NSAIDs for pain management. Spontaneous remissions documented but unexplained.

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Hepatozoonosis)

#### TOXOPLASMA GONDII

**Organism / Life cycle:**
- Obligate intracellular coccidian; cats = definitive host (sexual cycle → oocysts in feces).
- 3 life stages: sporozoites (in environmental oocysts, sporulate in 1–5 days), tachyzoites (rapid replication, dissemination), bradyzoites (tissue cysts, chronic persistence).
- Transmission to cats: primarily ingestion of prey with tachyzoites or bradyzoites.
- Oocyst shedding: 3–10 days prepatent (bradyzoite ingestion) or 19–48 days (oocyst ingestion). Only shed for days to weeks after primary infection; repeat shedding very rare.
- Warm-blooded vertebrates (incidental hosts) infected by: oocysts, bradyzoites in tissue, or tachyzoites transplacentally. Blood transfusion/transplant: rare.
- Seroprevalence: ~30–40% cats, ~20% dogs in US.

**Clinical signs:**

*Cats:*
- GI phase clinical disease rare (10–20% of experimentally infected cats develop diarrhea).
- Systemic disease (disseminated tachyzoites): lethargy, anorexia, fever → hypothermia, peritoneal effusion, icterus, tachypnea, cough. Uveitis (anterior ± posterior), chorioretinitis, optic neuritis.
- Reactivation risk: glucocorticoid therapy (NOT cyclosporine or retroviral infection — these do not appear to increase reactivation risk).

*Dogs:*
- Systemic dissemination (usually immunosuppressed dogs): respiratory signs, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, icterus, cutaneous disease.
- Neuromuscular: ataxia, seizures, tremors, cranial nerve deficits, paresis/paralysis. Rapid ascending tetraparesis possible. Myositis: weakness, stiff gait, muscle wasting.
- Myocarditis → ventricular arrhythmias.
- Uveitis, chorioretinitis, keratitis (less common than in cats).

**Lab:** Non-regenerative anemia, neutrophilic leukocytosis or neutropenia, lymphocytosis, monocytosis, eosinophilia; proteinuria, bilirubinuria, hyperglobulinemia, hyperbilirubinemia; elevated CK, ALT, ALP, lipase.

**Diagnosis:**
- Tachyzoites in clinical specimens: rarely found; not distinguishable from N. caninum.
- T. gondii oocysts in feces (10 × 12 microns): cats with diarrhea → intestinal phase infection. Cannot distinguish from Besnoitia or Hammondia.
- Serology: IgG (lifelong positive after infection) + IgM (high titer >1:64 suggests recent/active infection). **Diagnosis requires: high IgM titer or fourfold rise in IgG + compatible clinical signs + clinical response to antimicrobials with anti-Toxoplasma activity.** Aqueous humor or CSF antibody detection + PCR = most accurate for ocular/CNS toxoplasmosis.
- PCR on CSF, effusions, BAL, aqueous humor, blood (dogs/cats).

**Treatment:**
- **Clindamycin hydrochloride** (dose not specified in this chapter; typically 10–12 mg/kg PO/IM q12h for 4 weeks); or
- **Trimethoprim-sulfonamide combination**; or
- **Azithromycin** — all have been effective for clinical toxoplasmosis.
- Pyrimethamine + sulfa: effective human treatment but causes toxicosis in cats.
- Uveitis: topical, oral, or parenteral glucocorticoids to prevent secondary glaucoma/lens luxation.
- Duration: recommended minimum 4 weeks.

**Zoonosis:** Human risk greater from contaminated food or environment than from direct cat contact. Seronegative pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals: clean litter box daily, wear gloves when gardening, wash produce.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Toxoplasmosis (DIS-INFECT-TOXO)

#### NEOSPORA CANINUM

**Organism / Epidemiology:**
- Distinct from T. gondii (identified as separate species 1988). Dogs = definitive host (sexual cycle → oocysts shed by dogs). Tissue cysts in cattle, sheep, goats, horses, deer.
- Naturally occurring infection: dogs, cattle (cattle on farms with dogs have much higher prevalence).
- Worldwide; prevalence estimated 17% (range 2% to >50%); higher in rural/farm dogs.
- Transplacental transmission well documented; infected bitches repeat transplacental infection in subsequent pregnancies.
- Oocysts sporulate within 24 hours of passage.
- Cats: seropositive but no confirmed clinical disease in naturally infected cats.

**Clinical signs (dogs):**
- Congenitally infected puppies (most severe): ascending paralysis with hyperextension of pelvic limbs, muscle atrophy → generalized weakness, dysphagia, death.
- Polymyositis, multifocal CNS disease (cerebellum, cerebral cortex, brainstem).
- Post-natal infection in dogs up to 15 years old.
- Myocarditis, ulcerative dermatitis, pneumonia, hepatitis in some.
- Immunosuppressed dogs: reactivation or severe new infection.
- Elevated serum CK and aspartate transaminase (myositis); nonregenerative anemia.

**Diagnosis:**
- CSF: moderately to markedly elevated protein, mild-marked mononuclear pleocytosis.
- MRI: multifocal CNS disease typical.
- N. caninum tissue cysts: wall thickness >1 micron (T. gondii <1 micron — key differentiating feature).
- Immunohistochemistry or PCR: differentiates from T. gondii.
- IgG antibody titers ≥1:200 in most affected dogs; minimal cross-reactivity with T. gondii on IFA.
- N. caninum antibodies in CSF of some affected dogs.
- Tachyzoites rarely identified on cytology (CSF, dermatologic lesions, BAL).

**Treatment:**
- Trimethoprim-sulfadiazine + pyrimethamine; or
- Sequential clindamycin hydrochloride + trimethoprim-sulfadiazine + pyrimethamine; or
- Clindamycin alone.
- Note: clindamycin does not appear to clear infection; improvement only. Newer: artemisone showing promising in vitro results.
- Prognosis: guarded for severe neurologic involvement. Glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressives may predispose to worse infection.
- Spay affected bitches (pregnancy reactivates infection → clinical neosporosis in litters).

**Zoonosis:** N. caninum antibodies detected in some people (including AIDS/neurologic disease) but no isolates from humans — zoonotic link unclear. Avoid canine feces and undercooked meat.

> ClinIQ: ✓ Toxoplasmosis/Neosporosis (DIS-INFECT-TOXO) — Neospora-as-distinct: ✗ MISSING as separate entry (candidate: Neosporosis-distinct)
