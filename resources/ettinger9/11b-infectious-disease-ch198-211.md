### Ch 198 — Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — DIS-INFECT-FIV)

**Etiology & Epidemiology**
- Lentivirus (Retroviridae); lifelong infection. Transmitted primarily by bite wounds during territorial fighting. Horizontal spread between stable housemates is rare (unlike FeLV). Vertical/perinatal transmission uncommon. No known zoonotic risk.
- Seroprevalence: <2.5% to >14% depending on region/population. Average age at infection: 5–6 years. Risk factors: adult, male, free-roaming, crossbred, sick cats.

**Clinical Stages**
1. Acute (2–8 weeks): transient viremia, lymphadenopathy, fever
2. Chronic subclinical (several years): low viral load, progressive CD4+ T-cell decline
3. Terminal (weeks): immunodeficiency-like syndrome — wasting, severe cytopenias, opportunistic infections

**Clinical Signs**
- Majority of FIV-infected cats remain free of significant disease and have a normal life expectancy.
- Chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS) — linked to FIV infection.
- Lymphoma risk increased 5–6 fold (vs. 60-fold with FeLV); typically high-grade, extranodal B-cell tumors.
- Immune deficiency: atypical bacterial, viral, parasitic infections; mycobacterial infection, pulmonary cryptococcosis, demodicosis, cowpox.
- Neutropenia, lymphopenia common in advanced disease.
- Neurologic signs: neuropathology studied as HIV model; incidence of neurologic signs in natural infection unknown.

**Diagnosis**

*Test of Choice:* POC (point-of-care) ELISA or lateral flow immunochromatography detecting **anti-FIV antibodies** — high sensitivity and specificity.

Key interpretation rules (Ch 198):
- In cats >6 months of age NOT vaccinated against FIV: positive antibody = marker of infection.
- Negative result: most likely uninfected, but if recent exposure not ruled out → **retest in 60 days** (seroconversion timing variable).
- False positive risk higher in low-risk cats (indoor, purebred, young); confirm with alternative POC kit or PCR.
- **Maternal antibodies** can persist for months; kittens <6 months with positive titer → retest after 6 months, or use PCR.
- **Post-vaccination interference:** SNAP Combo FeLV Ag/FIV Ab (IDEXX) cannot reliably distinguish vaccination vs. natural infection; WITNESS FIV-FeLV and Anigen Rapid kits can distinguish with 100% specificity (after annual booster vaccination).
- **PCR for FIV:** Adjunct to serology (not screening); diagnostic sensitivity 5–15% LOWER than serology; use when terminal disease with negative serology suspected, or to clarify maternal antibody vs. infection in kittens.
- Terminal stage: some cats become seronegative as antibody production fails → PCR on these cats.

**Prognosis**
Long-term survival in FIV-infected cats is comparable with uninfected cats. Euthanasia of healthy FIV-positive cats is not justified.

**Treatment**
- No cure; no antiviral treatment routinely recommended.
- Zidovudine (AZT): 3′-azido-3′-deoxythymidine — some benefit reported; adverse effects include anemia, neutropenia, GI signs. **Monitor CBC weekly for first month, then monthly.** Discontinue if hematocrit drops below 20%. AZT-resistant FIV mutants can develop as early as 6 months.
- Feline interferon-omega (rFeIFN-omega, Virbagen Omega): some improvement in clinical parameters reported but convincing clinical evidence lacking. **Feline interferon-omega 10^6 IU/kg SQ q24h** may be considered (see also FeLV Box 199.2).
- Raltegravir (10–25 mg/kg PO q12h): integrase inhibitor, may be considered for recurring infections.
- Glucocorticoids: use judiciously — counterintuitive in immunosuppressed cats but may be indicated for immune-mediated conditions.
- FIV-associated neurologic signs: zidovudine (5 mg/kg PO q12h) — extrapolated from FeLV data.
- FIV-associated anemia: consider darbepoetin (off-label) in moderately or severely anemic cats.
- Bactericidal antibiotics preferred; extended or repeat courses may be required; avoid griseofulvin (risk of severe neutropenia).

**Prevention**
- Confine FIV-infected cats indoors; house singly or with other FIV-infected cats.
- FIV vaccine (Fel-O-Vax FIV, Boehringer Ingelheim): discontinued in USA/Canada 2015; available in Australia, New Zealand; considered "non-core" (WSAVA 2016); field studies suggest limited protection.

### Ch 199 — Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-INFECT-FELV

**Etiology & Prevalence**
- Retrovirus; p27 capsid antigen abundant in plasma of persistently infected cats — basis of diagnostic POC tests.
- USA prevalence: 2.3–3.3%. Higher in multi-cat, free-roaming, sick, intact male cats.

**Four Infection Outcomes ("Balance of Power")**
1. **Progressive** (worst): continuous viremia, antigenemia, rapid disease, ↓ lifespan. Median survival: 3.1 years (range 0.6–6.5 years). ~30% of cats exposed to FeLV-shedding cat for several weeks develop this.
2. **Regressive**: effective immune response suppresses virus; p27 antigen transient (detectable 2–8 weeks then clears); proviral DNA persists (may remain positive for 12+ years). Rarely develops FeLV-associated disease unless reactivated. Can still transmit via blood transfusion.
3. **Abortive**: complete clearance; only anti-FeLV antibodies remain; no provirus.
4. **Focal (atypical)**: virus sequestered in tissue; alternating/discordant test results; rare.

**Disease Associations (Progressive Infection)**
- Bone marrow suppression: most common syndrome — especially pure red cell aplasia (non-regenerative anemia, ↑ MCV, absent reticulocytes).
- Lymphoma: 60-fold increased risk vs. uninfected cats; mediastinal lymphoma most commonly associated.
- Immunosuppression → opportunistic infections, chronic gingivostomatitis.
- Panleukopenia-like syndrome (FeLV-C): mimics FPV infection.
- Neuropathy: anisocoria, mydriasis, central blindness, Horner's syndrome, urinary incontinence — likely direct neurotoxic effects.

**Diagnosis (Ch 199)**

*Primary test:* **POC for soluble p27 antigen** (ELISA or immunochromatographic) — detects progressive (and some regressive) infection. Use serum or plasma (whole blood may give more false positives in some studies).

Timing: p27 antigen becomes positive **3–6 weeks** after exposure (occasionally much longer).

*Confirmation of positive POC:* **PCR for FeLV proviral DNA** — preferred confirmatory method. If PCR unavailable, repeat antigen test with different manufacturer's kit.

*IFA (immunofluorescence assay) on blood smear*: detects intracellular p27 in neutrophils/platelets; NOT positive in first 3 weeks; not recommended for screening; requires experienced personnel.

*RT-PCR*: detects replicating viral RNA; available on blood or saliva samples. Saliva RT-PCR correlates well with antigenemia.

*Anti-FeLV antibodies (p15E PoCT — Europe)*: detects abortively infected cats; first routine commercial test available in Europe, insufficient field validation data as of publication.

Negative predictive value: **94% to ~100%** for negative antigen tests. If recent exposure <6 weeks cannot be ruled out → **retest ≥6 weeks after last potential exposure**.

*Diagnostic tree summary:*
- Negative p27 + no recent exposure risk → FeLV unlikely
- Positive p27 → immediately retest for proviral DNA (PCR); if PCR+ = confirmed FeLV infection; if PCR- → retest p27 at ≥6 weeks (may be focal infection if persistently p27+/PCR-)

**FeLV blood donor screening:** Every blood donor cat must be screened for BOTH antigen AND proviral DNA (regressive cats can transmit via transfusion).

**Treatment Recommendations (Box 199.2)**
- No clinical signs/lab changes: no treatment; strict indoor confinement.
- Identify and treat underlying/secondary diseases first.
- **Lymphoma:** COP protocol (cyclophosphamide, vincristine [Oncovin], prednisone); prognosis more guarded.
- **Anemia:** Blood transfusion if severe; consider glucocorticoids (immune-mediated component possible in both regenerative and non-regenerative anemia); rule out underlying treatable causes.
- **Neurologic signs:** Treat underlying diseases (e.g., lymphoma); if no underlying disease → **zidovudine 5 mg/kg PO q12h**.
- **Recurring infections:** Treat proactively; consider **feline interferon-omega 10^6 IU/kg SQ q24h** and/or **raltegravir 10–25 mg/kg PO q12h**.
- Avoid glucocorticoids, immunosuppressive, or myelosuppressive drugs in progressively/regressively infected cats unless clear indication.
- CBC every 6 months; biochemistry profile and urinalysis every 12 months.
- Surgery generally well-tolerated; use perioperative antibiotics.

**Vaccines**
- FeLV vaccine: non-core; recommended only for cats at risk of exposure. Duration of immunity ≥2 years; booster every 3 years adequate for cats >2 years. Does NOT prevent provirus integration (regressive infection still possible). Non-adjuvanted vaccines preferred (↓ injection-site sarcoma risk).

### Ch 200 — Coronavirus Infection (FCoV/FIP and CCoV)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-INFECT-FIP (FIP section) — **Important antiviral update**

#### Feline Coronavirus (FCoV) / Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP)

**Pathogenesis**
- FCoV is endemic in multi-cat environments; most cats develop subclinical enteritis (FECV biotype). In ~5% of infections, mutations (primarily in S protein, ORF3c, ORF7b) allow robust replication in blood monocytes/macrophages → FIP (FIPV biotype).
- **Wet ("effusive") FIP**: predominant Th2 humoral response → protein-rich effusion in thoracic/abdominal cavities; rapidly progressive (days to death); ADE (antibody-dependent enhancement) contributes.
- **Dry ("non-effusive") FIP**: mixed Th1/Th2 response; more protracted; granulomatous lesions in eyes and brain predominate.
- Risk factors: kittens/young cats <2 years (majority of cases); pedigreed male cats; stressed/overcrowded multi-cat environments; Abyssinian, Bengal, Birman, Devon Rex, Ragdoll breeds predisposed. About 33% of cats are resistant (SNP in IFN-gamma gene).

**Clinical Signs**
Early: fever, lethargy, anorexia, weight loss. Non-regenerative anemia, neutrophilia with left shift, lymphopenia. Hyperbilirubinemia, increased AST, decreased albumin:globulin ratio.
- Wet FIP: bilateral or unilateral pleural/abdominal effusion
- Dry FIP: uveitis (darkening iris, abnormal pupil, keratic precipitates), granulomas in brain → ataxia, tremors, seizures.

**Diagnosis (FIP) — Key Tests (Ch 200)**

*Gold standard (definitive):* **Immunohistochemistry (IHC)** of surgically biopsied tissues (liver, spleen, intestine, omentum, mesenteric LN) revealing granulomatous lesions containing FCoV antigen. Also in situ hybridization (ISH) now improving yield.

*Effusion analysis (Rivalta test — cage-side):*
- Procedure: 8 mL distilled water + 1 drop acetic acid; gently place 1 drop of effusion on surface.
- **Positive** (precipitate forms or sinks) = consistent with FIP; **negative** (effusion dissipates into clear solution) = FIP unlikely.
- Characteristic FIP effusion: straw-colored, sticky/viscous, high protein, LOW cellularity (vacuolated macrophages and neutrophils on microscopy).

*Albumin:Globulin (A:G) ratio on effusion or serum:* **A:G <0.8** — increases suspicion for FIP (from diagnostic algorithm, Ch 200).

*Effusion cytology + immunostaining:* Thought to be highly specific when macrophages stain positive for FCoV; however, false positives from nonspecific binding and FECV viremia reported. Testing sensitivity also limited.

*RT-PCR (Auburn University test)*: Detects FCoV RNA in effusion, CSF, FNA specimens; high copy numbers in extra-GI tissue more likely = FIP. IDEXX FIPVirus Real PCR: detects S protein mutations at nt positions 1058 and 1060 (associated with systemic spread, not specific for FIP) — run in parallel with RT-PCR for 7b gene.

*Serology (FCoV antibody titers):* **NOT diagnostic** — titers do not correlate with FIP development; can be very high or low in FIP-confirmed cats. 10% of confirmed FIP cats have false-negative serology (antibody-antigen complexes).

*CSF and aqueous humor:* Can be tested with RT-PCR and immunostaining; useful for dry FIP with neurologic/ocular signs; similar limitations apply.

**Critical laboratory indicators for FIP (effusive form):**
- Lymphopenia, non-regenerative anemia, thrombocytopenia
- Hyperglobulinemia; **A:G ratio <0.8** (effusion); hypoalbuminemia
- Hyperbilirubinemia; elevated AST

**Treatment — Antivirals (MAJOR UPDATE)**

*Nucleoside analog — GS-441524 (bioactive metabolite of remdesivir):*
- Mechanism: nucleoside analog incorporated into viral RNA → premature transcription termination.
- Initial trial: recovery in 25/31 (84%) naturally infected cats.
- Clinical response: fever resolves within **12–36 hours**; appetite returns. Effusions resolve over **10–14 days**.
- Dosing: **4 mg/kg SQ q24h for 12 weeks (84 days)**.
- Higher doses may be needed for non-effusive FIP: **8–10 mg/kg**.
- Relapse occurred in 8 cats; successfully re-treated in 5.
- Most common adverse effect: skin lesions at injection site.
- NOT FDA-approved in USA at time of publication; available through regulatory agencies in other countries; unlicensed "home" GS-441524-like therapy has shown effectiveness.
- **Oral formulations now available.**
- **Remdesivir (off-label IV):** Used for FIP treatment; expensive; not widely available at time of writing.
- **Molnupiravir:** Alternative replication inhibitor; distinct mechanism (ambiguous base-pairing/mutagenesis); has "distinct safety concerns due to its mode of action."

*Protease inhibitor — GC376 (3CL protease inhibitor):*
- Related to nirmatrelir/ritonavir (Paxlovid).
- Trial: 19/20 cats with wet FIP had initial response; 7 remained in remission.
- Under development as FIP treatment.

*Palliative (without antivirals):*
- Prednisolone (anti-inflammatory), fluid support, therapeutic abdominocentesis/thoracocentesis.
- Prognosis without antivirals: grave (near-universally fatal).

**Prevention:** No effective vaccine recommended (AAFP/AAHA). Avoid overcrowding; regular litter box/food bowl disinfection.

#### Canine Coronavirus (CCoV)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — low priority (mild enteric disease in most cases)

- CCoV: primarily mild diarrheal disease in young dogs; pantropic variant (CCoV-2b) can cause severe multisystemic disease.
- Diagnosis: RT-PCR from feces/tissues; virus isolation limited to CCoV-2 strains.
- Treatment: Supportive (mirrors CPV treatment — fluids, electrolytes, antibiotics, nutrition). No antiviral drugs available.
- Vaccines available (inactivated and MLV); reduce but do not eliminate replication.

#### Canine Respiratory Coronavirus (CRCoV)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-RESP-CIRD (component pathogen)

- Betacoronavirus (distinct from CCoV/FCoV); component of CIRD ("kennel cough").
- Diagnosis: PCR from respiratory swab, or acute/convalescent serology.
- No vaccines; CCoV vaccines not cross-protective.

### Ch 201 — Canine and Feline Parvovirus Infection

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — DIS-INFECT-PARVO)

**Etiology**
- CPV (Canine parvovirus): small non-enveloped ssDNA virus; variants CPV-2a, CPV-2b, CPV-2c. Extremely stable in environment; requires 0.75% hypochlorite or potassium peroxymonosulfate for disinfection.
- FPV (Feline panleukopenia virus): cats also serve as CPV reservoir; CPV-2a/2b can cause clinical panleukopenia in cats.
- Both target rapidly dividing cells: thymus, bone marrow, spleen, intestinal crypt epithelium.

**At-Risk Animals**
- Young (8–20 weeks), unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated. Maternal antibody half-life ~10 days; susceptibility increases as maternal immunity wanes at 12–14 weeks.
- Breeds at increased risk for CPV: Rottweilers, American Pit Bull Terriers, Doberman Pinschers, English Springer Spaniels, German Shepherd Dogs.

**Clinical Signs — Dogs (CPV)**
- Incubation: 4 days (experimental) to 1–2 weeks (natural).
- Lethargy, fever, anorexia → vomiting, fetid small bowel diarrhea → melena/hematochezia.
- **Leukopenia (neutropenia)** present by time hemorrhagic diarrhea appears — hallmark finding.
- Hypovolemic shock, dehydration (isotonic/hypotonic), hypokalemia, hypoglycemia.
- Acid-base: hypochloremic alkalosis (severe) or hyperchloremic acidosis (mild).
- Bacteremia/endotoxemia (most common cause of death): E. coli recovered from lungs/liver of 90% of CPV-infected puppies.
- Hypercoagulability (documented); DIC does NOT occur.
- Cardiac CPV (now rare): myocarditis in very young (<8 weeks) pups.
- Discospondylitis and bacterial polyarthritis: post-recovery complication.

**Clinical Signs — Cats (FPV/Panleukopenia)**
- Peracute: death within 12 hours of lethargy (profound septic shock).
- Vomiting and diarrhea LESS common than in dogs.
- **Cerebellar hypoplasia** (in utero or neonatal exposure): destruction of Purkinje cells and granule precursor cells — non-progressive, degree variable.
- FPV detected by PCR in cats with cardiomyopathy in one study.

**Diagnosis**
- **Fecal parvovirus antigen ELISA** (point-of-care): detects all CPV variants; specific but can lack sensitivity. Same test used for FPV in cats.
- False positives: vaccine shedding for 4–28 days post-vaccination with modified live CPV vaccine.
- **PCR**: available; useful if ELISA negative with suggestive clinical signs; subclinical shedding of low quantities may complicate interpretation.
- Serum antibody testing: semi-quantitative ELISA for anti-CPV antibodies — used more for determining vaccination need than diagnosis.
- Always perform fecal flotation/direct smear for concurrent parasites.

**Treatment — In-Hospital (Ch 201)**

*Fluids and Electrolytes:*
- Fluid of choice: **balanced electrolyte solution isotonic to blood (e.g., lactated Ringer's solution)**.
- Restore perfusion with IV boluses as needed; replace deficits within 1–6 hours; then adjust to maintenance + ongoing losses.
- **KCl added to IV fluids** for hypokalemia; normalize before discharge.
- Hypoglycemia (especially smaller dogs): **25% dextrose bolus** IV; then supplement IV fluid with 2.5–5% dextrose.
- Colloid support if albumin drops below 2.0 g/dL (20 g/L): hetastarch.
- FFP transfusion: provides albumin, immunoglobulin, protease inhibitors; however, poor for sustained colloid oncotic pressure (large volumes required). Blood transfusion if anemia from hemorrhage, bone marrow failure, or endoparasitism.

*Nutrition:*
- **Early enteral nutrition via nasoesophageal tube** when compared with nil-per-os → earlier clinical improvement, significant weight gain, improved GI barrier function.

*Antibiotics:*
- **IV broad-spectrum bactericidal antibiotics** indicated due to GI barrier disruption and leukopenia.
- Recommended: **Ampicillin 20 mg/kg IV q8h** + **amikacin 20 mg/kg IV, IM, or SQ q24h** (maximum 5 days once proper hydration established).
- OR: short course parenteral **fluoroquinolone** (e.g., enrofloxacin) for Gram-negative coverage.
- **Metronidazole 15–20 mg/kg PO q12h for up to 10 days** or **fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO q24h for 5 days** if motile protozoa found on fecal wet preparation.

*Antiemetics:*
- **Maropitant, ondansetron, or metoclopramide** — all appear equally effective for CPV emesis. With intractable vomiting: combination therapy via CRI.

*Analgesics (abdominal pain):* Buprenorphine, fentanyl, or lidocaine-ketamine CRI.

*Other adjuncts:* Recombinant interferons (shown to significantly ameliorate enteritis in some studies; limited availability). Fecal microbiota transplantation: faster resolution of diarrhea in one study.

**Treatment — Outpatient (welfare/resource-limited settings)**
- Daily SQ or IP fluids (KCl can be added to IP fluids).
- Parenteral antibiotics: **ampicillin or cefovecin**.
- Maropitant (± metoclopramide for home administration).
- Fenbendazole PO for 5 days.
- Oral rehydration mixture + high-energy liquid diet.
- Outcome not significantly different from in-hospital in some studies.

**Prognosis**
- 10% survival untreated → >90% with aggressive in-hospital treatment.
- Negative indicators: leukopenia, lymphopenia, monocytopenia, neutropenia; SIRS at admission; hypoalbuminemia; hypothyroxinemia; elevated CRP.

**Disinfection:** 0.75% hypochlorite (1:30 household bleach for 30 minutes) or potassium peroxymonosulfate; effective against both CPV and FPV.

### Ch 202 — Rabies

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — DIS-INFECT-RABIES; zoonosis alert)

**Etiology & Epidemiology**
- Lyssavirus, family Rhabdoviridae. Virtually 100% fatal once clinical signs develop (only one well-documented human survivor of clinical rabies).
- In USA: ~7,000–10,000 animal cases/year diagnosed (~85% in USA). Major wildlife reservoirs: raccoons (northeast), skunks (midwestern/California), bats (throughout North America), foxes (Canada/Alaska).
- **Cats represent 3× more diagnosed rabies than dogs** in USA — less often vaccinated, more likely to roam/interact with wildlife.
- USA, Canada, Mexico: free of dog-to-dog rabies transmission (extinct in Mexico 2015).

**Transmission**
- Predominant route: **bite wound** from rabid animal. Contaminating open wounds with saliva or nervous tissue from rabid animal = exposure.
- NOT transmitted by contact with intact skin.
- No viremia; no shedding via urine or GI tract. Highly neurotropic.
- **10-day observation period:** If a healthy dog, cat, or ferret bites a person, the biting animal should be observed for 10 days regardless of vaccination status. If alive and well at 10 days → no risk of rabies transmission from that bite.

**Pathogenesis & Clinical Signs**
- Incubation: typically several weeks to months (range 7 days to 6 months); inversely related to viral dose/severity of exposure.
- Earliest signs: lethargy, inappetence, diarrhea, vomiting — non-specific.
- **No waxing and waning** — daily/hourly irreversible deterioration.
- Neurologic: behavioral changes (reclusive or attention-seeking), episodes of aggression ("snapping at flies"), paresthesia at bite site, excess salivation, dysphagia, anisocoria, facial/tongue paresis, altered phonation.
- End stage: profound moribundity.

**Diagnosis**
- **Direct fluorescent antibody (dFA) test on fresh brain tissue** = **gold standard**. Requires brainstem + cerebellum full cross-section (National Standard Protocol, CDC).
- Fresh tissue required — **do NOT fix brain in formalin** (causes significant diagnostic delays).
- Histology (Negri bodies, Seller's stain): less sensitive; for fixed tissue only.
- Specialized IHC for rabies: available at reference labs but less sensitive than dFA.

**Treatment**
- **None.** Once clinical signs present, disease is fatal. No recognized treatment.

**Prevention & Post-Exposure Management**

*For vaccinated animals exposed to rabies:*
- Give **booster rabies vaccination as soon as possible**.
- Observe for **45 days** (Compendium of Animal Rabies Control).
- Vaccine failure manifestation would most likely occur within 45-day observation period if it occurred.

*For unvaccinated animals exposed to rabies:*
- Recommendation: **euthanasia**.
- If owner refuses: **6-month quarantine** (duration of risk for developing rabies).
- Alternative (based on 2015 study): If overdue on booster, immediate wound cleaning + rabies booster + baseline serum rabies neutralizing antibody titer collected **within 5–7 days** of booster.

*Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for rabies-naïve humans:*
- Prompt wound cleansing + rabies immune globulin (human or equine origin) + **5 IM vaccine doses on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 28**.
- Almost 100% successful when managed promptly and appropriately.
- ~55,000 people die yearly from rabies (mainly Asia/Africa).

*Pre-exposure vaccination for humans:* Recommended for veterinarians, staff, animal handlers, lab workers; serum neutralizing antibody titer checked every 2 years. If titer <complete neutralization at 1:5 dilution (rapid fluorescent focus inhibition test) → single booster dose. Previously vaccinated person subsequently exposed: booster on days 0 and 3 only.

**Public Health / Zoonosis**
- **Zoonotic risk: YES — 100% fatal in unvaccinated humans.** Mandatory reporting varies by jurisdiction.
- Rabies in cats is reportable and constitutes a public health emergency.
- Rabies from pets is uncommon when vaccinated; can occur rarely even in currently vaccinated animals (vaccine failure, inadequate immune response).

### Ch 203 — Canine Infectious Respiratory Disease Complex (CIRD/CIRDC)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-RESP-CIRD

**Causative Agents**
*Bacteria:* Bordetella bronchiseptica (most common), Mycoplasma cynos, Streptococcus equi subsp. zooepidemicus, Pasteurella spp., Staphylococcus spp.
*Viruses:* Canine adenovirus 2, canine respiratory coronavirus (CRCoV), CDV, canine herpesvirus, canine influenza (H3N8, H3N2), canine parainfluenza, canine pneumovirus.
- H3N8 originated from horses (may be extinct in USA as of publication); H3N2 active in USA; SARS-CoV-2 can cause mild CIRD signs in dogs with close contact to COVID-19-positive humans.

**Clinical Signs**
- Acute onset cough — dry or moist; "terminal retch" (sounds like vomiting).
- History of exposure to other dogs (dog park, boarding, shelter).
- Most maintain normal appetite, attitude, exercise tolerance.
- Fever, inappetence, lethargy → suspect pneumonia (most common causes of pneumonia: S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus and B. bronchiseptica).
- Mucopurulent nasal/ocular discharge ± conjunctivitis.
- Hemoptysis/epistaxis: most common in shelter dogs with H3N2 influenza or S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus pneumonia.

**Diagnosis**
- Clinical diagnosis sufficient for individual mild cases.
- **Molecular diagnostic panels (PCR):** Recommended for outbreaks in populations (shelters, kennels, boarding); specimens from multiple affected dogs; yields pathogens including B. bronchiseptica, M. cynos, S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus, CAV-2, CDV, CRCoV, CPIV, H3N2, H3N8, canine herpesvirus, pneumovirus. NOTE: recently vaccinated dogs may test positive on PCR (vaccine virus = wild-type virus not distinguished).
- **Culture + antimicrobial susceptibility:** Airway washings ideal for pneumonia cases.
- Serology (acute + convalescent): useful for determining outbreak causation when PCR falsely negative due to rapid cessation of shedding.

**Treatment (ISCAID Working Group Guidelines)**

*Antibiotics — First-line:*
- **Doxycycline 5 mg/kg PO q12h OR 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 7–10 days** — recommended for 16/17 ISCAID panelists; safe for puppies ≥4 weeks; active against B. bronchiseptica and Mycoplasma spp.
- Alternate: **Amoxicillin-clavulanate 11 mg/kg PO q12h** for B. bronchiseptica infection; also effective for S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus and secondary bacterial agents.
- **Amoxicillin 22 mg/kg PO q12h**: adequate for Pasteurella spp. and Streptococcus spp. infections.
- Oral antibiotics: 7–10 days generally sufficient.
- Consider antibiotics only within 10-day observation period if fever, lethargy, or inappetence present.

*Severe/Bacteremia/Sepsis:*
- **Enrofloxacin 5–20 mg/kg SQ or IM** + **clindamycin 10 mg/kg SQ** (or beta-lactam) while awaiting culture results.

*Duration:* Re-evaluate after 10–14 days; radiographic improvement lags behind clinical response in pneumonia.

**Vaccination**
- Core vaccines (adenovirus 2, CDV, CPIV) protect against CIRD agents.
- Canine influenza H3N2 and H3N8 vaccines available; may reduce severity.
- B. bronchiseptica: injectable, intranasal, and oral vaccines available; oral vaccine: duration of immunity >1 year, comparable to intranasal on 7-day challenge.
- Vaccination does NOT prevent colonization and shedding — morbidity generally decreased.

**Public Health**
- B. bronchiseptica: can infect immunocompromised people.
- S. equi subsp. zooepidemicus: rare zoonosis.
- H3N2 and H3N8: not believed to infect people currently.
- SARS-CoV-2 in dogs: reverse zoonosis from infected humans; low shedding, short duration.

### Ch 204 — Canine Distemper

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-INFECT-CDV

**Etiology**
- CDV: enveloped RNA virus, Paramyxoviridae, Morbillivirus; large host range (Canidae, ferrets, coyotes, skunks, raccoons, large felids). Does NOT infect domestic cats. Zoonotic transmission not demonstrated.
- Incubation: <1 week to 6 weeks PI; usually clinical signs within 2 weeks.

**Clinical Signs — Biphasic Pattern**
- *First phase (week 1–3 PI):* Fever (coinciding with immunosuppression), mucopurulent ocular discharge, blepharospasm, conjunctivitis — can resemble benign respiratory illness.
- *Respiratory:* Viral + secondary bacterial pneumonia (B. bronchiseptica, Streptococcus spp., Pasteurella multocida, E. coli, Staphylococcus spp., mycoplasmas).
- *GI:* Vomiting, diarrhea (± tenesmus, bloody feces), intussusception (uncommon).
- *Skin:* Pustules, measles-like rash (small %), digital/nasal hyperkeratosis ("hardpad disease"), keratoconjunctivitis sicca.
- *Ophthalmic:* KCS, retinal degeneration/necrosis (grey-pink densities on fundoscopy → post-infection hyper-reflective areas), optic neuritis, sudden blindness (rare).
- *Neurologic:* Myoclonus ("chewing gum" seizures — petite mal), grand mal seizures; ataxia; can develop concurrent with systemic signs, after resolution, or rarely >1–3 weeks later.

**Prognosis**
- Dogs without neurologic disease: best prognosis for recovery.
- **Neurologic signs: guarded to grave.** Mild neurologic signs (e.g., myoclonus) may be manageable; neurologic disease often progressive and irreversible.

**Diagnosis**
- **RT-PCR** (most widely used): nasal, deep pharyngeal, or conjunctival swab specimens; or blood. High sensitivity — **carefully control cross-contamination** (false positives possible). Cannot discriminate vaccine virus from wild-type. Positive RT-PCR confirms distemper is possible but does NOT prove viable/virulent/infectious virus.
- After vaccination, dogs may remain RT-PCR positive; duration and cutoff level not established. Quantitative RT-PCR (CT value near 40 = low viral load — may reflect mild, early, or resolving infection, or vaccine virus).
- **Virus Isolation on Vero-DST cells:** CPE visible 24 hours post-inoculation; complete CPE within 48–72 hours. Absence of live virus = definitive evidence virus not present. Not widely available clinically. VI not positive beyond 14–16 days in experimentally infected dogs (mean duration: 1.3 days for nasal swabs, 2.2 days for rectal swabs).
- **Fluorescent antibody (FA) test:** Detects CDV in tissue sections, conjunctival swabs, rectal mucosa swabs, leukocytes.
- **IHC:** On biopsy specimens; intranuclear and intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies diagnostic.
- **Serology (IgM/IgG):** Not reliable in field/shelter settings (vaccination confounds); useful for immunologic status assessment in outbreak settings. Positive titer + negative RT-PCR = good evidence of immunity.

**Treatment**
- No antiviral available; supportive care only.
- Fluid/electrolyte replacement; nutrition; antibiotics for secondary bacterial infections.
- B vitamins administration may have value.
- Antiemetics for vomiting.
- Anticonvulsants for seizures (outcome rarely good for neurologic signs beyond myoclonus).

**Vaccination**
- MLV vaccines: highly effective, rapidly induce strong protective immunity.
- 1 hour post-vaccination exposure: most dogs develop only mild-moderate signs (vs. unvaccinated).
- Maternally derived antibody (MDA) can interfere in puppies <20 weeks; revaccinate at intervals until MDA cleared.
- Safe recombinant canarypox rCDV vaccine for pregnant dogs (does not infect immunologically naïve fetuses).
- One serotype only → MLV vaccines from late 1950s/60s remain effective against all biotypes.

### Ch 205 — Feline Upper Respiratory Infections (FURI)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-INFECT-URTI

**Pathogens**
- *Primary viral:* FHV-1 (feline herpesvirus-1) — ubiquitous; latency in trigeminal ganglia; reactivation by stress (occurs 4–11 days post-stressful event). FCV (feline calicivirus) — high mutation rate; no single persistent carrier state but widespread exposure/reinfection.
- *Bacterial (secondary/primary):* Mycoplasma felis, Chlamydia felis (conjunctiva — obligate intracellular, sheds 60 days post-infection), Bordetella bronchiseptica.
- Coinfections common; clinical signs overlap.

**Clinical Signs**
- Sneezing (most common), nasal discharge (serous → mucopurulent/hemorrhagic), nasal congestion/stertor, ocular discharge, chemosis, blepharospasm, conjunctivitis.
- **FHV-1 hallmark:** Dendritic corneal ulceration (stained with fluorescein) — **only pathognomonic sign** of FHV-1 infection.
- **FCV hallmark:** Oral ulceration (lingual/lip); also acute lameness; VS-FCV (virulent systemic strain) → severe systemic disease with high mortality.
- **C. felis:** Conjunctivitis without concurrent nasal signs (or minimal).
- **B. bronchiseptica:** Cough (can extend to lower respiratory tract).
- Uncomplicated FHV-1: lasts 10–21 days. Adult recrudescence: milder (conjunctivitis, epiphora, mild sneezing).

**Diagnosis**
- Specific pathogen identification usually unnecessary in uncomplicated individual cases.
- **PCR panels (oropharyngeal/conjunctival swabs):** Recommended for outbreaks in shelters/catteries; FHV-1, FCV, C. felis, M. felis, B. bronchiseptica.
- Culture from conjunctival/oropharyngeal swabs (not nasal secretions) for bacterial species.
- Cytology of conjunctival smears: may show intracytoplasmic inclusions (C. felis).
- Virus isolation, IFA, ELISA: available but limited utility in acute uncomplicated cases.

**Treatment**

*Antibiotics (when indicated — worsening, unresolving with fever, lethargy, hyporexia):*
- **First-line: Doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q24h for 7–10 days** (coat with lubricating substance and/or follow with liquid/food; compounded liquid formulation loses efficacy after 7–10 days). Active against B. bronchiseptica, C. felis, Mycoplasma spp.
- Alternative: other antimicrobials per ISCAID respiratory guidelines.

*Antiviral for FHV-1 (severe acute/unresolving):*
- **Famciclovir 90 mg/kg PO q8–12h for 7 days** (prodrug of penciclovir; suggested starting dosage). Currently no safe antiviral for FCV.
- **Topical ophthalmic antivirals:** Idoxuridine, trifluridine, cidofovir — for corneal ulceration; idoxuridine and trifluridine require frequent application and poorly tolerated.

*Supportive care:*
- Appetite stimulants: **transdermal or oral mirtazapine** or **cyproheptadine**.
- Nebulization/airway humidification; saline nasal flushes (human infant formulation) twice daily.
- Clean nasal/ocular discharge.

*Immune modulators (adjunctive, mixed evidence):*
- Modified live FHV-1/FCV intranasal vaccine (immune modulation/cross-protection).
- Probiotics (Enterococcus faecium SF68), nutraceuticals, liposome TLR agonist complex.
- L-lysine: evidence against efficacy (systematic review concludes not effective).
- Feline or human recombinant interferon: mixed results.

**Disinfection**
- Most environmentally resistant pathogen is FCV; disinfect accordingly.
- 1:32 dilution of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (household bleach) effective against FCV; inactivated by sunlight, ineffective with organic material.
- **Preferred:** Accelerated hydrogen peroxide compounds — effective against FCV, safer, shorter contact time, maintain efficacy with some organic material.

**Prognosis**
- Most uncomplicated FURI: very good prognosis. FCV and FHV-1 positive cats not restricted to single-cat households. Morbidity severe in debilitated/immunocompromised/very young.

### Ch 206 — Emerging Viral Infections of Cats

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate for awareness; sub-diseases below)

**Domestic Cat Hepadnavirus (DCH)**
- Hepatitis B-like virus discovered in Australia 2018; 6.5–12.4% of cats test positive for virus DNA.
- Evidence linking DCH to chronic hepatitis and hepatocellular carcinoma in cats: detected in 43% (6/14) chronic hepatitis biopsies and 28% (8/29) hepatocellular carcinomas.
- Zoonotic transmission: extremely unlikely. No direct cat-to-cat transmission.

**Feline Morbillivirus (FeMV)**
- Associated with tubulointerstitial nephritis (major cause of cat death); causality evidence incomplete/conflicting.
- Detected worldwide by RT-PCR of urine/blood and serology.

**Felis catus Gammaherpesvirus 1 (FcaGHV1)**
- Prevalence of viremia: <6% to 23.6%; more common in adult, intact male cats with FIV/FeLV/hemoplasma coinfections.
- Most infections subclinical; no confirmed role in feline lymphoma as yet.

**Feline Bocavirus (FBoV)**
- Three genotypes; respiratory/enteric disease associations under investigation.

### Ch 207 — Cryptococcosis

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-RESP-CRYPTO

**Etiology**
- Cryptococcus neoformans species complex (worldwide; most dog infections; C. neoformans sensu stricto); C. gattii species complex (Australia, NZ, SE Asia, western North America — 50% of Aus dog/cat infections; nearly all CA cat infections).
- Dimorphic: filamentous in environment; encapsulated yeast (3.5–7 μm diameter, 1–20 μm thick capsule) in host.
- Source: pigeon droppings (C. neoformans), eucalyptus/tree litter (C. gattii). **No direct animal-to-animal or zoonotic transmission.**
- Polysaccharide capsule: major virulence factor — inhibits phagocytosis, depletes complement, shifts host from Th1 to Th2 response.
- Most infections occur in seemingly immunocompetent animals; FeLV/FIV co-infection may worsen course.

**Clinical Signs**

*Cats (most commonly infected):*
- Chronic progressive upper respiratory tract infection: sneezing, mucopurulent to hemorrhagic nasal discharge.
- Firm swelling or proliferative ulcerated cutaneous lesion over bridge of nose (~50% of cats with nasal involvement).
- Cutaneous/subcutaneous ulcerated draining nodules (~50%).
- **Ocular:** Chorioretinitis and optic neuritis (up to one-third of cats) — strongly associated with CNS involvement.
- **Neurologic:** ~20% — seizures, mentation change, circling, ataxia, cranial nerve deficits.
- Less common: lower respiratory (pleural effusion), osteomyelitis.

*Dogs:*
- 80% have multi-organ involvement. German Shepherd Dogs, Doberman Pinschers, Great Danes (Australia); American Cocker Spaniels (USA). Most <4 years.
- Lethargy, weight loss, anorexia predominate. **Up to 75% have neurologic signs.**
- Upper respiratory signs, ulcerated skin lesions, lymphadenomegaly, GI signs, chorioretinitis, lameness (osteomyelitis).

**Diagnosis**

*Test of Choice:* **Cryptococcal Antigen Latex Agglutination System (CALAS)** (also called LCAT — latex cryptococcal antigen test).
- Detects polysaccharide capsular antigens.
- Sensitivity: **95–100%** (cats); slightly lower in dogs (USA).
- Specificity: **100%** in cats.
- Pre-treat specimens with pronase to reduce false positives.
- **False positives:** Subclinical nasal colonization can cause titers up to 1:200 in cats. If titer <1:200, confirm with cytology/histopathology.
- Titer range in infected animals: 1:2 to >1:524,000.
- CALAS titers correlate with disease severity (but not mortality). **4-fold decrease in first 2 months = positive treatment response.**
- Use a single laboratory for monitoring (considerable variability between labs).

*POC Assays:*
- **CrAg lateral flow assay (IMMY Diagnostics):** Sensitivity 92–100%, specificity 81–93% vs. CALAS in cats/dogs. May detect antigen when CALAS has become negative during treatment (more sensitive during treatment monitoring). **Widely used as screening test of choice.**
- **CryptoPS (Biosynex):** Semiquantitative cassette assay; sensitivity 80%, specificity 95% vs. CALAS.

*Cytology/Histology:* **Standard for diagnosis** — cytological or histological identification of Cryptococcus yeasts.
- Specimens: nasal swabs, impression smears of ulcers, aspirates, effusions, CSF, urine.
- Stains: New methylene blue, Romanowsky, Gram (identify stained cell with non-staining capsule).
- India ink: highlights unstained yeasts vs. dark background; risk of confusion with lymphocytes/fat droplets.
- **Sensitivity: 50–75%** for cytology; negative does not rule out diagnosis.
- Narrow-based budding yeast on cytology.
- CSF: organisms in up to 90% of infected dogs with CNS involvement.

*Fungal culture:* Grows on Sabouraud's dextrose agar in 2–10 days. Do not diagnose based on nasal cavity culture alone (subclinical colonization occurs).

*PCR:* Available; not typically necessary; subclinical colonization can cause positive results in apparently healthy animals.

**Treatment**

*Disseminated disease or CNS involvement:*
- **Amphotericin B for 4 weeks** (induction). Lipid-complexed formulation less nephrotoxic (not more efficacious) — higher dosages tolerated.
- **5-flucytosine 250 mg/cat PO q8h** — synergistic with amphotericin B; **optimal treatment for cryptococcosis in cats** (this combination). Cannot be used as sole agent (rapid resistance). 5-flucytosine NOT recommended for dogs (induces toxic epidermal necrolysis within 2 weeks).
- If 5-flucytosine unavailable: **fluconazole** in combination with amphotericin B.
- After 4 weeks induction: transition to azole monotherapy.

*Localized nasal infection in cats — Azole monotherapy:*
- **Fluconazole 10 mg/kg PO q12h OR 50 mg/cat PO q12h** — good ocular and CNS penetration; limited adverse effects; **initial azole treatment of choice**.
- If fluconazole fails (titers remain high): **Itraconazole 5–10 mg/kg PO q24h (capsules)** or **3–5 mg/kg PO q24h (oral solution)** — less CNS penetration but has successfully treated cryptococcal meningitis.
- **Terbinafine 10 mg/kg PO q24h** — used in combination with azoles anecdotally.
- Voriconazole: toxic in cats due to impaired hepatic metabolism — **avoid in cats**.
- Posaconazole: activity against Cryptococcus; further studies needed for dogs/cats.
- Azole monotherapy NOT recommended in immunocompromised animals or suspected disseminated disease.

*Treatment Duration and Follow-up:*
- Duration ranges from **2 to 12 months** (median 4 months) in cats; longer for dogs.
- Continue 2 months beyond clinical resolution and first negative CALAS.
- Recheck + CALAS every 3–6 months for 2 years after discontinuation.
- Relapse: typically within 2 years of stopping; can occur up to 10 years later.
- Success rate: **75% of cats** (50% with CNS involvement, 84% without); 50% of dogs.
- Initial worsening with therapy (inflammatory response to dying fungi) is NOT a negative prognostic indicator.
- Worsening clinical/neurologic status in first 4 days of therapy with supportive care maintaining stability → not negative prognosis.

### Ch 208 — Coccidioidomycosis

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — DIS-INFECT-COCCI; geographic/regional)

**Etiology & Epidemiology**
- Coccidioides immitis (central California, New Mexico) and C. posadasii (New Mexico, elsewhere) — soil-borne fungi; dimorphic (mycelium in environment, spherules in tissue).
- Endemic: southwestern/western USA (California central valley ["Valley Fever"], Arizona — Tucson and Phoenix), eastern Washington state, Mexico, Central/South America.
- Infection follows cycle: moist conditions → dry period → soil disruption (construction, earthquakes, dust storms).
- Dogs of either sex, any breed/age can be affected; young adults at greatest risk.
- Predisposed breeds: Weimaraners, Dalmatians, Hungarian Vizslas, Norfolk Terriers, Greyhounds.
- Risk factors in dogs: outdoor housing, roaming >1 acre, walking in desert, digging (6.7-fold increased risk), travel to/residence in endemic region (4.4-fold increased risk).
- Cats: less commonly affected; most reported from Arizona; ~50% have skin lesions.
- Most infections in endemic areas are subclinical.

**Pathogenesis**
- Inhaled arthroconidia → phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages → enlarge into spherule (8–100 μm) → hundreds of endospores (3–5 μm) released when spherule ruptures → pyogranulomatous/granulomatous response → surviving endospores disseminate or form new spherules.

**Clinical Signs**
- Subacute to chronic course; signs present for months to years before diagnosis.
- Intermittent fever (range 103–106°F [39.4–41.1°C]; typically low-grade), lethargy, inappetence, weight loss.
- Respiratory: harsh cough, increased respiratory effort, tachypnea, exercise intolerance.
- Dissemination sites: bone, CNS, skin, peripheral lymph nodes, pericardium. Less commonly: joints, eyes, testes, GI, prostate, liver, spleen, urinary.
- **Bone:** Nonspecific pain, lameness, firm swellings on appendicular/axial skeleton.
- **CNS:** Obtundation, blindness, nystagmus, absent menace, ataxia, postural deficits, pacing, circling, cervical pain, seizures.
- **Ocular:** Chorioretinitis, uveitis, endophthalmitis.
- **Pericarditis:** Right-sided heart failure, ascites, pleural effusion.
- Cats: approximately 1/3 have respiratory signs, 20% lameness, ocular, or neurologic signs.

**Lab Findings**
- Mild non-regenerative anemia, mild neutrophilia, hypoalbuminemia.
- ~50% of dogs/cats are hyperglobulinemic.
- Proteinuria (60% of dogs), urine protein:creatinine ratios up to 9.5 (reference <0.5).
- CSF (CNS involvement): protein generally <300 mg/dL (<3 g/L); nucleated cells usually <50 cells/mcL; mixed or neutrophilic pleocytosis.

**Diagnosis (Algorithm, Ch 208)**

*Serology (antibody tests — primary diagnostic tool):*
- Gel immunodiffusion for IgM (tube precipitin) or IgG (complement fixating).
- IgM detectable within **2–5 weeks** of infection; IgG after **8–12 weeks**.
- Quantitative IgG by immunodiffusion: titers typically 1:2 to 1:256 (median 1:32).
- Titer of **1:16 or lower**: interpret with caution (especially in hyperendemic Arizona where healthy dogs can have titers up to 1:16).
- Titers **≤1:2** = treatment success target.
- False negatives: rare in California dogs; described in Arizona dogs.
- **POC lateral flow assay (sona Coccidioides, IMMY):** High sensitivity and specificity vs. gel immunodiffusion; useful initial test in endemic region dogs; further validation required.
- Urine Coccidioides antigen: **unacceptably low sensitivity (≤20%) — NOT recommended.**

*Cytology/Histology:*
- Aspirates or lavage specimens: granulomatous or pyogranulomatous inflammation; spherules (deeply basophilic, 8–70 μm diameter with endospores) visible in some cases.
- Histopathology: special stains (PAS, GMS) recommended.

*Culture:* Health hazard for laboratory personnel; only in suitably equipped labs; warn lab of suspicion.

**Treatment**

*Pulmonary / localized disease:*
- **Fluconazole 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h** OR **itraconazole 5 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- Itraconazole preferred for bone involvement and when fluconazole fails (greater activity).

*Refractory / disseminated disease:*
- Deoxycholate or lipid-complexed amphotericin B (alone or combined with azole).
- Voriconazole or posaconazole: used for refractory coccidioidomycosis in people; excellent CNS penetration; consider for refractory meningoencephalitis if finances permit.

*CNS involvement:*
- Concurrent glucocorticoids and anti-seizure medications may be necessary.
- Topical prednisolone acetate + anti-glaucoma agents for ocular lesions.

*Duration:* Minimum **6 months** to potentially lifelong (disseminated). Treatment until clinical resolution and titers ≤1:2. Dogs with pulmonary-only disease: best prognosis. Monitor liver function monthly during treatment; do not use compounded formulations.

*Surgical:* Amputation for persistent osteomyelitis; enucleation for endophthalmitis; subtotal pericardiectomy for pericarditis (perioperative mortality 24%).

**Public Health:** Not transmitted between infected animals/people. Dogs used as sentinel species for human exposure. Inoculation coccidioidomycosis can follow bites from infected animals or contaminated instruments. Necropsies: suitable PPE + immediate incineration of carcass (conversion to mycelial form can occur post-death). Burial not recommended (environmental contamination).

### Ch 209 — Blastomycosis and Histoplasmosis

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — DIS-INFECT-BLASTO and DIS-INFECT-HISTO)

**Etiology**
- *Blastomycosis:* Blastomyces dermatitidis, B. gilchristii, B. helicus ("atypical" blastomycosis). Thermally dimorphic; mold in environment (<30°C), budding yeast in body (>33°C). Not contagious; infection by inhaled conidia.
- *Histoplasmosis:* Histoplasma capsulatum (≥5 cryptogenic species; H. mississippiense [NAm1] and H. ohioense [NAm2] in USA). Thermally dimorphic.
- Blastomyces: moist, acidic, sandy soil enriched with decaying plant matter, near waterways, wet-then-dry conditions.
- Histoplasma: soil rich in nitrogen, adequate moisture, bird or bat droppings.

**Epidemiology**
- Blastomycosis: enzootic to eastern North America (Great Lakes, St. Lawrence River, midwestern, mid-Atlantic, southeastern, south central USA; Canada — provinces bordering Great Lakes and Saskatchewan).
- Histoplasmosis: worldwide; high prevalence in western edge of enzootic region (KS, OK, TX); overlaps with blastomycosis.
- Blastomycosis: much more common in dogs than cats. Dogs at greatest risk: outdoor exposure, 2–5 years, 20–30 kg, sporting/working breeds, living within 400–500 m of waterways; late spring to early fall.
- Histoplasmosis more common in cats than blastomycosis; ~1/3 of affected cats live exclusively indoors.
- Mean age of affected animals: 7 years (cats), 3 years (dogs) for histoplasmosis.

**Clinical Signs**

*General (both):* Lethargy, fever, anorexia, weight loss.

*Respiratory:* Cough more common in blastomycosis (>60% of dogs). Only ~10% of histoplasmosis cats/dogs exhibit cough despite ~50% having respiratory involvement.

*Eyes (exam required for all suspected cases):* Chorioretinitis, retinal detachment, anterior uveitis, secondary glaucoma, conjunctival hyperemia, corneal opacity, blindness. Posterior segment most often involved in histoplasmosis; both segments in blastomycosis. **82% of cats with histoplasmosis had ocular involvement** (one study of 55 cats).

*Skin:* Cutaneous/subcutaneous nodules ± ulceration and draining tracts. Blastomycosis more common in dogs; histoplasmosis skin lesions common on head/neck of cats. Mucocutaneous lesions may mimic eosinophilic plaques or stomatitis/glossitis.

*Bone/Joint:* Blastomycosis more common than histoplasmosis in dogs. Histoplasmosis: ~20% of cats; most common cause of polyarthritis in enzootic areas; carpi/tarsi and adjacent distal metaphyses most common. Severe bone pain; multi-modal analgesia required.

*GI (Histoplasmosis):* Large-bowel diarrhea in ~50% of dogs; can manifest as proctitis; GI less common in cats with histoplasmosis and rare in dogs with blastomycosis. GI histoplasmosis biomarkers: ↑ anti-porin IgA, anti-calprotectin IgA, anti-gliadin IgA — overlap with IBD; **rule out GI histoplasmosis before starting immunosuppression for IBD in enzootic areas.**

*CNS:* Blastomycosis: more common in cats (~40%). Multifocal or localized signs.

**Lab Findings**
- CBC: neutrophilic leukocytosis (± left shift), monocytosis; occasionally severe lymphocytosis (>15,000/mcL) in histoplasmosis.
- ~50% of dogs/cats with histoplasmosis are anemic; mild-moderate thrombocytopenia common (13–48%).
- Biochemistry: hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia. Hyperbilirubinemia in ~50% with histoplasmosis.

**Imaging**
- Thoracic radiographs: variable; ~50% show "classic" diffuse interstitial pattern; also tracheobronchial/sternal lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion (7–12%).
- Abdominal US (histoplasmosis): free abdominal fluid; hepato-splenomegaly with mottled echotexture; mesenteric lymphadenopathy; GI wall thickening/loss of layering in descending colon (indistinguishable from lymphoma/IBD).
- CNS MRI (blastomycosis): hypo-/isointense T1, hyperintense T2, contrast-enhancing lesions (granulomas, meningitis, ventriculitis, myelitis).

**Definitive Diagnosis**

*Cytopathology/Histopathology:*
- **Blastomyces yeasts:** 8–15 μm diameter, thick double-refractile wall, **single broad-based budding** (distinguishes from Cryptococcus). Found extracellularly in dense pyogranulomatous inflammation.
- **Histoplasma yeasts:** 2–6 μm diameter, thin clear rim, eccentrically placed crescent-shaped basophilic nuclei. Found in **clusters within macrophages**. Hemophagocytosis common — search thoroughly for organisms.
- Highest-yield specimens for blastomycosis: **peripheral lymph node FNA and skin lesion swabs/impressions**. Rectal scrapings for histoplasmosis in dogs.
- Special stains: GMS (Grocott methenamine silver) or PAS for histopathology.

*Antigen testing (galactomannan [GM] — urine is BEST):*

Blastomycosis (Table 209.1):
- **Antigen EIA, urine: sensitivity 93%, range 88–100%; specificity 98%, range 95–100%.**
- Antigen EIA, serum: sensitivity 91%, range 87–100%; specificity 100%.
- Antibody EIA (recombinant Blastomyces adhesin-1, MiraVista): sensitivity 82%, specificity 97%.

Histoplasmosis (Table 209.2):
- **Antigen EIA, urine (canine): sensitivity 92%, range 89–95%; specificity 99%, range 99–100%.**
- Antigen EIA, urine (feline): sensitivity 94%; specificity 98%, range 97–100%.
- Antigen EIA, serum (feline): sensitivity 73%.
- Antibody EIA (canine): sensitivity 77%; specificity 93%.
- NOTE: **High cross-reactivity between Blastomyces and Histoplasma** on antigen assays; lesser cross-reactivity with Coccidioides, Sporothrix, Aspergillus; **no cross-reactivity with Cryptococcus.**

Urine antigen testing recommended every 3 months during treatment, then annually after. **Discontinue treatment when urine antigen ≤0.4 ng/mL** on 2 consecutive rechecks (dogs with blastomycosis). Continue treatment until no antigen detected in urine in cats with histoplasmosis.

*Fungal culture:* Up to 30 days; health hazard; low sensitivity (12–67%); rarely used clinically.

*Itraconazole:* Drug of choice for mild-moderate (not immediately life-threatening) disease. High animal-to-animal variability in absorption → **therapeutic drug monitoring required** to guide individualized dosing. Adverse effects (hepatotoxicosis, ulcerative skin lesions — itraconazole-induced vasculitis) related to toxic blood concentrations.

*Fluconazole:* Reasonable alternative when itraconazole not tolerated. Acquired resistance has only been demonstrated with fluconazole. Unclear if better than itraconazole for ocular/CNS/prostate-testicular involvement.

*Amphotericin B (lipid/liposomal):* Drug of choice for **severe, life-threatening disease** (fungicidal; rapid therapeutic concentrations). Must be followed by azole therapy.

*Voriconazole and posaconazole:* **Salvage therapy** (progressive disease after 2–3 months itraconazole). Terbinafine 30–40 mg/kg/day + azole: less expensive salvage option.

*Glucocorticoids:* Short course at anti-inflammatory doses (**0.25–1 mg/kg/day prednisolone or equivalent**) for: ocular/CNS involvement, severe respiratory disease, with amphotericin B administration, persistent tracheobronchial lymphadenopathy causing chronic cough. Retrospective studies failed to show survival benefit.

*Duration:* **Minimum 6 months**; disseminated disease often >1 year. Stop only when: 6+ months completed, 1 month past resolution of all clinical signs/exam abnormalities, imaging resolved, urine antigen negative or very low.

**Prognosis:** Survival to hospital discharge: 87–90%. At 1–2 months: 75%. At 6 months: 65–69%. Relapse after treatment: ~20% for blastomycosis; 20–40% for histoplasmosis.
Negative prognostic indicators: CNS involvement, severe respiratory disease requiring O2 supplementation, extensive lung involvement.

### Ch 210 — Sinonasal and Sino-orbital Aspergillosis

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NASAL-ASP

**Etiology**
- **SNA (sinonasal aspergillosis):** Aspergillus fumigatus (most common dog/cat). Also A. niger complex, A. flavus, A. nidulans in dogs; cryptic A. fumigatus species (e.g., A. lentulus) in cats.
- **SOA (sino-orbital aspergillosis):** Almost exclusively in cats; caused by cryptic Aspergillus viridinutans subgenus species — especially **Aspergillus felis** and A. udagawae. Invasive; extends from sinonasal cavity to orbit.

**Signalment**
- Dog breeds overrepresented for SNA: dolichocephalic large breeds (Golden/Lab Retrievers, German/Belgian Shepherd Dogs, Rottweilers, Malamutes, Rough/Border Collies), Bull Terriers, Staffordshire Terriers, Miniature Schnauzers.
- Brachycephalic cat breeds overrepresented: Persians, Himalayans, Exotic/Scottish/British Shorthairs, Ragdolls.
- Median age at diagnosis: 5–7 years. Slight male predisposition in dogs.

**Clinical Signs**

*SNA (dogs and cats):*
- Chronic serous to mucopurulent nasal discharge; sneezing; reverse sneezing/stertor.
- Crusting or ulceration of nasal planum; hyperkeratosis/depigmentation of nasal planum (dogs).
- Mandibular lymphadenomegaly; muzzle pain.
- Severe disease: bilateral discharge ± epistaxis.
- Rarely: seizures (cribriform plate erosion with meningeal inflammation).

*SOA (cats only):*
- **Unilateral exophthalmos** with prolapse and inflammation of nictitating membrane.
- Central corneal ulceration, blepharospasm, conjunctival discharge.
- Nasal signs may be subtle.
- Swelling in pterygopalatine fossa adjacent to last molar tooth (open-mouth exam): may expose white-to-yellow orbital granuloma.
- Hard palate ulceration; facial swellings.
- Advanced: vision loss from ulcerative keratitis or optic nerve/chiasm infiltration.

**Diagnosis**

*Definitive:* **Cytological or histological detection of fungal hyphae** in plaques attached to sinonasal mucosa (SNA) or invading mucosal/paranasal tissues (SOA).

*Imaging (before rhinoscopy):* CT or MRI (prevent imaging artifacts from iatrogenic hemorrhage).
- SNA-CT: turbinate lysis, increased nasal cavity/sinus soft-tissue opacity, thickened mucosal rim, frontal bone hyperostosis, mandibular/retropharyngeal lymphadenopathy. Cribriform plate, palatine, orbital, frontal bone lysis possible.
- SOA-CT: ventromedial orbital mass ± sinonasal changes. Heterogeneous contrast enhancement with lobulated hypoattenuating foci and peripheral rim enhancement.

*Endoscopy:* Nasopharyngoscopy (flexible) → antegrade rhinoscopy (rigid). Typical findings: cavernous nasal cavity (turbinate destruction), mucopurulent secretions, fungal colonies/plaques adherent to mucosa. A. fumigatus colonies: white before sporulation → grey-green after sporulation.

*Cytology (endoscopic brush specimens or squash preparations):*
- **Correctly identified hyphae in 93% from brush specimens and 100% from squash preparations of biopsied plaques.**
- Hyphae identified in only 20% from blind nasal swabs.
- Characteristic: mats of branching, septate fungal hyphae attached to mucosa or within exudates; lymphoplasmacytic ± neutrophilic inflammation background.

*Aspergillus-specific IgG serology:* Excellent specificity; sensitivity variable → **not reliable as stand-alone test.**

*Fungal culture:*
- Sensitivity: 88% (fungal plaques), 75% (distal nasal mucosal biopsies), 19% (blind nasal swabs); specificity 100% for all methods.
- Definitive species identification requires molecular PCR (macroscopic/microscopic morphology unreliable). MALDI-TOF MS alternative but limited library availability.
- Visible growth within 4 days on Sabouraud's dextrose agar or malt extract agar.

**Treatment — Canine SNA**

*Prerequisite:* **Mechanical debridement** of ALL visible fungal plaques (endoscopic forceps + copious saline irrigation) before topical antifungal.

*Topical antifungal protocols:*
- **Clotrimazole 1% solution or cream** OR **Enilconazole 1% or 2% solution** — applied into nasal cavity and frontal sinuses after debridement.
- Avoid propylene glycol (causes severe mucosal edema/airway obstruction) — use **polyethylene glycol (PEG)** carrier instead.

*Enilconazole protocol comparison (retrospective, 48 dogs):*
- Group 1 (24 dogs): **2% enilconazole, 1-hour infusion** via sinus catheter, 90° rotation every 15 minutes, then 1% bifonazole cream instillation into sinuses.
- Group 2 (24 dogs): **2% enilconazole, 15-minute infusion** without rotation or bifonazole cream.
- Results: Simplified protocol quicker (median 92 min vs. 201 min); **overall treatment success 96% for BOTH groups**.

*Clotrimazole protocols:*
- 1% clotrimazole cream or solution (via endoscopy or trephination) after plaque debridement: overall success **94% to 100%** in reported studies.
- One study using Seldinger technique (rigid urinary catheter over guidewire, endoscopically placed in frontal sinus): success 94%; eliminated need for trephination.
- **Fluconazole is NOT recommended for SNA** (minimal activity against molds).

*Cribriform plate defects:* Topical treatment can still be attempted (small case series without complications using 1% clotrimazole or 1% enilconazole); systemic triazoles (itraconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole) ± terbinafine as alternative/adjunct for cribriform plate defects, failed topical therapy.

*Response assessment:* Endoscopic reevaluation **2–4 weeks after treatment** (CT unreliable since bony changes persist; serology persists for months to years after cure). If plaques visualized at recheck → repeat debridement + topical azole + endoscopic evaluation 2–4 weeks later. **Median: 2 treatments for cure; overall success 94–100%.**

*Long-term prognosis:* Confirmed relapse/reinfection: 11–20%, 2–36 months after cure. Persistent serous/mucopurulent discharge after mycological cure: common (52% in one study) — usually bacterial rhinosinusitis secondary to turbinate destruction.

**Treatment — Feline SNA:** Similar to canine SNA (endoscopic debridement + topical imidazole infusion). Frontal sinuses require trephination or sinusotomy if involved. Imidazole solutions preferred over depot cream (cream causes nasal obstruction; cats reluctant to mouth-breathe).

**Treatment — Feline SOA (Systemic antifungals required, ≥6 months):**
- **Antifungal susceptibility testing required** (intrinsic resistance common in cryptic Aspergillus spp. A. felis/A. udagawae).
- Empirical: **posaconazole + terbinafine** (causative agents typically have low MICs to posaconazole; itraconazole and voriconazole MICs often HIGH for A. felis → itraconazole NOT recommended for SOA).
- **Voriconazole: severe adverse effects in cats** (paraplegia, blindness, anorexia) — avoid.
- Posaconazole: well-tolerated; hepatotoxicosis uncommon. Therapeutic drug monitoring of trough plasma concentrations useful to ensure MICs reached.
- Amphotericin B: used successfully in susceptible isolates (usually combined with posaconazole or posaconazole/terbinafine) — empirical therapy NOT recommended (variable MICs).
- **Caspofungin** (echinocandin; inhibits beta-1,3-D-glucan synthesis): used successfully with posaconazole or posaconazole/terbinafine; well-tolerated IV.
- Prognosis: **guarded to poor** for SOA cure. Comprehensive orbital debridement with enucleation has NOT improved outcomes.

### Ch 211 — Disseminated Invasive Aspergillosis (DIA) and Systemic Mold Infections

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — DIS-INFECT-DIA)

**Etiology**
- DIA: multisystemic infection by Aspergillus spp. in Circumdati subgenus — most commonly **A. deflectus, A. terreus, A. fumigatus, A. niger** in dogs. Distinct from SNA/SOA agents.
- Other opportunistic molds: Rasamsonia spp. (Trichocomaceae/Eurotiales — similar to Aspergillus clinically; reported in 8 dogs; primarily German Shepherd Dogs), Talaromyces spp., Scedosporium spp. (cause osteomyelitis and polyarthritis).
- DIA rare in cats — typically associated with immunosuppression (chemotherapy, FIV/FeLV co-infection).

**Epidemiology**
- Primarily in animals with immune compromise (known or suspected).
- **Breed predisposition: German Shepherd Dog and Rhodesian Ridgeback** — likely genetic/uncharacterized immunodeficiency. Multiple other opportunistic mold cases also in German Shepherd Dogs.
- Also: dogs on immunosuppressive drugs (chemotherapy, glucocorticoids, cyclosporine).
- Young adult animals in predisposed breeds. Female predisposition reported in one study.
- A. deflectus and A. terreus: most often implicated in DIA (vs. A. fumigatus/A. felis in SNA/SOA).

**Clinical Signs**
- Diverse; depend on site(s) of infection.
- Lethargy, anorexia universal; fever in ~25% of affected dogs.
- Common: apparent pain, lameness, vestibular signs, lymphadenopathy.
- **Ophthalmic signs often PRECEDE apparent systemic illness**: anterior uveitis (flare, keratic precipitates), chorioretinitis (hypo-/hyperreflective tapetal lesions, retinal separation), optic neuritis, panophthalmitis.
- Typical sites: brain, eye, vertebrae/intervertebral discs (discospondylitis in ~50%), kidneys, liver, spleen, lymph nodes.
- Renal: polyuria/polydipsia, hematuria, proteinuria, isosthenuria, pyuria, funguria.
- Neurologic: altered mentation, proprioceptive ataxia, seizures, vestibular signs.
- Respiratory: pleural effusion, enlarged tracheobronchial/sternal lymph nodes, pulmonary infiltrates (less common).
- Intracardiac vegetative lesions: associated with fungemia and thromboembolic disease.

**Diagnosis**

*Imaging:*
- Spinal radiographs: discospondylitis in ~50% of DIA cases (narrowed disc spaces, vertebral endplate lysis, sclerosis, periosteal reaction).
- Abdominal US: renal papillary blunting, pyelectasia, echogenic pelvic debris; splenic hypoechoic nodules/granulomas; lymphadenomegaly.
- MRI (brain): multifocal hypo-/isointense T1, hyperintense T2, contrast-enhancing lesions; normal MRI does not rule out brain disease.

*Serology — Galactomannan (GM) antigen EIA (Platelia, Bio-Rad):*
- **Serum: sensitivity 92%; specificity 93%** for diagnosis of DIA.
- Urine: slightly lower sensitivity.
- **False positives:** oral probiotics containing Aspergillus-derived ingredients, penicillin, some crystalloid fluids buffered with fungal-culture-derived products. High-level false positives from other mold or dimorphic fungal infections (cross-reactivity).
- Gel immunodiffusion for anti-A. fumigatus antibodies: **lacks sensitivity for DIA** (not recommended).
- Beta-D-glucan (Glucatell): marketed for early detection of invasive fungal infections; data validating use in dogs/cats NOT available.

*Microbiologic testing:*
- **Gold standard: fungal culture of specimens from normally sterile sites** (IMA — inhibitory mold agar) or microscopic identification + molecular identification.
- FNA of affected lymph nodes, liver, spleen, vertebral bodies, other bony lesions, urine → submit for fungal culture and/or cytology.
- **Urine culture: positive in ~50% of dogs with DIA.**
- Blood culture on IMA to detect fungemia.
- **PCR-sequencing required to identify isolates to species level** (morphologic similarities between Aspergillus spp. and other invasive molds prevent reliable identification by culture morphology alone).
- Antifungal susceptibility testing: specialist labs; no established clinical breakpoints for companion animals but useful for identifying likely resistance.

*Cytology/Histopathology:*
- Aspergillus: thin-walled, septate, branching hyphae ~5 μm diameter. Refractile with H&E; more visible with GMS or PAS stains.
- Pyogranulomas: neutrophils, sheets of multinucleated giant cells, central necrosis with fungal hyphae.
- Aleuriospores (laterally branching spherical vesicles) = suggests A. terreus specifically.
- Specimens: FNA of lymph nodes, spleen, kidney, liver, vertebral endplates; BAL fluid, effusions, urine sediment.

**Treatment and Prognosis**
- **Prognosis: typically grave.** Advanced disease and compromised immunity at diagnosis. In one study of 30 dogs, ~2/3 euthanized within 1 week of diagnosis.
- Dogs receiving antifungal treatment: survival 2 days to 25 months.
- **Posaconazole (10 dogs with DIA):** Median survival 241 days; all but 1 dog ultimately succumbed to infection.
- Dogs on immunosuppressive drug therapy: longer survival if disease diagnosed before widespread dissemination AND immunosuppressive therapy can be discontinued.
- Systemic antifungal selection: based on species identification + antifungal susceptibility testing (variable susceptibility; intrinsic resistance in some species).

**Public Health:** Not considered zoonotic. Aspergillus spp. are shared environmental pathogens. Dogs with DIA do not shed infectious propagules.
