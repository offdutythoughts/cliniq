Chapters 212–222. Each disease block follows: signalment/breed predisposition → diagnostic criteria / gold-standard tests → treatment doses (verbatim) → prognosis/survival.

### Ch 212 — Clinical Evaluation of the Respiratory Tract

**Scope:** Diagnostic framework chapter; no specific disease entities or drug doses. Key reference thresholds for downstream chapters:

- **Hypoxemia** defined as PaO2 < 80 mm Hg (arterial blood gas gold standard for oxygenation).
- **A-a gradient** normally < 15 mm Hg on room air; elevated indicates V/Q mismatch, diffusion barrier, or R→L shunt.
- **Minimum radiographic pleural fluid** (medium-sized dog): ~100 mL before fluid apparent on radiograph.
- **CT pulmonary nodule detection:** radiography limit ≈ 7–9 mm; CT reliably detects nodules ≥ 1 mm.
- **Breathing pattern rules:** extrathoracic obstruction → predominantly inspiratory effort + inspiratory noise; intrathoracic obstruction (small airway disease, intrathoracic tracheal collapse) → predominantly expiratory effort + expiratory noise.
- **Paradoxical breathing** in dyspneic dogs and cats strongly correlated with pleural space disease.
- **Canine cough quality localizer:** "goose honk" → central airways (trachea/mainstem bronchi); crescendo cough preceding deep inspiration → alveoli/small airways.

> ClinIQ: This is a diagnostic-approach chapter only; no standalone disease entry needed. Content directly supports triage within existing sign pages (cough, dyspnea, etc.).

### Ch 213 — Diseases of the Nose, Sinuses, and Nasopharynx

---

#### Idiopathic Lymphoplasmacytic Rhinitis (LPR)

> ClinIQ: ✓ NASAL-LPR — enrichment warranted.

**Signalment/breed:**
- Most common form of chronic non-infectious rhinitis in dogs; ~20% of dogs with nasal discharge (30% in some series).
- Middle-aged, larger-breed dogs; males and females equally affected; normocephalic > brachycephalic.
- Also ~30% of cats with nasal discharge.

**Diagnosis:**
- Diagnosis of exclusion. CT + endoscopy (rigid rhinoscopy) + bacteriology/mycology/histopathology required to rule out all other causes.
- No unique cytological or histopathological marker; diagnosis requires ruling out neoplasia, aspergillosis, foreign body, OND.
- Positive bacterial culture does NOT confirm primary bacterial rhinitis (primary bacterial rhinitis is extremely rare in dogs).

**Treatment:**
- No commonly accepted protocol; must be individualized.
- Options include: secretolytics, long-term antibiotics with immunomodulatory activity (e.g., doxycycline), corticosteroids or NSAIDs, desensitization, immunosuppression.
- Treatment often yields little or only temporary success.

**Prognosis:** Unfavorable for cure; progressive if untreated, may lead to secondary upper and lower respiratory sequelae.

---

#### Sinonasal Malignancy (Nasal Neoplasia)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NASAL-NEO — significant enrichment warranted.

**Signalment/breed:**
- ~30% of dogs with nasal discharge have nasal neoplasia (10% prevalence in oncology clinics; third most common indication for radiation treatment).
- ~40% of cats with nasal discharge have nasal neoplasia.
- 80–90% of nasal masses are malignant.
- Dogs: 2/3 are carcinomas (most commonly adenocarcinoma); 1/3 sarcomas (chondrosarcoma most common, then fibrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, undifferentiated/anaplastic sarcoma).
- Cats: lymphoma and adenocarcinoma are most common.
- Primarily locally invasive; metastasis uncommon and late.
- Middle-aged to old dogs; all ages can be affected.
- Dolichocephalic breeds overrepresented for nasal aspergillosis (Ch 212).

**Diagnosis:**
- Advanced examination under general anesthesia required for definitive diagnosis.
- CT scan → endoscopically guided biopsy of suspicious tissue (mandatory in suspected tumor; blind biopsies cause high rate of false-negative results — many neoplasms are initially misdiagnosed as inflammatory disease).
- Endoscopically guided biopsy preferred over blind biopsy; ensures representative tissue, minimizes injury to nasal structures and large vessels.

**Treatment:**
- Cure is rarely possible regardless of therapy; prolongation of survival is the realistic goal.
- **Untreated nasal carcinoma (dogs):** median survival 3.1 months (n=139 dogs) (Ch 213).
- **Radiation therapy (RT):** Tumor prescription of 42–54 Gy in 10–15 fractions = most effective means of achieving local control; median survival ranging from 8 to 19.7 months (multiple studies).
- RT local control limited because most failures occur within the original tumor mass; most dogs ultimately die from local disease progression.
- Chemotherapy as sole therapy not recommended; tyrosine kinase inhibitors under investigation in combination with RT.
- Surgical endoscopic tumor ablation (endonasal endoscopic tumor ablation [EETA]): primarily palliative; case reported alive and free of clinical signs at 24 months (chondrosarcoma).
- **Cats:** palliative hypofractionated radiotherapy; survival data limited.

**Prognosis:** Long-term prognosis poor. Most dogs die from local disease.

#### Sinonasal Aspergillosis

> ClinIQ: ✓ NASAL-ASP — note for enrichment.

- ~10% of dogs and ~5% of cats with nasal discharge.
- Antibiotic therapy without definitive diagnosis is contraindicated — can dramatically worsen aspergillosis.
- Diagnosis and treatment covered in detail in Ch 210 (not Sec XII); treatment referenced here for context only.

---

#### Nasal Foreign Body

> ClinIQ: ✓ NASAL-FB

- ~10% of dogs and ~10% of cats with nasal discharge.
- Acute onset sneezing and facial pawing; can persist chronically.
- Diagnosis: endoscopic detection under anesthesia.
- CT superior to radiographs for localization.

---

#### Nasopharyngeal Polyp

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-POLYP — note enrichment available.

- In cats: ~15% of cases with nasal discharge; includes nasopharyngeal polyps, stenoses.
- Clinical signs: stertor, reverse sneezing, dysphagia, otological signs (Eustachian tube involvement).

---

#### Nasopharyngeal Stenosis

> ClinIQ: ✓ NASAL-NPS

- Congenital or acquired (post-inflammatory, post-surgical, traumatic).
- High recurrence rate after treatment (any method).
- Treatment options: surgical mucosal advancement, balloon dilation (Ch 114); balloon-expandable metallic stents reported (Ref 105).
- Greatest problem: restenosis.

#### Brachycephalic Obstructive Airway Syndrome (BOAS)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — significant content, strong clinical need)

**Signalment/breed:**
- Intentionally bred hereditary disease; all conformationally exaggerated breeds.
- Dogs: English Bulldog, French Bulldog, Pug, Boston Terrier, Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, Shih Tzu, Lhasa Apso, Pekingese, Chihuahua, Yorkshire Terrier (miniaturization variant).
- Cats: Persian, Exotic Shorthair, British Shorthair, Himalayan.
- Oronasal defect (OND) prevalence ~57% in brachycephalic dogs vs. normocephalic dogs (malformed dental arch).
- Laryngeal collapse occurs as early as 4.5 months of age in brachycephalic puppies; more commonly in adults.

**Pathophysiology:**
- Obstructions at 3 segmental levels: nasal entrance (stenotic nares, nasal vestibule malformation), nasal cavity (intranasal turbinate aberrations, caudally-growing aberrant turbinates [CATs]), nasal exit (nasopharyngeal meatus narrowing, elongated/thick soft palate causing nasopharyngeal collapse, hamulus bottleneck stenosis).
- Nasopharyngeal meatus in small dogs: physiologic size ~5 mm (Yorkshire Terrier, 3 kg); extreme narrowing ~1 mm seen in Chihuahua (1.1 kg) with clinical signs.
- Chronic negative pressure → progressive laryngeal collapse (stages 1–3; see Ch 214).
- Impaired thermoregulation: reduced nasal airflow → reduced evaporative cooling → heat susceptibility/collapse.
- Aerodigestive disorders very common in brachycephalic dogs; pre-surgical GI treatment improves post-operative outcomes.

**Laryngeal collapse grading (from Ch 214):**
- Stage 1: eversion of laryngeal saccules into glottis — present in 50–60% of dogs with BOAS.
- Stage 2: cuneiform processes lose rigidity, collapse into laryngeal lumen; aryepiglottic folds collapse ventromedially.
- Stage 3: corniculate processes of each arytenoid fatigue and collapse toward midline — complete laryngeal collapse.

**Diagnosis:**
- CT preferred (better than radiographs for intranasal and pharyngeal detail; guides rhinoscopy targets and surgical planning).
- Rhinoscopy (rigid, 0°, 2.7 mm; or 1.9 mm for small dogs/cats).
- Laryngeal examination under light anesthesia (see Ch 214 for protocols).
- Staging for laryngeal collapse requires direct laryngoscopy.

**Treatment (surgical):**
- Correction of nasal entrance stenosis (widening stenotic nares).
- Shortening of elongated soft palate.
- Resection of everted laryngeal ventricles (sacculectomy — but one study showed increased post-operative morbidity after sacculectomy for BOAS).
- Endoscopic LASER-assisted or HF-surgery techniques: avoid excessive thermal energy at nasal entrance — causes constrictive, stenosing wound healing (major complication documented).
- Combined correction "reduced clinical signs associated with brachycephaly in all cases" and "reduced respiratory events by 90%"; sleeping problems decreased markedly; respiratory sounds declined by 50%.
- Advanced laryngeal collapse (Stage 2–3): removal of everted laryngeal saccules + surgical correction of soft palate + nares; unilateral arytenoid laryngoplasty (cricoarytenoid lateralization + thyroarytenoid caudolateralization) for stage 2–3 with reasonable long-term outcomes (small numbers).
- Permanent tracheostomy: last resort; high complication rate.

**Prognosis:** BOAS clinical signs develop by ≥6 months of age; "free-breathing" parents do not guarantee absence of disease in offspring.

### Ch 214 — Laryngeal Diseases

#### Laryngeal Paralysis / GOLPP

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-LP — significant enrichment warranted.

**Signalment/breed:**
- **Acquired (most common):** primarily older (>9 years) large- and giant-breed dogs.
  - Overrepresented: Labrador Retriever (most common), Golden Retriever, Saint Bernard, Newfoundland, Irish Setter, Brittany Spaniel.
  - Hypothyroidism concurrent in up to 30% of acquired LP cases (direct causal link not established).
- **Congenital:** Bouvier des Flandres (autosomal dominant; Wallerian degeneration of recurrent laryngeal nerves), Siberian Husky, Alaskan Malamute, Bull Terrier, White-Coated German Shepherd Dogs.
  - RAPGEF6 variant = major genetic risk factor for early-onset LP in miniature Bull Terriers.
  - CNTNAP1 missense variant = associated with LP-polyneuropathy in Leonberger and Saint Bernards.
  - LP-polyneuropathy complexes described in Dalmatians, Rottweilers, Leonbergers, Saint Bernards, Pyrenean Mountain Dogs, American Staffordshire Terriers.
- **GOLPP (geriatric-onset LP polyneuropathy):** Many dogs previously classified as idiopathic LP actually have progressive generalized polyneuropathy; LP is an early sign. 10/32 dogs with idiopathic LP had neurologic signs at enrollment; ALL had neurologic signs by 1 year.
- **Cats:** LP constitutes 40% of laryngeal diseases in cats; mean age 9–14 years; both unilateral and bilateral reported; often trauma-, neoplasia-, or iatrogenic-related.

**Diagnosis:**
- Definitive diagnosis: lack of arytenoid abduction during inspiration on laryngoscopy.
- **Drugs for functional laryngeal examination (Box 214.3):**
  - Premedications: Glycopyrrolate 0.005–0.01 mg/kg IV/IM/SC + one of: Butorphanol 0.2–0.4 mg/kg IV/IM/SQ; Buprenorphine 0.005–0.02 mg/kg IV/IM/SC; Hydromorphone 0.1–0.2 mg/kg IV/IM/SC.
  - Induction: Propofol 4–8 mg/kg IV (administered slowly) OR Alfaxalone 2–4 mg/kg IV (administered slowly).
  - To stimulate respiration: Doxapram 1–2 mg/kg IV.
  - To decrease laryngeal swelling: Dexamethasone 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IV.
- Paradoxical arytenoid movement: arytenoids move inward during inspiration (negative intraglottic pressure), return during expiration — can be misinterpreted as normal; require assistant to call respiratory phase.
- Workup includes thoracic radiographs (rule out aspiration pneumonia, megaesophagus, cardiac disease), thyroid function testing, neurologic exam.

**Treatment — acute respiratory distress (Box 214.2):**
- To decrease laryngeal swelling: **Dexamethasone sodium phosphate 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IV**
- To reduce anxiety: **Acepromazine 0.01–0.02 mg/kg IV**, OR **Buprenorphine 0.0005–0.001 mg/kg IV**, OR **Butorphanol 0.1–0.25 mg/kg IV**

**Treatment — surgical:**
- **Unilateral arytenoid lateralization ("tie-back"):** current technique of choice; bilateral lateralization NOT recommended (unacceptable morbidity).
- Aspiration pneumonia: most common postoperative complication; occurs in 10–21% of dogs after unilateral arytenoid lateralization (Ch 214).
- Preoperative temporary tracheostomy = negative prognostic indicator; major complications in 21–26% of tracheostomy cases (airway obstruction, tube dislodgement, aspiration pneumonia, stoma swelling).

**Permanent tracheostomy outcomes (salvage):**
- 21 dogs with permanent tracheostomies: 50% had major complications, 20% required revision surgery, 26% died acutely at home (likely airway obstruction); overall median survival 1825 days, but shorter for dogs with tracheal collapse, older dogs, or those receiving corticosteroids (n=21; Ch 214).
- 69 dogs: major complications 61% (aspiration pneumonia, skinfold occlusion, stoma stenosis); revision surgery 35%.

**Cats — conservative management:** Reported survival times 120–2520 days; median 811 days in 7 cats treated conservatively.

**Prognosis — laryngeal tumors in cats:**
- Squamous cell carcinoma with tube tracheostomy alone: median survival 3 days (n=4 cats).
- Laryngeal masses with chemotherapy: median survival 141 days (n=5 cats).
- Laryngeal lymphoma with chemotherapy: survival 60 and 1440 days (n=2 cats).
- Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma with prednisolone: 1 cat survived 180 days.

### Ch 215 — Large Airway Diseases (Tracheal Collapse, Bronchomalacia, Chronic Bronchitis, EBP, Feline Asthma)

---

#### Tracheal Collapse

> ClinIQ: ✓ TRACOLL — significant enrichment warranted.

**Signalment/breed:**
- Middle-aged to older miniature, toy, and small breed dogs.
- Overrepresented: Yorkshire Terrier, Pomeranian, Pug, Poodle, Maltese, Chihuahua.
- Multifactorial etiology: cartilage ring abnormality (reduced glycosaminoglycan + chondroitin sulfate content) + triggering factors (obesity, cardiomegaly, inhalation of irritants/allergens, periodontal disease, respiratory infections, recent endotracheal intubation).
- Concurrent bronchomalacia is common; PH can develop secondary to tracheal collapse + bronchomalacia.

**Diagnosis:**
- Strongly suspected on signalment + cough history + physical exam (palpable tracheal flattening, easily elicited "goose honk" cough).
- **Imaging:** Lateral radiographs in both maximal inspiration and maximal expiration; cervical collapse occurs on inspiration; thoracic segment collapses on expiration.
- **Fluoroscopy:** More sensitive than static radiographs for dynamic collapse; also evaluates bronchial collapse and contribution of cardiomegaly.
- **Bronchoscopy/tracheoscopy:** Required to accurately grade severity and detect concurrent bronchomalacia, coexisting airway disease, infection; recommended before stent placement.

**Tracheal collapse grading (endoscopic):**
- Grade 1: mild dorsoventral flattening (Figure 215.4C).
- Grade 3: severe flattening approaching complete obstruction (Figure 215.4D).
- Grade IV (fixed obstruction): can cause complete airway obstruction; listed as a "fixed airway obstruction" differential in Ch 212 breathing pattern algorithm.

**Treatment — acute:**
- Oxygen supplementation.
- **Butorphanol 0.05–0.2 mg/kg q4–6h** SC and/or **acepromazine 0.01–0.1 mg/kg** SC.
- **Prednisolone sodium succinate 15–30 mg/kg slow IV bolus** (reduce acute inflammation).

**Treatment — long-term medical:**
- Weight loss essential in obese animals; replace neck collars with harnesses.
- Oral antitussives:
  - **Hydrocodone 0.22 mg/kg PO q12h** OR
  - **Butorphanol 0.55 mg/kg PO q12h**
- Low-dose corticosteroids: **prednisone 0.2 mg/kg PO daily for 1–2 weeks, at 3-month intervals** OR inhaled: **fluticasone, one 120 mcg puff q12h** (via spacer/mask).
- Sedative-antihistamines as adjunct.
- Bronchodilators (methylxanthines or beta-agonists) — reduce smaller airway spasm, reducing intrathoracic pressures.

**Treatment — mechanical:**
- Intraluminal tracheal stents (Ch 114): indicated for severe dynamic obstruction causing obstructive dyspnea; placed under fluoroscopic guidance; stent granulomas are a recognized complication.

**Prognosis:** Generally progressive. Most dogs improve with individualized treatment plan; recent data suggest improved long-term prognosis with stent placement, though significant complications reported.

#### Canine Chronic Bronchitis

> ClinIQ: ✓ BRONCHITIS — significant enrichment warranted.

**Definition (3 criteria all required):**
1. ≥2 months of coughing
2. Evidence of mucus hypersecretion
3. Exclusion of other chronic cardiopulmonary diseases as sole cause of cough

**Signalment:**
- Middle-aged to old smaller breeds (predominant but also larger breeds).
- Obese/overweight animals; dental tartar common risk factor.
- Possible triggers: CIRDC pathogens (Bordetella), canine distemper virus, parasites, tracheal collapse, chronic cardiac disease, GERD/microaspiration.

**Diagnosis:**
- BAL cytology: excessive mucus, hyperplastic epithelial cells, increased neutrophils, goblet cells, macrophages.
- Eosinophilia on BAL → points to EBP or parasitic infection rather than CB.
- NT-proBNP if LAE/CHF or PH suspected (→ echocardiography).
- Fluoroscopy to evaluate for concurrent airway collapse.

**Cough suppressants (Table 215.1):**
| Drug | Dosage |
|---|---|
| Butorphanol (opioid) | 0.25–1.1 mg/kg PO q8–12h |
| Hydrocodone (opioid) | 0.2–0.3 mg/kg PO q6–12h |
| Tramadol (opioid) | 2–5 mg/kg PO q8–12h |
| Gabapentin (non-opioid) | 2–5 mg/kg PO q8h |
| Methocarbamol (non-opioid) | 15–30 mg/kg q12h |

**Anti-inflammatory therapy:**
- Glucocorticoids = mainstay.
- **Prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q24h** initially; taper to lowest effective dose.
- Alternate-day therapy preferred to allow HPA axis normalization.
- Inhaled glucocorticoids (e.g., **fluticasone via AeroDawg spacer/face mask**) — growing use; avoids systemic steroid effects.

**Antibiotics (acute exacerbation with suspected infection):**
- Doxycycline or macrolides (azithromycin) preferred — anti-inflammatory + antimicrobial properties.
- Fluoroquinolones: good tissue penetration but concurrent use with theophylline can cause theophylline toxicosis.

**Prognosis:** Bronchial alterations not readily reversible; management aims to prevent/slow progression. Obesity correction alone can produce major clinical improvement.

#### Eosinophilic Bronchopneumopathy (EBP)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — significant clinical entity)

**Signalment/breed:**
- Young dogs (predominantly), some middle-aged.
- Siberian Husky and Alaskan Malamute predominantly affected, but many other breeds susceptible.
- Slight female predominance (per Ch 217 reference).
- Etiology unknown; immunological hypersensitivity suspected; possible relationship with Bordetella bronchiseptica load in high-severity cases.

**Diagnosis:**
- **BAL/brush cytology:** always demonstrates marked eosinophilic component; usually >50% of inflammatory cells are eosinophils; total BAL cell count often elevated (example from Figure 215.8: 47% eosinophils, total 7000 cells/mcL).
- BAL eosinophil % >50% → diagnostic hallmark.
- Peripheral blood eosinophilia in ~60% of cases.
- Radiographic pattern: moderate to severe bronchointerstitial pattern; CT: marked bronchial wall thickening, mucus/debris plugging, bronchiectasis, pulmonary nodules, lymphadenopathy.
- Exclude parasites (fecal Baermann + zinc sulfate centrifugation; heartworm antigen test; Angiostrongylus antigen test/PCR in endemic regions) before diagnosing idiopathic EBP.

**Treatment:**
- **Prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg q12h PO × 1 week**, then **q24h × 1 week**, then **q48h × 1 week**, then gradual decrease to lowest effective dose.
- Inhaled steroid therapy (IST) growing use, but long-term IST alone may be insufficient; sustained IST may cause HPA axis inhibition.
- Response to corticosteroids generally very good.

**Prognosis:** Relapse often occurs within weeks to months after drug discontinuation; some dogs maintain complete remission after discontinuation.

#### Feline Lower Airway Disease (Feline Asthma / Feline Chronic Bronchitis)

> ClinIQ: ✓ ASTHMA — significant enrichment warranted.

**Signalment/breed:**
- Cats of any age (usually begins in young or middle age); either sex.
- Middle-aged females (2–8 years) somewhat more frequently presented.
- Siamese cats may be overrepresented (some studies; regional breed distribution confound).
- Disease is chronic/slowly progressive.

**Diagnosis:**
- **BAL cytology cutoffs:**
  - Predominantly eosinophilic asthma: BAL eosinophils >20% (with neutrophils within reference range) OR >50% eosinophils.
  - Predominantly neutrophilic CB: BAL neutrophils >7% (with eosinophils within reference limits) OR >50% neutrophils.
  - Mixed: concurrent increases in both.
  - Healthy cats: eosinophils <5% in BAL.
- NOTE: Prior glucocorticoid therapy reduces eosinophilic content → threshold interpretation compromised.
- Peripheral blood eosinophilia in ~20% of cases (not correlated with airway eosinophilia).
- Heartworm antibody/antigen test; fecal Baermann (Aelurostrongylus abstrusus); qPCR for Troglostrongylus spp.
- Radiographic pattern: bronchial/bronchointerstitial; air trapping; possible right middle lung lobe atelectasis; flattening of diaphragm.
- CT (VetMousetrap for awake/conscious acquisition): more sensitive for bronchial wall thickening, bronchiectasis.
- Asthma = most common cause of spontaneous pneumothorax in cats (25% of feline spontaneous pneumothorax).

**Treatment — acute crisis:**
- Minimize stress; oxygen-enriched environment.
- **Bronchodilators (beta-2 agonists): terbutaline 0.01 mg/kg IV, IM, or SC** (Ch 215).
- **Rapidly acting corticosteroid: dexamethasone 0.25–0.5 mg/kg IV or IM** (Ch 215).
- If no response: consider spontaneous pneumothorax → emergency thoracocentesis.

**Treatment — chronic:**
- **Oral prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg PO q12h for 1–2 weeks, followed by gradual tapering of dose.**
- Inhaled corticosteroids (chronic therapy):
  - **Budesonide 400 mcg q12h** (inhaled): clinical signs and plethysmography improved; other treatment withdrawn in 47% of cats; no corticosteroid side-effects but HPA axis suppression in 20%.
  - **Fluticasone propionate (Flixotide) 2 puffs × 250 mcg/puff q12h** (inhaled): significant improvement in clinical scores; lower inhaled dose (44 mcg) showed identical effect to 220 mcg in asthma model → lower dose is preferred.
- Inhaled albuterol (salbutamol): useful for quick alleviation of acute signs; NOT to be used as monotherapy (does not control inflammation); caution — tachycardia, CNS effects; daily racemic albuterol associated with adverse effects in feline asthma model.
- Bronchodilators should accompany but not replace corticosteroids.
- Propentofylline (methylxanthine): possible additive benefit with prednisolone.
- Weight loss in obese cats.
- Avoid nebulized substances (can trigger bronchospasm).

**Prognosis:** Substantial morbidity and mortality possible; most cats require lifelong treatment; unchecked inflammation leads to irreversible remodeling and decline in lung function. Severe signs + failure to respond to glucocorticoids = unfavorable.

#### Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia (PCD)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (rare; recurrent pneumonia differential)

- Hereditary ciliary dysmotility; recurrent upper and lower airway bacterial infections; Kartagener's syndrome (bronchiectasis + situs inversus + chronic rhinosinusitis) reported in dogs with PCD.
- 50% chance of situs inversus in affected animals.

### Ch 216 — Small Airway Diseases

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING as standalone class (candidate — "small airway disease" is an emerging veterinary category)

**Key points:**
- Small airways: <2 mm diameter, lack cartilage; not visible on radiograph or CT in healthy animals.
- Classification (cats): (1) primary bronchiolar disorders, (2) secondary from large airway disease, (3) secondary as component of ILD.
- No pathognomonic clinical signs or cytologic findings.
- CT scan (anesthetized, paired inspiratory/expiratory views) = most sensitive diagnostic test for small airway disease; reveals tree-in-bud pattern, bronchiolectasis, peribronchiolar ground-glass opacity (direct signs); mosaic attenuation from air trapping (indirect sign).
- Histopathologic evaluation = definitive diagnosis (rarely performed ante-mortem).

**Empiric treatment (when infection/neoplasia excluded):**
- **Prednisolone (cats) or prednisone (dogs) 0.5–2 mg/kg/day** (empiric glucocorticoid trial).
- Constrictive/obliterative bronchiolitis unresponsive to glucocorticoids (and bronchodilators in cats) → grave prognosis.

### Ch 217 — Pulmonary Parenchymal Diseases

---

#### Bacterial Pneumonia

> ClinIQ: ✓ BACPNEU — significant enrichment warranted.

**Signalment:**
- Dogs > cats; unvaccinated, juvenile, immunocompromised, or debilitated animals at highest risk.
- B. bronchiseptica accounts for nearly half of all community-acquired pneumonia in dogs <1 year.
- About 1/3 of pneumonia cases involve multiple bacterial species; up to 20% involve anaerobes.
- Most common bacteria: streptococci, enteric pathogens (E. coli, Klebsiella), Pasteurella, coagulase-positive staphylococci, Mycoplasma spp., Bordetella bronchiseptica.

**Diagnosis:**
- Classic radiographic pattern: alveolar infiltrate with predominantly ventral (cranioventral in dogs) distribution.
- Cats: more often multifocal or patchy, asymmetrical alveolar pattern.
- Airway lavage (BAL/ETW/TTW) for cytology + aerobic + anaerobic culture + susceptibility (C/S) = definitive diagnosis; Mycoplasma PCR if suspected.
- Fever inconsistent; normothermia does NOT rule out bacterial pneumonia.
- <50% of dogs with aspiration pneumonia febrile; even fewer cats.
- CBC: neutrophilia ± left shift, lymphopenia, mild anemia — common but inconsistent.
- Oxygen supplementation: indicated when **PaO2 <80 mm Hg or SpO2 <94%**.

**Treatment — empiric antimicrobials (Box 217.4):**

Severe, unstable disease — combination parenteral therapy:
- Beta-lactam (e.g., ampicillin, ampicillin-sulbactam, 2nd- or 3rd-generation cephalosporin) OR clindamycin
- AND EITHER: Fluoroquinolone (e.g., enrofloxacin, marbofloxacin) OR Aminoglycoside (e.g., amikacin, gentamicin)

Moderate, stable disease:
- Monotherapy: amoxicillin-clavulanate OR trimethoprim-sulfonamide
- Combination: beta-lactam AND fluoroquinolone OR clindamycin AND fluoroquinolone

Mild, stable disease:
- Monotherapy: amoxicillin-clavulanate OR fluoroquinolone OR trimethoprim-sulfonamide OR doxycycline*
  - *Doxycycline most useful when Bordetella bronchiseptica or Mycoplasma spp. is suspected in otherwise stable animal with recent dog exposure.

**ISCAID guidelines:** Re-evaluate 10–14 days after treatment begins; extend/alter/halt based on clinical response.
- NAC (mucolytic) oral dosage: **125 mg up to 600 mg PO q8–12h** (author's experience; no published studies in naturally occurring veterinary pneumonia).

**Prognosis:** ~80% of dogs and cats survived to discharge in published studies.
- Worse prognosis: >1 involved lung lobe (slightly >1/3 dogs and >1/2 cats had ≥2 lobes involved).

#### Aspiration Pneumonia

> ClinIQ: ✓ ASPPNEU — enrichment warranted.

**Predisposing conditions (Box 217.6):**
Impaired conscious protection (anesthesia, sedation, seizures, coma), laryngeal paralysis/surgical alteration of larynx, myasthenia gravis, impaired swallowing, regurgitation (megaesophagus), vomiting, gastric overdistension, forced feeding.

**Signalment in retrospective study (n=88 dogs):**
- Underlying disorders: esophageal disease 39.8% (megaesophagus in 71.4% of esophageal disease cases), vomiting 38.6%, neurologic disease 27.3%, laryngeal disease 18.2%, post-anesthetic aspiration 13.6%.

**Diagnosis:**
- Classic radiograph: focal/patchy alveolar infiltrate; most commonly right middle, right cranial, and caudal portion of left cranial lung lobes (gravity-dependent aspiration).
- <50% of dogs febrile; even fewer cats (some cats hypothermic).
- Left lateral view essential (improves detection of right middle lobe alveolar pattern — largely silhouetted against cardiac shadow on right lateral).
- Radiographic changes can lag aspiration event by up to 24 hours.

**Treatment:**
- Primarily supportive; correct predisposing factors.
- H2 antagonists or PPIs: increase gastric pH but not proven to reduce morbidity/mortality.
- Secondary bacterial infection requires C/S-guided antimicrobials — not all aspiration results in bacterial infection; antimicrobial overuse common.
- Nebulization + physiotherapy (coupage, prolonged slow expiration, assisted cough — Video 217.3).

**Prognosis:** ~80% of dogs and cats survived to discharge. Prognosis cannot be predicted by radiographic severity alone.

#### Pulmonary Parasites

**Lungworm treatment doses:**
- **Fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO q24h × 10–14 days** (for most lungworms; general approach).
- **Ivermectin 0.4 mg/kg SC** (for Filaroides; repeated in 1 week; use with caution — MDR-1/ABCB1 mutation in Collies and related breeds).
- **Ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg SC each week × 2 doses** (Angiostrongylus vasorum treatment option).
- **Fenbendazole 25–50 mg/kg PO q24h × 10–20 days** (Angiostrongylus vasorum or Filaroides alternative).
- **Milbemycin oxime 0.5 mg/kg PO each week × 4 weeks** (Angiostrongylus vasorum option).
- Post-treatment inflammatory reaction (dying worms) can worsen severity; ameliorate with: **predniso[lo]ne 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q24h × 3–7 days**.
- **Praziquantel 25 mg/kg PO q8h × 3 days** (for Paragonimus kellicotti, in addition to fenbendazole).

**Angiostrongylus vasorum:**
- "French heartworm"; endemic in Europe, Asia, Africa, Newfoundland Canada.
- Diagnosis: Baermann fecal flotation; point-of-care antigen test (Europe); A. vasorum PCR in BALF.
- Thoracic ultrasound: subpleural hypoechoic nodules — 100% sensitive and 92% specific for infection (recent study).
- Coagulopathy, neurologic and ocular signs possible; severe PH with cor pulmonale reported.
- Post-treatment reactions including dyspnea, ascites, sudden death reported.

> ClinIQ: ✓ LUNGWORM — enrichment with specific doses above.

#### Canine Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (CIPF)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (significant clinical entity; candidate)

**Signalment/breed:**
- Strong breed predisposition: vast majority of CIPF = West Highland White Terriers.
- Also reported in Staffordshire Bull Terriers, Bull Terriers, Cairn Terriers, Scottish Terriers, Pekingese (Hong Kong cohort).
- Mean age at recognition: 9–13 years.
- Cats of any age can have IPF; often present with sudden respiratory distress or sudden death (clinically silent until late).

**Diagnosis:**
- Multidisciplinary approach essential (clinician + imaging specialist + pathologist).
- Prominent inspiratory crackles widespread in dogs with mild-moderate CIPF — distinguishing feature from cardiogenic edema.
- CT findings: ground-glass opacity, mosaic attenuation, reticular pattern in dogs; traction bronchiectasis in cats.
- **CT cannot confirm IPF** (unlike humans, findings not specific enough in dogs/cats to eliminate lung biopsy requirement).
- Histopathologic confirmation of fibrosis mandatory; totality of findings required for IPF diagnosis.
- Echocardiography: rule out primary heart disease; assess for pulmonary hypertension (a common, treatable complication of CIPF).
- Dogs: absent alveolar pattern with loud crackles → suggests pulmonary fibrosis over cardiogenic edema.

**Treatment:**
- Irreversible; supportive care only.
- Cough suppression to improve quality of life.
- **Pulmonary hypertension (if present):** Sildenafil 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q8h OR tadalafil 2 mg/kg PO q24h.
- Pirfenidone/nintedanib (human IPF drugs): untested and too expensive for veterinary use.

**Prognosis:** Poor long-term. Some dogs with good PH response to treatment live near-normal length/quality of life for their age; in others, diagnosis comes only towards end of life or disease progression is rapid. Most reported cats euthanized or succumb quickly; some long-term responses reported.

#### Eosinophilic Pneumonia / Pulmonary Eosinophilic Granulomatosis (EPG)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — distinct from EBP which is primarily airway; EP = parenchymal)

**Treatment:**
- **Oral corticosteroids 0.5–2 mg/kg predniso[lo]ne q24h, tapered to effect** (Ch 217).
- OR inhaled steroids (Ch 90).
- Response often rapid; relapse not uncommon.
- EPG prognosis: guarded to poor (unlike EBP/EG).

#### Lung Lobe Torsion (LLT)

> ClinIQ: ✓ LLT — enrichment warranted.

**Signalment/breed:**
- Uncommon in dogs, rare in cats.
- Pugs and sighthounds (especially Afghan Hounds) overrepresented.
- Most affected dogs are young to middle-aged; also reported in puppies.
- Chylous effusions often identified in Afghan Hounds with and without LLT (breed susceptibility to chylothorax).

**Diagnosis:**
- Duration of signs usually <1 week but signs can persist for months.
- Pleural effusion (blood-tinged modified transudate; high neutrophils and lymphocytes; chylous in some); remove effusion before re-radiographing.
- **Vesicular gas pattern (small scattered bubbles) in affected lobe** strongly suggestive of torsion.
- CT (with or without virtual bronchoscopy): more sensitive and specific than radiographs; not obscured by pleural effusion.
- Thoracic US: hypoechoic band around periphery of affected lung = highly suggestive of torsion (recent description).

#### Pulmonary Neoplasia

> ClinIQ: ✓ PULMNEO — enrichment warranted.

**Primary lung cancer:**
- Adenocarcinoma predominates; less commonly carcinoma, SCC, anaplastic carcinoma; primary histiocytic sarcoma now key differential.
- Most common in older animals.
- Just over 50% of dogs presented for chronic non-productive cough; up to 1/3 have NO respiratory signs.
- Cats: only 20–40% present with dyspnea.
- Lung-digit syndrome in cats: metastasis to phalanges.
- Typical CT appearance: well-circumscribed, bronchocentric mass with internal air bronchograms; up to 1/4 of tumors have metastasis at diagnosis.
- Up to 1/3 of primary lung tumors affect multiple lobes.

**Primary lung tumor surgical prognosis:**
- Excision is treatment of choice for single-lobe disease.
- Favorable factors: complete resection, single tumor without metastasis, lack of respiratory signs, lower histologic grade, peripheral location, no pleural effusion.
- Postoperative survival: widely variable; can be up to 2 years (may be normal life expectancy in geriatric patients).
- Histiocytic sarcoma: primary pulmonary — median survival 374 days with surgery + chemotherapy.

**Metastatic lung cancer:**
- Prognosis generally poor.
- Slow-growing tumors (e.g., thyroid carcinoma, anal sac adenocarcinoma) may have minimal impact on overall survival.
- Radioiodine for thyroid carcinoma (treat primary + metastasis).
- Metastasectomy in selected cases (single/few nodules, long disease-free interval, confined to one lobe).

#### Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

**ARDS Berlin Definition severity (adapted for veterinary use):**
- Mild: **PaO2/FiO2 = 200–300 mm Hg with PEEP or CPAP ≥5 cm H2O**
- Moderate: **PaO2/FiO2 = 100–200 mm Hg with PEEP ≥5 cm H2O**
- Severe: **PaO2/FiO2 <100 mm Hg with PEEP ≥5 cm H2O**

**PPV indicated if:** initial therapy unable to maintain **SpO2 ≥90%, PaO2 ≥60 mm Hg, and PaCO2 <60 mm Hg**.

Prognosis: guarded to grave.

### Ch 218 — Pulmonary Hypertension

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (major candidate — important clinical entity)

**Definition:**
- In humans: mean pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) ≥25 mm Hg at rest (RHC); evidence-based cutoff of mean PAP >20 mm Hg now offered.
- Veterinary diagnosis: primarily via echocardiography (RHC rarely performed).

**ACVIM Echocardiographic probability algorithm (Figure 218.1, 2020 ACVIM Consensus):**

| TRV | Additional echo signs of PH | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| ≤3.0 m/s or not measurable | Signs at 2+ anatomic sites | Intermediate |
| 3.0–3.4 m/s | Signs at ≥2 anatomic sites | Intermediate |
| 3.0–3.4 m/s | Signs at <2 sites | Low |
| >3.4 m/s | Signs at ≥1 site | **High** |
| >3.4 m/s | No signs | Intermediate |

TRV = tricuspid regurgitation velocity.

**Echo signs of PH at 3 anatomic sites:**
1. **Ventricles:** IVS flattening, underfilling/decreased LV chamber size*, RV hypertrophy, RV systolic dysfunction.
2. **Pulmonary artery:** PA enlargement, reduced PA distensibility, peak early PR velocity >2.5 m/s, systolic notching of RV outflow profile.
3. **RA/caudal VC:** RA enlargement, caudal VC and hepatic venous dilation.

(*Not applicable for Group 2 PH.)

**Conventional TRV pressure gradient categorization (empirical, cited in Ch 218):**
- TRV → pressure gradient via Bernoulli: PG (mm Hg) = 4 × velocity (m/s)²
- Mild PH: 30–50 mm Hg; Moderate: 50–75 mm Hg; Severe: >75 mm Hg.

**ACVIM 6-group clinical classification (Table 218.2):**
1. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) — precapillary, no LA enlargement.
2. Left heart disease (postcapillary, LA enlargement, ± increased PVR).
3. Respiratory disease/hypoxia (precapillary, no LA enlargement).
4. Thrombotic/thromboembolic disease (precapillary, no LA enlargement).
5. Parasitic (heartworm or Angiostrongylus).
6. Multifactorial/unclear.

**Treatment (PDE5 inhibitors):**
- **Sildenafil 1–4 mg/kg PO q8h** (highly selective PDE5i; first-line when PA vasodilator indicated; administered q8h due to short half-life; conservative dosage for CpcPH/Group 2: **0.5 mg/kg PO q8h**).
- **Tadalafil 2 mg/kg PO q24h** (longer half-life; randomized pilot study: non-inferior to sildenafil).
- PDE5i **contraindicated as first-line** in IpcPH (isolated postcapillary PH) and if cardiogenic pulmonary edema is apparent.
- Empirical PDE5i without echocardiography is **discouraged**.

**Prognosis:**
- PH worsens prognosis of dogs with MMVD and respiratory disease/hypoxemia.
- Echocardiographic right atrial area and RV systolic dysfunction, and right-sided CHF independently associated with worse outcome (in acquired, irreversible PH excluding HW disease and cardiac shunts).

### Ch 219 — Pulmonary Thromboembolism (PTE)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (significant candidate)

**Predisposing factors:** Trauma, dirofilariasis, indwelling IV catheters, surgery, neoplasia, IMHA, protein-losing nephropathy, protein-losing enteropathy, hyperadrenocorticism, sepsis. Studies: majority of dogs AND nearly half of cats with PTE have >1 predisposing condition.

**Diagnosis:**
- ABG: hypoxemia, hypocapnia, increased A-a oxygen gradient.
- D-dimer: **>1000 ng/dL + predisposing factors** = supportive of PTE; **<100 ng/dL** = PTE unlikely (high negative predictive value).
- Antithrombin <60% = moderate risk; <30% = immediate risk.
- CTPA (computed tomography pulmonary angiography) = diagnostic gold standard: partial or complete intraluminal filling defect or abrupt cutoff of pulmonary arteries.
- POCUS: "shred sign" and "wedge sign" in perihilar/caudodorsal regions — supportive but not diagnostic.
- One canine study of naturally occurring PTE: **14% hospital discharge rate.**

**Treatment — Thrombolysis:**
- Alteplase (tPA) in dogs: **0.2 mg/kg IV bolus → 0.7 mg/kg IV over 30 minutes → 0.5 mg/kg IV over 60 minutes (total dose: 1.4 mg/kg over 90 minutes)**.
- Alteplase in cats: **0.75 mg total IV bolus → 2.5 mg total IV over 30 minutes → 1.75 mg total over 60 minutes (total dose: 5 mg over 90 minutes)**.
- Risk of hemorrhage, ischemia/reperfusion; used only in hemodynamically unstable PTE with exquisite monitoring.

**Antithrombotic drugs (Table 219.1):**
| Drug | Cats | Dogs |
|---|---|---|
| Clopidogrel | 18.75 mg/cat PO q24h | 1–3 mg/kg PO q24h |
| Unfractionated heparin | 100 U/kg IV once; then 200 U/kg SC q6–8h | 100 U/kg IV once; then 100–300 U/kg q6–8h |
| Dalteparin | 150 U/kg SC q8–24h | 75–150 U/kg SC q12–24h |
| Enoxaparin | 1 mg/kg SC q6–8h | 1 mg/kg SC q6–8h |
| Rivaroxaban | 2.5 mg/cat PO q24h | 1–2 mg/kg PO q24h |

**Monitoring:** aPTT target 1.5–2.5× baseline; anti-factor Xa target 0.35–0.7 U/mL.

**Pulmonary vasodilation (if PH documented):**
- **Sildenafil 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q8h; may increase up to 3 mg/kg PO q8h** based on clinical signs.
- **Aminophylline 3–11 mg/kg IV or IM q6–12h** (reduced PVR in experimental canine PH model).
- Norepinephrine for hypotension: **0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min IV CRI**.

**Prognosis:** Guarded to poor. One canine study: 14% hospital discharge rate. Massive hemodynamically unstable PTE = very poor prognosis.

### Ch 220 — Pleural Space Diseases

---

#### Pyothorax

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-PYOTHORAX — enrichment warranted.

**Etiology:**
- Dogs: bite wounds and penetrating foreign bodies (sticks/plant awns) most frequent.
- Cats: previously thought to be bite wounds; more recent work identifies parapneumonic extension from upper respiratory tract infection as more common.
- Mixed aerobic + anaerobic infections; anaerobic infections may be foul-smelling.

**Diagnosis:**
- Degenerative neutrophils ± intra- and extracellular pathogens on cytology.
- Glucose levels in pleural fluid: do NOT reliably identify sepsis (unlike abdominal effusion).
- Bacterial culture (aerobic + anaerobic) + sensitivity; PCR for atypical organisms (Bartonella).

**Treatment:**
- Chest tube drainage + antibiotics (culture/sensitivity-guided, with anaerobic coverage even if anaerobic culture not performed).
- Some dogs require surgical intervention (resection + drainage of affected tissues).
- **Prognosis frequently good** if not in moribund condition and comprehensive treatment provided. "Pyothorax is one of the few effusions that can be cured."

---

#### Chylothorax

> ClinIQ: ✓ CHYLO — significant enrichment warranted.

**Signalment:**
- Cats: mean age 6.3 years (range 0.5–14 years); most frequent presenting concern rapid/labored breathing (78% of cats).
- Dogs: mean age 4 years (range 1.5–12 years); labored breathing in 79% of dogs.
- Afghan Hounds: chylous effusions with and without LLT (breed predisposition).

**Diagnosis:**
- Grossly: milky white or pinkish fluid.
- **Chylothorax triglyceride cutoffs:**
  - **Cats:** mean effusion TG 1464 mg/dL (range 303–4670 mg/dL; 3.4–52.87 mmol/L) vs. mean serum TG 84 mg/dL (0.3–2.4 mmol/L) (Ch 220).
  - **Dogs:** median effusion TG 877 mg/dL (range 158–2750 mg/dL; 1.8–31.1 mmol/L) vs. median serum TG 73 mg/dL (0.4–6.2 mmol/L) (Ch 220).
- Predominant cell type: small lymphocytes.
- Check for underlying causes: cardiac disease, cranial vena cava thrombus/mass, LLT, heartworm disease, neoplasia (especially cranial mediastinal masses).
- Idiopathic = ~50% of cases.
- Hyponatremia + hyperkalemia (hypoadrenocorticism-like profile) documented in dogs with chylothorax.
- Rounding of lung lobes on radiograph → fibrosing/constrictive pleuritis (cats with chronic chylothorax).

**Treatment:**
- Specific cause → treat underlying cause.
- Idiopathic: medical (low-fat diet, rutin, octreotide) seldom effective.
- **Surgery (preferred for idiopathic):** thoracic duct ligation ± subphrenic pericardectomy ± cisterna chyli ablation.
- **No therapy is universally successful; cure rates ~50–70%.**
- **Cats (n=15):** median survival after thoracic duct ligation + subphrenic pericardectomy = 774 days (range 3–2844 days).
- **Dogs (thoracoscopic thoracic duct ligation + pericardectomy):** 50% alive at median 43 months post-operatively.

---

#### Pneumothorax

> ClinIQ: ✓ Pneumothorax (DIS-RESP-PNX) — enrichment warranted.

**Types:**
- Traumatic (most common overall): blunt or penetrating; most blunt traumatic pneumothoraces heal with supportive care in 1–4 days; rarely require surgery.
- Spontaneous: primary (no underlying disease) or secondary (underlying lung disease).
- Tension pneumothorax: closed; if not promptly evacuated → death.

**Spontaneous pneumothorax (dogs):**
- Northern breeds (Huskies) and Golden Retrievers overrepresented.
- Considered a **surgical disease** in dogs; prompt surgical intervention → higher cure rate + shorter hospitalization.
- Surgical exploration via median sternotomy (both hemithoraces); higher ventilation pressure (to 25 cm H2O) may help identify air leak source.
- Cats: secondary spontaneous pneumothorax most commonly due to asthma/airway disease or heartworm disease; less clear if immediate surgery warranted.
- Asthma = most common cause of spontaneous pneumothorax in cats (25%).

**Mesothelioma (pleural):**
- Dogs: pleura affected in 79%, pericardium 64%, peritoneum 7%.
- Exposure to asbestos significantly associated with intrathoracic mesothelioma in dogs.
- Median survival (dogs, n=34): **195 days** (95% CI 53–324); 1-year survival rate = 22%.
- Treatment: IC chemotherapy — 5-fluorouracil 150–200 mg/m² IC q2 weeks or weekly ×4 then q2 weeks; OR carboplatin 250–300 mg/m² IV or IC q3 weeks × 6 doses.
- Cats (IC carboplatin 180 mg/m² × 2, 6 weeks apart): reduced thoracocentesis frequency; survival 4 months (single case report).

### Ch 221 — Aerodigestive Disorders

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING as standalone category (candidate; strong overlap with existing differential databases for cough, laryngeal disease, LPR, aspiration pneumonia)

**Key clinical points:**
- 80% of dogs presenting exclusively for cough had evidence of swallow dysfunction on free-feeding VFSS (Ch 221).
- Upper digestive tract abnormalities treated prior to BOAS surgery → decreased complication rate + improved prognosis.
- Respiratory signs in dogs improved with treatment exclusively for concurrent upper digestive tract abnormalities.
- **VFSS = criterion standard for evaluation of dysphagia in dogs**; superior to radiographs for detecting sliding hiatal hernias; allows detection of subtle, dynamic, segmental motility disorders.
- Bile acids documented in BALF of healthy West Highland White Terriers → microaspiration of GERD common even in "normal" dogs of this breed.
- Reflux often detected in normal dogs during VFSS; dysfunction determined by severity and degree of margination, not mere presence.
- Nuclear scintigraphy: can measure reflux and cumulative small-volume aspiration in dogs.

**Aspiration risk factors in retrospective study (n=88 dogs):**
- Esophageal disease 39.8% (megaesophagus 71.4% of this group)
- Vomiting 38.6%
- Neurologic disease 27.3%
- Laryngeal disease 18.2%
- Post-anesthetic aspiration 13.6%

### Ch 222 — Diseases of the Mediastinum, Chest Wall, and Diaphragm

---

#### Mediastinal Lymphoma

> ClinIQ: ✓ MEDLYM — enrichment warranted.

**Signalment:**
- More common in cats than dogs.
- **Cats:** typically young (2–4 years of age); often FeLV-positive.
- Dogs: hypercalcemia may be present (paraneoplastic).
- Location: cranial mediastinum (lymph node or thymic origin).

**Diagnosis:**
- Often associated with pleural effusion (obscures radiographic mass).
- CT superior for localization, mass characterization, vascular invasion assessment.
- Diagnosis usually confirmable by cytology of pleural fluid OR FNA/core biopsy of mass (neoplastic lymphocytes).

---

#### Thymoma

> ClinIQ: ✓ THYMOMA — enrichment warranted.

**Signalment:** Well-recognized but uncommon in dogs and cats.

**Associations:**
- Paraneoplastic syndromes common: notably **myasthenia gravis** (Ch 253), which is often associated with megaesophagus in dogs with thymoma.
- Additional paraneoplastic: polymyositis, exfoliative dermatitis (cats), pure red cell aplasia, hypercalcemia.

**Diagnosis:**
- Radiographs: soft-tissue mass in cranioventral (or occasionally craniolateral) mediastinum.
- CT: invaluable for mass location, morphology, vascular invasion, pulmonary metastasis.
- Cytology: variable mature lymphocytes + mast cells; difficult to distinguish from mediastinal lymphoma cytologically.
- **Surgical or US-guided percutaneous biopsy required for definitive diagnosis**.

**Treatment:** Surgical resection = treatment of choice; recurrence and metastasis reported.

---

#### Traumatic Diaphragmatic Hernia

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — high clinical relevance)

**Organ herniation frequency:**
- **Cats:** liver (most common) → small intestine → stomach → omentum → spleen → pancreas → large intestine.
- **Dogs:** liver → small bowel → stomach → spleen → omentum.

**Diagnosis:**
- Herniated gas-filled stomach or intestines visualized cranial to diaphragm on radiograph.
- Solid organs (liver, spleen) may be difficult to distinguish from pleural effusion or pulmonary consolidation on radiograph.
- US and CT: best adjunct imaging for detection of viscera cranial to diaphragm, especially when pleural effusion obscures visualization.
- Clinical signs of chronic hernia may be nonspecific (anorexia, lethargy, vomiting, weight loss) — not all patients have respiratory signs.
- Pleural effusion often present, especially with chronic hernias.

---

#### Pectus Excavatum

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate)

- Most often congenital; especially Maltese and English Bulldog.
- Mild to severe dorsal deviation of caudal sternebrae ± cardiac malposition.
- Clinical signs depend on severity: exercise intolerance, tachypnea, cyanosis, respiratory distress, heart murmur (functional or concurrent congenital cardiac defect).
- Treatment: conservative to surgical repair depending on severity.
