### Ch 223 — Heart Disease and Kidney Disease (Cardiorenal Syndrome)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate page: Cardiovascular-Renal Axis Disorders / Cardiorenal Syndrome)

**Core concept (Ch 223)**
Concurrent heart disease + CKD is termed **cardiovascular-renal axis (CVRA) disorders** in veterinary medicine (preferred over "cardiorenal syndrome"). The fundamental dilemma is that cardiac function requires adequate circulating volume for glomerular perfusion, while treating CHF mandates volume reduction (diuresis) that risks prerenal azotemia. Managing these patients requires maintaining intravascular pressure within a narrower-than-normal window.

#### Pathophysiology links
- Shared RAAS activation, sympathetic hyperactivity, and fluid/sodium dysregulation.
- CKD does NOT cause hypertension in the cardiac sense — the heart is the victim, not the culprit of hypertension in systemic HTN.
- Diuretics (furosemide, torsemide) → potassium wasting → hypokalemia common in both CHF + CKD (20–30% of cats with CKD have hypokalemia).
- Hypokalemia: prolongs action potential, causes tachyarrhythmias refractory to Class I antiarrhythmics (e.g., lidocaine), causes muscular weakness/neck ventroflexion in cats.
- Anemia reported in 47%, 71%, and 82% of dogs with IRIS CKD stages 2, 3, and 4, respectively; negatively prognostic in DMVD.

#### Key management thresholds and targets (Ch 223)
- **Acute CHF with azotemia**: furosemide 3–4 mg/kg IV PRN is acceptable even if it worsens azotemia, but high-dose use must be **brief** — transition to chronic management within first **24–36 hours**.
- **Chronic CHF**: use **lowest diuretic dose** that controls pulmonary edema/effusions; add dietary sodium restriction once home and eating.
- **Resting respiratory rate monitoring** (home): normal <32–40/min dogs, <30/min cats; elevations sustained for minutes–hours warrant veterinary contact.
- **Diuretic resistance assessment**: check urine specific gravity (should be <1.030); have owner measure daily water intake.
- **Recurrent CHF escalation**: switch furosemide → torsemide; add spironolactone (if hypokalemia) or hydrochlorothiazide as second/third diuretic; for large-volume effusions prefer centesis over higher diuretic doses (renal-sparing).

#### Potassium supplementation (Ch 223)
- **Cats** (hypokalemia): potassium gluconate 4 mEq/cat PO q24h (significantly increases serum and muscle K+ in CKD cats).
- **Dogs** (hypokalemia): potassium gluconate 2–8 mEq/dog PO q12h based on body weight and severity; titrate to normokalemia.
- Avoid KCl salt substitute — unpalatable; use flavored/coated potassium gluconate (e.g., MicroK).
- Furosemide/torsemide-treated CHF patients: spironolactone **1–2 mg/kg PO q12h** can be added for hypokalemia + mild recurrent edema/effusion (also potassium-sparing effect).
- Decreasing furosemide/torsemide dose when transitioning to a lower-sodium diet also reduces potassium wasting.

#### Fluid management (Ch 223)
- IV fluid type: change from sodium-rich (LRS, 0.9% NaCl) → sodium-limited (0.45% NaCl ± 2.5% dextrose) once a dehydrated patient reaches euvolemia.
- Avoid simultaneous SQ/IV fluids + diuretics — mutually antagonistic; enteral hydration (nasogastric or esophagostomy tube) preferred over IV in chronic management.
- Body weight monitoring q12h; respiratory rate/effort monitoring q1–2h in critical patients; gallop sound auscultation q6h.
- Gallop sound onset during fluid therapy = early sign of iatrogenic CHF.

#### Systemic hypertension (Ch 223)
- CKD is the most common cause of systemic HTN (20–65% prevalence in cats with CKD).
- Treatment typically amlodipine or an angiotensin-receptor blocker (Ch 236 for doses).

#### Dietary sodium (Ch 223)
- Commercial dog/cat foods vary up to 20-fold in sodium content.
- No known benefit to restricting sodium below maintenance level in subclinical (ACVIM B1 or B2) heart disease.
- Avoid sodium peaks (treats, dietary indiscretion).
- FLUTD prevention diets are high-sodium by design → if cat has had CHF, switch to a lower-sodium stone diet or increase diuretic dose slightly.

#### Acute vs chronic management summary tables (Ch 223)
**CHF-predominant acute**: IV diuretics (bolus or CRI preferred over SQ/IM); monitor body weight, respiratory rate, urine output, gallop; percutaneous centesis preferred for large effusions over escalating diuretics.
**CKD-predominant acute**: suspend most long-term oral drugs until eating ("medication holiday"); essential drugs parenterally (antiemetic, KCl); use fluid type matching needs; identify reversible causes (e.g., pyelonephritis).
**CHF-predominant chronic**: verify compliance; identify diuretic resistance; taper diuretic to lowest effective amount; serial resting respiratory rate monitoring.
**CKD-predominant chronic**: identify and manage systemic HTN; manage hypokalemia (potassium supplementation + spironolactone if also on diuretics); manage anemia of CKD.

### Ch 224 — Comorbidities: Diabetes Mellitus and Corticosteroid-Responsive Disease

> ClinIQ: concept — could enrich existing DM pages (canine and feline diabetes)

**Core dilemma (Ch 224)**
Diabetic patients (dog or cat) commonly need corticosteroids for concurrent immune-mediated, allergic, or inflammatory disease. Glucocorticoids cause insulin resistance, raise blood glucose (gluconeogenesis ↑, peripheral glucose uptake ↓, lipolysis ↑), and can precipitate DKA or hyperosmolar syndrome if insulin is not adjusted. However, corticosteroids are NOT always contraindicated — their judicious use can be essential, and in some cases (e.g., feline triaditis with pancreatitis/IBD) reducing inflammation actually *improves* glycemic control.

#### Corticosteroid potency (Table 224.2) (Ch 224)
| Drug | Glucocorticoid | Mineralocorticoid | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrocortisone | 1 | 1 | Short (8–12 h) |
| Cortisone | 0.8 | 0.8 | Short |
| Prednisolone | 4 | 0.8 | Intermediate (18–36 h) |
| Deflazacort | 3 | Minimal | Intermediate |
| Methylprednisolone | 5 | Minimal | Intermediate |
| Dexamethasone | 30 | Minimal | Long (36–54 h) |
| Betamethasone | 30 | Negligible | Long |
| Fludrocortisone | 15 | 150 | — |

- Prednisolone preferred over prednisone in cats (less hepatic activation of prednisone in cats).
- Dexamethasone: longer duration, more diabetogenic per equipotent dose than prednisolone.
- Topical/inhaled steroids: minimal systemic disruption to glycemic control in practice (dogs and cats).

#### Evidence for glycemic impact (Ch 224)
- **Cats**: 14 healthy cats given prednisolone (4.4 mg/kg) or dexamethasone (0.55 mg/kg) for 56 days → glycosuria, elevated fructosamine; dexamethasone trend toward greater effect.
- **Cats**: methylprednisolone injection → substantial serum glucose increase at 3–6 days; variables only returned to baseline 16–24 days after treatment.
- **Dogs**: topical dexamethasone → exaggerated insulin rise despite normal serum glucose in Beagles; may take 1 week post-withdrawal for insulin secretion to normalize.
- In 143 diabetic cats at a referral centre, 14 had received prednisolone prior to diagnosis; 12 became diabetic within **3 months** of starting prednisolone.

#### Insulin adjustment guidance when using steroids in diabetic patients (Ch 224)

**Short course oral anti-inflammatory prednisolone (up to 0.5 mg/kg, up to 10 days):**
- Insulin changes typically **within 10% of original dose**.
- Cats: increase of 0 to 1 unit twice daily typical.
- Dogs: increase of 0 to 4 units twice daily typical.

**Long-course immunosuppressive prednisolone (2 mg/kg):**
- Insulin requirements may rise by **as much as 20% per injection** with long-term use.

#### Key management principles for steroids in diabetic patients (Ch 224)
1. Only use oral/parenteral steroids with a firm diagnosis; use lowest effective dose for minimum duration; **avoid long-acting (depot) preparations**.
2. Assess risk: age, comorbidities (especially infection, insulin resistance syndromes), concurrent drugs (ketoconazole ↑ glucocorticoid levels; digoxin + prednisolone → hypokalemia).
3. Select shortest-acting, lowest-potency steroid appropriate; topical/inhaled preferred if suitable for the condition.
4. **Once-daily dosing preferred over every-other-day** for glycemic stability in diabetic patients (EOD leads to inconsistent insulin requirements). Feline patients: some clinicians recommend **evening** steroid dosing (less food consumed overnight); canine patients: **morning** dosing (aligns with diurnal cortisol rhythm).
5. Long-term steroid taper: carefully adjust insulin downward to avoid hypoglycemia as steroids are reduced. Continuous glucose monitoring (e.g., Freestyle Libre) helpful.
6. Stable diabetic animal on low-dose steroids: home glucose/urine monitoring acceptable. Unstable diabetic or high-dose steroids: hospital monitoring may be required.
7. Very high steroid doses + poor appetite / pancreatitis / infection: consider switching to short-acting soluble insulin preparation (more precise titration per blood glucose and appetite).

#### Specific clinical scenarios (Ch 224)
- **Topical steroids (e.g., keratitis)**: typically no insulin adjustment needed. Topical dexamethasone least likely to be systemically absorbed; prednisolone acetate drops more likely. Maximum 10–14 days.
- **Seasonal oral steroids (atopy)**: lowest effective dose prednisolone 0.1–0.2 mg/kg/day; anti-inflammatory dose 0.2–0.5 mg/kg. Monitor with continuous glucose monitoring.
- **Inhaled steroids (asthma/chronic bronchitis)**: minimally disruptive to glycemic control; preferred route. May take up to 4 weeks for maximum benefit. If severe disease, bridge with short oral course: prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg for 5–10 days, then transition to inhaled over 7–10 days.
- **IBD cat on prednisolone that becomes diabetic**: do NOT rapidly taper prednisolone (risks IBD relapse); focus on maintaining GI control while gradual steroid reduction; gradual insulin reduction if cat heads toward remission; consider budesonide (first-pass metabolism, limited systemic effect) or chlorambucil + lower prednisolone dose.
- **Steroid-free / steroid-sparing alternatives by condition** (Table 224.1): cyclosporine (atopy, IMHA, IBD, polyarthritis, vasculitis); oclacitinib (atopy); chlorambucil (IBD, cholangitis); azathioprine (dogs only — not cats — for IMHA, IBD); mycophenolate mofetil (IMHA, glomerulonephritis); budesonide (IBD); inhaled fluticasone/budesonide (asthma, chronic bronchitis); metronidazole (IBD, cholangitis).
  - Note: cyclosporine raises glucose/fructosamine in dogs (impaired insulin secretion) — relevant if already predisposed to diabetes; azathioprine associated with pancreatitis risk in dogs.

#### Canine vs feline diabetes difference (Ch 224)
- Diabetic **dogs** have essentially no remaining beta cell function — unlikely to enter remission; steroids do not confer added risk of permanent beta cell loss.
- Diabetic **cats** may retain some beta cell function and can enter remission — steroids risk permanent islet damage and loss of remission potential; avoid if possible, especially in cats previously in remission.
- Cats in DM remission: avoid steroids to prevent recurrence. Non-diabetic cats with known risk factors (obesity, prior DM) — avoid corticosteroids where possible.

### Ch 225 — Feline Triaditis (Cholangitis + IBD + Pancreatitis)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate page: Feline Triaditis)

**Definition (Ch 225)**
Feline triaditis = concurrent inflammation of the small intestine (IBD or diet-responsive enteropathy), pancreas (pancreatitis, EPI), and liver (neutrophilic or lymphocytic cholangitis). A definitive diagnosis requires histological confirmation from biopsy of all three organs (laparotomy or laparoscopic). Many cats have two-organ disease. The three conditions overlap in clinical signs and laboratory findings, making individual attribution difficult.

#### Prevalence (Ch 225)
- In a retrospective study of 54 cats with cholangitis: 26% had pancreatitis, 46% had IBD, 17% had triaditis.
- In a prospective study (27 cats with GI signs): IBD + cholangitis = 22%; IBD + pancreatitis = 4%; **triaditis = 30%**.
- Histopathological changes of ≥2 organs found in 55% of 11 overtly healthy cats undergoing ovariohysterectomy — stresses caution in over-interpreting mild histological changes.
- Cats with triaditis are significantly **older** than those with single-organ disease.
- Interstitial nephritis is a common comorbidity — authors have proposed triaditis may be part of a wider "feline inflammatory syndrome."

#### Etiopathogenesis (Ch 225)
Three proposed models:
1. **Enteritis → dysbiosis → bacterial translocation** → hematogenous/portal seeding of liver/pancreas → acute pancreatitis + neutrophilic cholangitis. E. coli most common biliary isolate.
2. **Acute pancreatitis → acute enteritis/cholangitis** (via close anatomical relationship of pancreaticobiliary duct system in cats); enteritis → further bacterial translocation.
3. **Loss of immune tolerance originating in the small intestine** → aberrant lymphocyte homing → chronic pancreatitis + lymphocytic cholangitis.

The cat's unique anatomy: the pancreatic duct and common bile duct share a common opening into the duodenum in many cats, facilitating retrograde translocation.

#### Diagnostic approach (Ch 225)

**Laboratory findings (not individually diagnostic):**
- IBD: hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia, hypophosphatemia; decreased or low-normal cobalamin or folate (absence does not rule out IBD).
- Pancreatitis: elevated liver enzymes, azotemia, hypocalcemia; **fPLI** (good sensitivity and specificity, but false positives and negatives occur); DGGR lipase (substantial but incomplete agreement with fPLI); traditional serum amylase/lipase have **limited utility** in cats.
- Cholangitis — neutrophilic: hyperbilirubinemia, elevated liver enzymes, inflammatory leukogram (neutrophilia > lymphocytosis), anemia of chronic disease.
- Cholangitis — lymphocytic: elevated liver enzymes, hyperglobulinemia, mild hypoalbuminemia (less common than NC), chronic presentation.
- Bile acids more sensitive for liver dysfunction than bilirubin; prolonged PT/aPTT possible.

**Abdominal ultrasound (suggestive but cannot confirm diagnosis):**
- IBD: increased small intestinal wall thickness (normal thickness does NOT rule out IBD); muscularis thickening suggestive of GI small cell lymphoma.
- Pancreatitis: enlarged pancreas, hypoechogenicity, hyperechoic mesentery, hyperechoic areas (fibrosis), hypoechoic areas (hemorrhage/inflammation/necrosis), dilated pancreatic duct; absence of findings does NOT rule out pancreatitis.
- Cholangitis: altered hepatic echotexture, hepatomegaly, gallbladder/common bile duct wall thickening, hyperechoic gallbladder contents, choleliths/choledocholiths, dilated common bile duct.

**Percutaneous cholecystocentesis (Ch 225):**
- Ultrasound-guided, heavily sedated/anesthetized cats.
- Cytologically confirmed septic suppurative inflammation + bacterial growth = diagnostic for **neutrophilic cholangitis**.
- Common isolates: E. coli, Enterococcus spp., Streptococcus spp.

**Definitive histologic diagnosis:**
- Requires biopsy of all 3 organs (laparotomy or laparoscopic).
- In icteric cats: vitamin K **0.5–1.5 mg/kg SQ q12h for 3 doses** before liver biopsy to reduce hemorrhage risk (assess bleeding risk first).
- Pancreatitis: heterogeneous distribution of lesions — a single negative biopsy does not exclude disease.
- IBD histology: villous stunting, crypt distension, lacteal dilation, mucosal fibrosis; lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate most common but eosinophilic/neutrophilic/mixed also occur. Distinguish from GI small cell lymphoma (difficult — may require immunohistochemistry/clonality testing).
- Lymphocytic cholangitis histology: portal lymphocyte infiltrate, bile duct proliferation, bile duct epithelial degeneration, bile duct loss, varying fibrosis.
- Neutrophilic cholangitis histology: neutrophils + edema centered around bile ducts (acute); mixed neutrophilic/lymphocytic/plasma cell + biliary hyperplasia + fibrosis (chronic).

#### Treatment (Ch 225)

**Supportive care:**
- Individualized IV fluid therapy (maintenance + deficits + ongoing losses; assess cardiac tolerance).
- Address electrolyte abnormalities promptly: hypokalemia (Ch 68), hypophosphatemia (Ch 69).
- Antiemetics: maropitant **1 mg/kg SQ/IV/PO q24h** (NK1 antagonist; also analgesic); ondansetron **0.2–0.5 mg/kg IV/PO q24–48h** (5HT3 antagonist; if PO, use higher end of dose range and shorter end of interval).
- Pain management: buprenorphine **0.01–0.03 mg/kg sublingual/IV/SQ q8–12h** (partial mu opioid agonist; can be given sublingually by owners).
- Appetite stimulants: mirtazapine **1.875–3.75 mg/cat PO q24h** or **1.5 inches (~2 mg) transdermally q24h**.
- Caution: mirtazapine + tramadol → serotonin syndrome risk.
- Enteral nutrition preferred; **do not withhold food** from cats with pancreatitis (early enteral nutrition beneficial). Feeding tube placement often indicated.
- Fat restriction not proven beneficial in feline pancreatitis; commercial intestinal diet often fed.
- Antioxidants (well-tolerated, unproven benefit): S-adenosyl methionine (SAMe) **20 mg/kg PO q24h** on empty stomach; silymarin **10 mg/kg (of silymarin complexed with phosphatidylcholine) PO q24h**; vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol) **15 IU/kg PO q24h**.

**IBD treatment:**
- Prednisolone **1–2 mg/kg PO per day**, tapered to lowest effective dose (Ch 262).
- Chlorambucil **2 mg/cat PO q48–72h** (several protocols exist) as adjunctive therapy if incomplete response to prednisolone or prednisolone not tolerated.
- Diet trial: novel protein or hydrolyzed antigen diet; up to 50% of cats with chronic enteropathy respond to dietary intervention. Strict trial — no other food or treats.

**Pancreatitis treatment:**
- Primarily supportive care and nutrition (as above).
- Antibiotics NOT routinely recommended for pancreatitis.
- Antibiotics **are** indicated if: infected pancreatic fluid accumulation, concurrent aspiration pneumonia, or sepsis suspected.
- Prednisolone for chronic pancreatitis: some panel members (ACVIM consensus) felt prednisolone could help reduce inflammation and prevent fibrosis, but no consensus reached on whether anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive dose should be used initially; hyperglycemia is a potential side-effect.
- Cyclosporine or another immunomodulatory agent can be considered if prednisolone not tolerated (evidence lacking).

**Neutrophilic cholangitis treatment:**
- Antimicrobials are **primary treatment**, ideally based on culture/susceptibility of bile/liver samples.
- Empirical therapy while awaiting culture: choose agent(s) effective against enteric bacteria with good biliary penetration.
  - Potentiated penicillin alone.
  - Enrofloxacin **5 mg/kg PO q24h** + potentiated penicillin or clindamycin.
  - Pradofloxacin **7.5 mg/kg PO q24h** alone.
- Treatment duration: typically **4–6 weeks**.

**Lymphocytic cholangitis treatment:**
- Prednisolone (immunomodulation) is primary treatment (Ch 271).
- Retrospective study: prednisolone-treated cats had median survival time **>800 days**.
- If incomplete response or unacceptable glucocorticoid side-effects: add chlorambucil.
- Ursodeoxycholic acid **10–15 mg/kg PO q24h**: cats treated with prednisolone monotherapy survived longer than those treated with ursodeoxycholic acid monotherapy — benefit of adding UDCA to prednisolone is unknown.

#### Critical treatment interaction: IBD + neutrophilic cholangitis (Ch 225)
- Immunosuppressive doses of prednisolone (indicated for IBD) could worsen neutrophilic cholangitis (bacterial) or cause sepsis.
- Management strategy: **treat neutrophilic cholangitis with appropriate antimicrobial first** for a period of time to control infection **before** starting prednisolone.
- After successful antimicrobial treatment, prednisolone can be initiated for IBD.

#### Prognosis (Ch 225)
- Variable: some cats do not survive initial hospitalization; others respond well long-term.
- Cats with cholangitis + concurrent conditions (IBD, pancreatitis) had **lower survival rate** than those without concurrent disease.
- Cats with triaditis had **more severe histological inflammation** than those with single-organ or two-organ involvement.

### Ch 226 — Obesity and Immobility

> ClinIQ: concept — could enrich existing obesity/OA pages

**Key clinical principles (Ch 226)**

**Weight loss targets:**
- Dogs: ideal weight loss rate **1–2% of body weight (BW) per week**.
- Cats: ideal weight loss rate **0.5–2% of BW per week**.
- Target BW estimation: each BCS point above 5 ≈ 10% excess BW. Formula: target BW = current BW ÷ (1 + [BCS − 5] × 0.10). Example: 45 kg dog with BCS 8 → target = 45 ÷ 1.30 = **34.6 kg**.

**Energy requirement:**
- RER = 70 kcal/kg^0.75/day. Example: 10 kg dog: 70 × 10^0.75 = **394 kcal/day**.
- Initial daily energy goal (dogs): **100% of RER for target weight**.
- Initial daily energy goal (cats): some prefer **80% of RER for target weight**.
- If weight loss is insufficient: reduce to **80–90% of original recommendation**.
- If weight loss is too rapid: raise to **120% of original recommendation**.
- Treats: should not exceed **10% of daily caloric consumption**.
- Monitoring: reassess at **2–4 week intervals**.
- Once ideal BW/BCS attained: increase daily calories by **10–20%** to prevent continued loss.

**OA/pain context:**
- Labrador Retriever lifespan study: 25% caloric restriction vs. ad libitum → multi-joint OA present in **10% vs 77%** of dogs at 8 years; median lifespan **1.8 years shorter** in unrestricted group.
- Leading cause of death in unrestricted group: euthanasia prompted by OA-related immobility and pain.
- Improved mobility typically not noticed until **≥10% weight loss** — owners need this counseling at the outset.
- Physical rehabilitation program: expect 3–6 weeks of comprehensive management before discernible improvement in activity.

**Novel/emerging therapies for OA (Ch 226):**
- Anti-NGF monoclonal antibodies (species-specific): phase of development for dogs and cats.
- Tin-117m radioisotope colloid intra-articular injection: up to 1 year of OA pain relief in dogs (microparticles engulf macrophages → apoptosis via conversion electrons, 0.3 mm range; preserves bone/cartilage/tendons/ligaments).
- Undenatured type II collagen (UC-II): growing evidence of pain relief comparable to NSAID therapy.

### Ch 227 — Concurrent Infection and Immune Suppression

> ClinIQ: concept — could enrich immune-mediated disease pages (IMHA, ITP, IBD, atopy) and infectious disease pages

**Core tension (Ch 227)**
Treating autoimmune disease requires immunosuppression that opens the patient to new infection, reactivation of latent infection, or aggravation of subclinical infection. Infections can also trigger autoimmunity (secondary autoimmunity) or cause immunosuppression themselves, creating a bidirectional and complex interplay.

#### Prevalence data (Ch 227)
- Autoimmunity affects approximately **5% of animals**.
- **One-third** of cyclosporine-treated dogs had ≥1 positive urine culture during treatment.
- **Just under one-fifth** of dogs on long-term corticosteroids developed culture-positive UTIs.
- Neither prednisolone nor cyclosporine appears to increase the incidence of UTIs in **cats**.
- Oclacitinib does NOT increase UTI risk.

#### Infection-induced immunosuppression (Ch 227)
- Feline retroviruses (FeLV, FIV): classic cause of secondary immunodeficiency.
- Parvovirus (puppies): severe gut barrier disruption → sepsis risk; combined with immature immunity.
- CDV: T- and B-cell lymphopenia → secondary bacterial pneumonia; also predisposes to Toxoplasma, Salmonella, Nocardia, demodicosis.
- Ehrlichia spp. (A. phagocytophilum): resides within neutrophils, compromises neutrophil function.
- Nasal aspergillosis: disrupts normal turbinate barrier.
- Bordetella: paralyzes respiratory cilia.

#### Glucocorticoid use in infectious disease (Ch 227)
**Helpful guidelines when steroids used with infections (Ch 227):**
1. **Diagnose the infection before initiating steroid treatment.**
2. **Only use steroids with or after employing anti-infective drugs.**
3. Think critically about justification for steroid use in each case.
4. Monitor patients closely for progressive infection — steroids reduce inflammation and may mask progression.

**Steroid selection when necessary alongside infection (Ch 227):**
- Preferred: short-acting oral or IV formulations with lower relative potency: **prednisolone, prednisone, prednisolone sodium succinate, or methylprednisolone sodium succinate**.
- Dexamethasone: less ideal as first choice.
- **Depot formulations: never use in this context.**
- Typical prednisolone dose: **should not usually exceed 1 mg/kg/day** unless life-threatening autoimmune disease present.

**Context-specific applications (Ch 227):**
- **Blastomycosis/histoplasmosis**: antifungal-induced kill liberates pro-inflammatory fungal components → glucocorticoids may assist. Retrospective: dogs with hilar lymphadenopathy histoplasmosis + glucocorticoids showed quicker clinical response.
- **Feline herpesvirus reactivation (cyclosporine-treated cat)**: as long as respiratory disease remains mild and cat maintains normal habitus → treatment adjustment probably not required; if signs worsen, withdraw cyclosporine and provide supportive care ± antiviral.
- **FIP**: glucocorticoids are commonly suggested as part of management protocol despite lack of controlled studies.
- **Kennel cough**: many veterinarians use anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid doses for short periods for airway irritation.
- **CNS infections**: controlled studies lacking in small animals, but corticosteroids may reduce inflammation-induced injury; collect all diagnostic samples (culture, cytology, titers, PCR) before commencing glucocorticoid treatment.
- **Blood-borne infections (Babesia, Ehrlichia, hemoplasmas) with immune-mediated cytopenias**: addition of glucocorticoids is common to reduce immune-mediated hemolysis/thrombocytopenia — no controlled studies exist.

#### Clinical scenarios: balancing immunosuppression and infection (Ch 227)
1. **Dog on glucocorticoid monotherapy for IHA develops UTI:**
   - Taper steroids as quickly as hematologic response permits + bactericidal antibiotic for 1–2 weeks (to cytologic cure).
   - Reculture urine after antibiotic completion to confirm bacteriological cure.
   - Danger: ascending infection → pyelonephritis; suppression of hemolysis remains therapeutic priority.
   - If infection becomes life-threatening: increase antibiotic intensity to 4-quadrant parenteral drugs.

2. **Cat on cyclosporine for eosinophilic granulomas develops mild upper respiratory tract infection (herpetic):**
   - If mild and cat maintains normal habitus: no treatment adjustment needed.
   - If signs worsen: withdraw cyclosporine + supportive care ± antiviral.
   - Aim for lowest controlling cyclosporine frequency (**>50% of cats maintain control on alternate-day or less frequent dosing**).

3. **Young dog on high-dose prednisolone for steroid-responsive meningitis-arteritis develops sepsis (non-healing ulcer → bacteremia → mitral valve vegetation):**
   - Immediate withdrawal of prednisolone + high-dose IV broad-spectrum antibiotic (after culture/sensitivity samples).
   - Very cautious reintroduction of immunosuppression + long-term oral antibiotics following stabilization.
   - Lesson: non-healing ulcer should prompt evaluation for bacteremia/endocarditis BEFORE potent immunosuppression.

4. **Dog on glucocorticoids for myasthenia gravis develops aspiration pneumonia:**
   - Balance: absolute need for immunosuppression to prevent further aspiration vs. acute septic pneumonia.
   - Add non-steroid lymphocyte-specific agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporine) to allow lowered glucocorticoid dose.
   - Empirical antibiotics (parenteral high-dose combination, e.g., amoxicillin/clavulanate + enrofloxacin); avoid neuromuscular junction-impairing antibiotics (**aminoglycosides, ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, erythromycin, imipenem**).
   - Consider percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube (if anesthesia risk acceptable) to bypass upper GI tract.

#### Sepsis and immune dysregulation (Ch 227)
- Sepsis = hyperinflammation (cytokine storm) early → later immunosuppression + recurrent infection/organ failure; these phases may actually be concurrent.
- In small animals, severe inflammatory disease is a common reason for ICU admission.
- Macrolide antibiotics: beyond antimicrobial effect, potent immunomodulatory potential (key pathways in immune dysregulation); shown to improve survival and reduce inflammation-induced tissue damage in animal models and human RCTs — of emerging interest for sepsis.
- Glucocorticoids in sepsis: long-running debate; associated with relative adrenal insufficiency syndrome; clinical experience suggests new infection/flare-up as complication is not common.

#### Genetic/primary immunodeficiency alerts (Ch 227)
Clinical features suggesting primary immunodeficiency (investigate genetic cause):
- Recurrent, protracted, or chronic infections.
- Infections with opportunistic organisms not usually associated with disease.
- Severe or atypical manifestations.
- Delayed, incomplete, or no response to antimicrobials.
- Adverse events following modified live virus vaccine.

Breed predispositions:
- Miniature Dachshund: Pneumocystis pneumonia.
- German Shepherd Dog: systemic aspergillosis.
- Rottweilers, Doberman Pinschers: parvovirus susceptibility.
- Basset Hounds, Miniature Schnauzers: systemic avian mycobacteriosis, toxoplasmosis, neosporosis.
- Gray Collies: cyclic hematopoiesis (neutrophil malfunction).
- Shar-Pei: myxedematous skin disease with pyoderma predisposition.

#### Post-transplant infection (Ch 227)
- Feline renal transplant (169 cats): infection = second most common cause of death (after organ rejection); 14% of mortalities.
- Most infections occurred **within 2.5 months** of surgery.
- Cats that became diabetic following surgery were **more likely to develop infections**.
