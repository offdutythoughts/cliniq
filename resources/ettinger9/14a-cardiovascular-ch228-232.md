### Ch 228 — Pathophysiology of Heart Failure
*Author: Katherine F. Scollan*

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CARD-MVD, DIS-CARD-DCM, DIS-HCM — pathophysiology background; enriches understanding of remodeling, neurohormonal staging rationale.

#### Definitions (Ch 228)
- **Heart disease**: any cardiac finding outside accepted normality (murmur, ECG abnormality, reduced contractility on echo).
- **Heart failure**: pathophysiologic state where the heart is impaired in its ability to eject or receive blood, overwhelming compensatory mechanisms — present when abnormal cardiac function results in either (a) retention of sodium/water + elevated venous/capillary pressures (congestive/backward failure) **or** (b) inadequate cardiac output (low-output/forward failure).
- **Myocardial failure**: decrease in myocardial contractility — not synonymous with heart failure; heart failure can occur with preserved contractility (e.g., tamponade, acute valve insufficiency).

#### Functional Classification of Heart Failure (Box 228.1)
1. **From impeded cardiac filling**: pericardial effusion with tamponade; constrictive pericarditis; AV valve stenosis; cor triatriatum; HCM; RCM.
2. **From increased resistance to ejection**: outflow tract obstruction (pulmonic/subaortic stenosis, HO cardiomyopathy); thromboembolism; pulmonary hypertension.
3. **From impaired ejection or volume overload**: DCM; ischemic/infectious/nutritional/toxic myocardial disorders; valvular insufficiency; left-to-right shunts, AV fistulas; chronic high-output states (thyrotoxicosis, chronic anemia).
4. **From arrhythmias/conduction disorders**: sustained supraventricular or ventricular tachyarrhythmias; complete heart block.

#### Neurohormonal Alterations
- **SNS**: Dominant early compensatory mechanism; increased HR and contractility. Chronic high NE is maladaptive — beta1 downregulation/uncoupling via beta-arrestin. NE plasma levels correlate with heart failure severity and inversely with survival in humans and dogs. Plasma NE elevated in dogs with DCM and DMVD vs. normal dogs.
- **RAAS**: Activated later than SNS. Stimuli: decreased effective renal perfusion, reduced Na reabsorption, beta1-adrenergic stimulation. Major stimuli for aldosterone: AT II, elevated K, ACTH; also catecholamines, ET-1, AVP. Substantial elevations of plasma renin and aldosterone in dogs with overt CHF due to MR and DCM, and in cats with HCM/RCM. Activation especially marked when furosemide used.
- **AVP (ADH)**: Released by hyperosmolarity, hypovolemia, sympathetic stimulation, AT II. V1A receptors (vasoconstriction, inotropic); V2 receptors (water reabsorption via aquaporin-2). Elevated in dogs with DCM and DMVD. In severe CHF with dilutional hyponatremia.
- **Endothelin-1 (ET-1)**: ETA receptor — vasoconstriction, hypertrophy, aldosterone secretion. ETB — vasodilation via NO. Plasma ET-1 **more than doubles** in dogs with overt CHF due to DMVD or DCM; **more than treefold** increase in cats with cardiomyopathy and CHF.
- **Natriuretic peptides**: ANP and BNP oppose RAAS; natriuresis, diuresis, vasodilation, inhibit renin and aldosterone. NT-proBNP and NT-proANP cleared more slowly (renal excretion), so plasma levels higher and less labile than C-terminal counterparts. Sensitive markers of heart disease; correlate with severity. Point-of-care NT-proBNP assay for cats yields results in-house within 10 minutes.

#### Cardiac Remodeling
- **Law of Laplace**: Wall stress = (pressure × radius) / (2 × wall thickness).
- **Pressure overload** (SAS, PS, hypertension): concentric hypertrophy — sarcomeres replicate in parallel, wall thickens, radius unchanged or decreased. Normal wall stress maintained. Most common consequence of severe SAS in dogs: **sudden death from ventricular arrhythmia**, presumed secondary to myocardial ischemia.
- **Volume overload** (MR, PDA, AR): eccentric hypertrophy — sarcomeres replicate in series, chamber enlarges, wall thickness modestly increased. Wall stress normalized. >75% of total LV stroke volume can be ejected into LA in severe MMVD.
- **DCM**: Mixed pattern, eccentric hypertrophy predominates; collagen matrix disintegrates; chamber dilates.

#### Clinical Thresholds for Signs
- **Pulmonary edema signs** (left heart failure): pulmonary capillary wedge pressure exceeds **25 mm Hg** (normal <10 mm Hg).
- **Ascites/hepatomegaly signs** (right heart failure): central venous pressure exceeds **15 mm Hg** (normal <5 mm Hg).
- **Pleural effusion**: uncommon with isolated right- or left-sided failure; **develops frequently when both systemic and pulmonary venous pressures are elevated concurrently**.

### Ch 229 — Heart Failure: Diagnosis and Management
*Authors: Adrian Boswood, Melanie Hezzell*

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CARD-MVD, DIS-CARD-DCM — enriches with full drug dose tables. ✗ MISSING: dedicated Heart Failure Management protocol page (candidate for new ClinIQ page).

#### Diagnosis of Heart Failure
- Signs: breathlessness/tachypnea, lethargy, restlessness, exercise intolerance, inappetence, weight loss, weakness, collapse, cyanosis. **Coughing** reported in canine advanced heart disease but **rarely, if ever, a consequence of heart failure in cats**.
- CHF unlikely in patient with heart disease but **without significant atrial enlargement** (absence of pericardial effusion).

**Definitive radiographic findings supporting CHF**:
- Cardiomegaly + pulmonary venous congestion + alveolar/interstitial pattern (gold standard for left-sided CHF).
- Pleural effusion + abnormal cardiac silhouette.

**Effusion type**: pleural and abdominal effusions in heart failure are **modified transudates** (high-protein transudates). Any other fluid type makes heart failure much less likely.

**Cardiac biomarkers**: NT-proBNP elevated in cardiac causes; low concentrations make CHF less likely. Point-of-care cTnI also helps differentiate heart failure from other dyspnea causes, particularly in cats.

**Resting respiratory rate (RRR) monitoring**: dogs with CHF well-controlled rarely have RRR >30 breaths/min. In CHF monitoring, **RRR <40 breaths/min strongly associated with adequate CHF control in dogs**.

#### Drug Doses — Heart Failure Treatment (Box 229.2)

**Preload Reduction — Diuretics:**
- **Furosemide**:
  - Oral: **1–4 mg/kg q8–12h**
  - IV bolus (emergency): **1–4 mg/kg**, repeat at intervals of 4 hours (or less if severe) until clinical signs improve
  - IV CRI: **0.5–1 mg/kg/h** until signs improve
  - Total daily doses >**12 mg/kg/day** should be avoided if possible
  - Failure to improve after multiple IV doses or CRI >4 hours → re-evaluate diagnosis
- **Spironolactone**: Oral: **2 mg/kg q24h** (dog). No evidence that higher doses improve control. Potassium-sparing diuretic + aldosterone receptor antagonist. Effective in clinical trials of dogs with CHF from MMVD. Possible benefit in cats (limited data). Caution: possible cutaneous adverse reactions in cats.
- **Torsemide (torasemide)**:
  - **0.1–0.6 mg/kg q24h** (majority of dogs initially stabilized at ≤0.3 mg/kg q24h)
  - Furosemide-to-torsemide conversion: total daily torsemide dose ≈ 1/10th total daily furosemide dose (example: 120 mg furosemide/day → 12 mg torsemide/day → 6 mg PO q12h). At higher doses, equivalence may be 1:20.
  - Two recent studies (CARPODIEM, TEST): oral torsemide once daily equivalent in effectiveness to furosemide as first-line CHF treatment in dogs with MMVD.
  - Oral only — cannot be given parenterally in emergencies.
- **Hydrochlorothiazide**: Oral: **0.5–4 mg/kg q12–24h** (lower end in combination with other diuretics). Sequential nephron blockade with loop + potassium-sparing diuretics.

**Venodilators:**
- **Nitroglycerin**: 2.5 cm of 2% transdermal ointment per 10 kg body weight (percutaneous).

**Afterload Reduction — Vasodilators:**
- **Enalapril**: Oral: **0.5 mg/kg q12–24h**
- **Benazepril**: Oral: **0.25–0.5 mg/kg q24h**
- **Ramipril**: Oral: **0.125 mg/kg q24h**
- **Imidapril**: Oral: **0.25 mg/kg q24h**
- **Amlodipine** (for heart failure in dogs): Oral: **0.05–0.1 mg/kg q24h**. Considered second/third-line vasodilator in normotensive dogs with refractory CHF already on pimobendan + ACEI.
- **Nitroprusside**: IV CRI: **1–5 mcg/kg/min**. Start at low dose, titrate up, monitor blood pressure. ICU only.

**Right ventricular afterload — Pulmonary vasodilator:**
- **Sildenafil**: Oral: wide range reported **0.25 mg/kg q12h up to 2 mg/kg q8h**; start low, increase gradually.

**Improved Cardiac Filling — Lusitropic:**
- **Diltiazem** (cats): Oral: **10 mg/cat q8h**; various slow-release preparations also used but pharmacokinetics less established.

**Enhanced Cardiac Contractility — Inotropic:**
- **Dobutamine**:
  - Dog: IV CRI **2–15 mcg/kg/min**
  - Cat: IV CRI **1–5 mcg/kg/min**
  - Start at low end; titrate to effect; monitor for arrhythmias.
- **Pimobendan**:
  - Dog: Oral **0.1–0.3 mg/kg q12h**
  - Cat (empirical): similar dosages used empirically
  - IV (available in Europe): bolus **0.15 mg/kg**
  - "Inodilator" — calcium sensitization + phosphodiesterase inhibition.
  - Indicated for all dogs with CHF secondary to MMVD or DCM. Contraindicated in dogs with ventricular outflow tract obstruction or pericardial tamponade.
  - Recent prospective randomized trial in cats with HCM-related CHF **failed to show clear benefit** of pimobendan.

#### ACEI in Heart Failure
- BENCH and LIVE trials (1990s): dogs with CHF receiving ACEI + standard therapy had less severe signs and lived longer than standard therapy alone.
- QUEST study (stage C MMVD): pimobendan + diuretics → median survival **267 days** vs. benazepril + diuretics → median survival **140 days** (P=0.0099).
- ACEI still recommended at stages C/D, but VALVE trial (ramipril added to furosemide + pimobendan) showed no additional survival benefit; debate ongoing.
- **Preclinical MMVD (Stage B)**: Three prospective placebo-controlled studies in dogs with preclinical MVD **failed to conclusively demonstrate** prolongation of time to onset of CHF with ACEIs.

#### Prognosis — General CHF Outcome
- **Dogs with DCM**: median survival time **<6 months** after CHF diagnosis.
- **Most canine and feline patients** with heart failure die of heart disease within **1 year of diagnosis**.

### Ch 230 — Cardiac Arrhythmias
*Authors: Kathy Wright, Yanni Giatis*

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CARD-ARRHYTHMIA — major enrichment; full antiarrhythmic drug doses, arrhythmia classification, pacemaker indications. ✗ MISSING: individual pages for AF, SSS, AVB, OAVRT (all candidates for new ClinIQ pages).

#### ECG Definitions
- **Tachyarrhythmia**: ≥3 consecutive complexes at rate >**160 bpm** (dogs) or >**220 bpm** (cats).
- **Bradyarrhythmia**: HR <**70 bpm** (dogs) or <**140 bpm** (cats).
- **Narrow complex tachyarrhythmia (NCT)**: QRS ≤**60 msec** (dogs) or ≤**40 msec** (cats).
- **Wide complex tachyarrhythmia (WCT)**: QRS beyond these values.
- **First-degree AVB**: P-Q interval >**120 ms** (dogs) or >**80 ms** (cats).
- **High-grade 2nd-degree AVB** (dogs): AV conduction at least **3:1** with atrial rate <200 bpm.

#### Narrow Complex Tachyarrhythmias

**Atrial Fibrillation (AF)**
- Most common arrhythmia in dogs; prevalence 0.1–0.43% in dogs presenting to clinic; 6.27–10.5% in dogs with clinical heart disease.
- Predisposed populations: (1) severe underlying structural heart disease with severe atrial dilation; (2) giant breed dogs (Irish Wolfhounds: prevalence 8.9–12%, up to 28% in IWs >8 years; dominant inheritance).
- AF in cats: almost always associated with severe structural heart disease.
- ECG: absent P waves; fibrillatory (f) waves; **irregularly irregular** R-R intervals; narrow QRS (unless conduction defect).

**AF Rate Control Goal**: average daily ventricular rate **<125 bpm** in dogs. In cats: in-hospital ECG ventricular rate **<200 bpm**.

**AF Treatment:**
- **Diltiazem** (first-line): standard release **1–2 mg/kg PO q8h** (dogs and cats); sustained release **1–4 mg/kg PO q12h** (dogs) or **30–45 mg total dose PO q12–24h** (cats). Sustained-release has higher GI side-effects in cats.
- **Digoxin** (added if rate control inadequate): **0.003–0.007 mg/kg PO q12h** (dogs, based on lean body weight, normokalemic, normal renal function). **0.0312 mg total PO q24–48h** in normokalemic cats with normal renal function. Rarely used in cats.
- **Atenolol**: less commonly used; reserved for select cases (e.g., SAS, HO cardiomyopathy) when no active CHF.
- Pharmacologic cardioversion: rarely attempted; amiodarone is agent of choice for maintaining sinus rhythm after electrical or pharmacologic cardioversion.
- Electrical cardioversion (DCCV): success inversely linked to AF duration and atrial size.

**Focal Atrial Tachycardia (FAT)**
- Acute IV rate control: **Diltiazem 0.125 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes** (can repeat up to cumulative 0.375 mg/kg).
- Acute IV termination: **Nexterone (amiodarone without preservatives) 3–5 mg/kg IV over 15 minutes** in dogs; if successful, CRI: **0.014 mg/kg/min for 6–8 hours, then 0.007 mg/kg/min for up to 18 more hours**.
- Chronic suppression: propafenone **5–8 mg/kg PO q8h** (non-sustained-release, dogs); sotalol **1–3 mg/kg PO q12h** (dogs and cats); amiodarone **15 mg/kg PO q24h × 7–10 days, then 10 mg/kg PO q24h × 7–10 days, then 5–8 mg/kg PO q24h** long-term (dogs).
- Negative dromotropes: diltiazem **1–2 mg/kg PO q8h** (non-sustained-release, dogs and cats); digoxin (as above); atenolol **0.5–1 mg/kg PO q12h** (dogs and cats).

**OAVRT (Orthodromic Atrioventricular Reciprocating Tachycardia)**
- Median OAVRT rate: 250–428 bpm in dogs.
- Acute termination:
  - **Lidocaine**: **2 mg/kg IV bolus over 20–30 seconds**; if no cardioversion after 2 min and no adverse effects, repeat 2 mg/kg IV boluses to **total cumulative dose 8 mg/kg**; if successful → CRI **25–75 mcg/kg/min**.
  - Cats (much more sensitive to lidocaine): **0.25–0.75 mg/kg IV slowly**; CRI **10–20 mcg/kg/min**.
  - **IV Diltiazem**: **0.125–0.35 mg/kg slowly IV over 3 minutes**; if needed → CRI **0.125–0.35 mg/kg/hr**.
  - Nexterone: dosages as for FAT above.
- Chronic therapy: AP suppression with class IA (procainamide), IB (mexiletine **4–8 mg/kg PO q8h** dogs; cats: **1–2 mg/kg PO q12h**), IC (propafenone, flecainide), III (sotalol, amiodarone). AVN slowing with CCBs, beta-blockers, digoxin.
- RFCA: highly effective cure for OAVRT; TICM resolves within **1–6 months** of successful ablation.

#### Wide Complex Tachyarrhythmias (VT/VPCs)

**Decision to treat VAs:**
- Single VPCs, AIVRs, and VAs with mild complexity and rate <200 bpm: **generally do not require AA therapy**.
- Sustained rapid VT (>200 bpm): **medical emergency** — can degenerate into VF.
- Non-sustained rapid runs (≥3 consecutive VPCs at >200 bpm, <30 sec): generally warrant AA therapy (limited survival benefit evidence).
- VF: always requires immediate **defibrillation**.

**Acute/Emergency VT Treatment:**
- **Lidocaine** (first-line): same protocol as for OAVRT above (2 mg/kg IV bolus, repeat to 8 mg/kg cumulative, then CRI 25–75 mcg/kg/min in dogs).
- **Procainamide** (second-line): **6–8 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes** (dogs); **1–2 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes** (cats). Infusion: **20–40 mcg/kg/min** (dogs); **10–20 mcg/kg/min** (cats).
- **Nexterone (amiodarone)** (alternative second-line): **3–5 mg/kg IV over 15 minutes** (dogs); used successfully in cats with VT at **2–3 mg/kg IV over 15 minutes** (limited cat data).
- **Magnesium sulfate** (torsades de pointes, refractory VT, hypomagnesemia): **30 mg/kg IV slowly over 5–10 minutes** (equivalent to 0.243 mEq/kg).

**Chronic Oral VT Treatment:**
- **Sotalol**: **1–3 mg/kg PO q12h** (dogs and cats); one of most commonly used.
- **Mexiletine**: **4–8 mg/kg PO q8h** (dogs); class IB, often combined with sotalol or other AA. Rarely used in cats (side-effects).
- **Propafenone**: effective class IC (dogs, dose as for FAT above).
- **Amiodarone** (dogs): superior efficacy to sotalol for severe VAs; retrospective study: improvement in 26/28 dogs. Monitor serum biochemistry + total T4 + TSH every 3–4 months; CBC every 6 months. Adverse effects in 30% of Dobermans at high doses (vomiting, anorexia, hepatopathy, thrombocytopenia).

**Accelerated Idioventricular Rhythm (AIVR)**: spontaneous ventricular rhythm **40–160 bpm**; not hemodynamically compromised; treat underlying extracardiac cause; **AA therapy not generally required**.

#### Bradyarrhythmias

**Sinus Bradycardia:**
- HR <70 bpm (dogs) or <140 bpm (cats) in clinical setting.
- Atropine response test: **0.04 mg/kg atropine IV**, ECG q1 min × 5 min, then at 10, 15 min.
- Oral treatment: **Theophylline SR 10 mg/kg PO q12h**; **terbutaline 0.1–0.2 mg/kg q8–12h** (dogs); **propantheline bromide 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q8h**; **hyoscyamine sulfate 0.003–0.006 mg/kg PO q8h**.

**Atrioventricular Block:**
- First-degree: P-Q >120 ms (dogs), >80 ms (cats); no clinical signs; no treatment needed.
- Second-degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach): physiologic/non-pathologic (involving compact AV node); no treatment.
- Second-degree Mobitz II: typically involves infranodal conduction system; can progress to 3rd degree; structural disease.
- Third-degree AVB:
  - AV junctional escape rates (dogs): **35–65 bpm**.
  - Ventricular (Purkinje) escape rates (dogs): **20–40 bpm**.
  - In cats: 90% have narrow escape complex (block within AV node); escape rates typically **80–140 bpm**.
  - Prognosis: high-grade 2nd-degree and 3rd-degree AVB — high risk of sudden death (~40%) in dogs; in cats with 3rd-degree AVB, median survival **386 days**, with or without CHF.
  - Treatment: **permanent pacemaker implantation** (choice); oral therapy (theophylline, hyoscyamine) as bridge in some patients.

**Sick Sinus Syndrome (SSS) / Sinus Nodal Disease (SND):**
- Breeds: Cocker Spaniels, West Highland White Terriers, Miniature Schnauzers. Older females.
- Syncope in ~95% when clinical signs present; multiple daily episodes possible.
- Mean longest sinus arrest: **2.5 seconds** in clinic; **7.3 seconds** on Holter.
- Preferred oral therapy: **hyoscyamine 0.003–0.006 mg/kg PO q8h**.
- SSS/SND patients do **not** have higher risk of sudden cardiac death (unlike high-grade AVB).
- Median survival: **~18 months** with median age of death **14 years**.
- Pacemaker improves clinical signs but does not appear to prolong survival vs. oral therapy.

**Persistent Atrial Standstill (PAS):**
- No P waves; nodal escape rhythm 35–65 bpm (dogs).
- Breeds: English Springer Spaniels, Labrador Retrievers.
- Treatment: CHF management + permanent pacemaker.
- With pacemaker: median survival **866 days**.

#### Pacemaker Indications
Permanent pacemaker implantation indicated for: 3rd-degree AVB; high-grade 2nd-degree AVB (with clinical signs); SSS (with clinical signs); persistent atrial standstill; vasovagal syncope (when clinical signs present).

#### Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)

**Key findings:**
- Sex predisposition: **2.49/1000 females vs. 1.45/1000 males**.
- Classic: **continuous murmur** loudest near S2, best at left craniodorsal heart base; hyperkinetic ("waterhammer") pulse.
- Peak left-to-right ductal jet velocity: typically **≥4.5–5.0 m/s**, occurring at end-systole on CW Doppler.
- LVOT velocity with PDA: **1.8–2.3 m/s** (sometimes higher).

**Natural history (left-to-right PDA):**
- Untreated: median age of death **2 years**; treated (occlusion/ligation): median age of death **12 years**.
- Hazard ratio of **not correcting**: 16.9.
- 64% of untreated dogs died within 1 year.

**Right-to-left PDA (reversed PDA):**
- Causes: wide cylindrical ductus + persistent pulmonary hypertension (most develop before 6 months in dogs; cats more gradual).
- Signs: differential cyanosis (caudal mucous membranes cyanotic, cranial pink); exertional hindlimb weakness; secondary erythrocytosis.
- PCV: hyperviscosity typically when **PCV >68%**; manage PCV **58–65%** with phlebotomy.
- Treatment: exercise restriction, prevent dehydration, periodic phlebotomy (avoid aggressive); sildenafil **1–3 mg/kg PO q8–12h** may improve signs.
- **Surgical closure strongly contraindicated** in reversed PDA — invariably leads to acute right heart failure and death.

**Concurrent pulmonary hypertension with left-to-right PDA:** Try sildenafil **1–3 mg/kg PO q8–12h** for at least 3–4 weeks before considering closure.

#### Pulmonic Stenosis (PS)

**Severity classification** by Doppler-derived RV–PA systolic pressure gradient:
- **Mild**: <50 mm Hg
- **Moderate**: 50–80 mm Hg
- **Severe**: >80 mm Hg (commonly used clinical cutoff for intervention)
- **Very severe / very high risk**: >125 mm Hg (frequent secondary TR, heart failure, syncope, serious arrhythmias)

**Key points:**
- Most common canine breeds: Basset Hound, Beagle, Boykin Spaniel, Bull Terrier, Chihuahua, Chow Chow, Cocker Spaniel, English Bulldog (risk for anomalous coronary), Boxer (anomalous coronary), Samoyed, Schnauzer, Terriers, West Highland White Terrier.
- ~35% of dogs with severe PS show clinical signs (exertional fatigue, syncope, ascites).
- Dogs with gradients >125 mm Hg: frequently develop serious complications; those with gradients <80 mm Hg usually live normal/near-normal lives.
- **CRITICAL**: In English Bulldogs and Boxers with PS — **anomalous coronary artery (R2A malformation)** encircles RVOT; vigorous balloon valvuloplasty **contraindicated** — associated with coronary artery avulsion and sudden death. Coronary arteriography (or CT) **mandatory** before surgery or valvuloplasty in these breeds.
- Treatment: balloon valvuloplasty in dogs with severe PS → reduced clinical signs and cardiac-related death.

#### Subaortic Stenosis (SAS)

**Severity classification** by Doppler-derived LV–aortic pressure gradient:
- **Mild**: <50 mm Hg
- **Moderate**: 50–80 mm Hg
- **Severe**: 80–130 mm Hg
- **Very severe**: >130 mm Hg

**Key echo thresholds:**
- Averaged maximum pulsed-wave aortic velocity in healthy dogs without murmurs: **<1.8 m/s** from left apex.
- Diagnosis of mild SAS more secure when averaged maximal velocity exceeds **2.4 m/s** + accompanied by disturbed flow, anatomic lesion on 2D, holodiastolic AR, abrupt acceleration over discrete LVOT region.
- LV outflow tract area indexed to body surface area **<1.46 cm/m²** or aortoseptal angle **<145°** as indicators of mild/developing SAS in Golden Retriever pups.

**Natural history and prognosis:**
- Pressure gradient <50 mm Hg: likely normal life.
- Pressure gradient >130 mm Hg: very likely to develop endocarditis and/or CHF or **sudden death**; **median survival 3 years** in severe cases.
- Most common complication of severe SAS: sudden death (most likely during/shortly after vigorous activity); typically at **1–3 years of age**.
- Complicating factors: progressive LV diastolic/systolic dysfunction, MR, AR, aortic valve endocarditis, atrial fibrillation.

**Management:**
- Mild SAS: prophylactic antibiotics during anticipated bacteremia (dental procedures, surgery, infection).
- Moderate-severe: exercise restriction. Beta-blockers (atenolol) to reduce heart rate, decrease O2 consumption, increase diastolic coronary flow. Retrospective study: **no survival benefit of atenolol** in dogs with SAS.
- Balloon dilation: reduces severity by ~50% short-term; benefit attenuates over time; has **not been shown to improve survival vs. medical therapy with atenolol**.
- Open resection (cardiopulmonary bypass): can substantially reduce gradient; does **not appear to substantially alter prevalence of sudden death**.

#### Septal Defects

**ASD:**
- Typically soft grade 2–3/6 systolic ejection murmur over left heart base + splitting of S2.
- Left-to-right shunt; rarely needs treatment (small isolated ASDs).
- Right heart failure possible with large defects.

**VSD:**
- Small resistive membranous VSD: harsh holosystolic murmur best over right ventral mid-to-cranial precordium; high-velocity jet >4.5 m/s through defect (RV pressure ~20–25 mmHg vs. LV ~100–120 mmHg).
- Large non-resistive VSD: pressures equilibrate; CHF or pulmonary hypertension unavoidable without PS.
- Doppler peak velocity <4.5 m/s through VSD → RV systolic pressure is elevated.
- Small animals with small isolated VSD: usually live normal lifespan.
- Eisenmenger's syndrome (reversed shunting): PCV management (58–65%); sildenafil for pulmonary vasodilation; **surgical closure contraindicated**.

#### Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF)

**Four defining features**: (1) RV outflow obstruction (infundibular + often pulmonic valve hypoplasia); (2) secondary RV hypertrophy; (3) large perimembranous VSD; (4) rightward-positioned aorta (dextroaorta).

**Mean survival**: ~2 years in case series; with soft/no murmurs, mean ~3 months.

**Management:**
- Periodic phlebotomy: maintain PCV **62–68%**.
- Acute hypoxemic spells: cage rest, O2, IV fluids, sodium bicarbonate (if metabolic acidosis), phenylephrine (reduce right-to-left shunting). **Avoid systemic vasodilators**.
- Surgical palliation: systemic-to-pulmonary shunt (Blalock-Thomas-Taussig variant) can increase pulmonary perfusion and improve oxygenation.
- Beta-blockers (propranolol): blunt dynamic infundibular obstruction and limit exercise intensity (no controlled veterinary trials).

#### Tricuspid Valve Dysplasia (TVD)

- Autosomal dominant mutation with incomplete penetrance mapped to chromosome 9 in Labrador Retrievers.
- Distinctive ECG: **splintered or notched QRS complexes**.
- May cause cyanosis via right-to-left shunting across PFO or ASD.
- Treatment: medical CHF management; periodic paracentesis for refractory right heart failure; atenolol for LVOT obstruction secondary to SAM of mitral valve.

### Ch 232 — Valvular Heart Diseases of Adult Dogs and Cats (MMVD + Infective Endocarditis)
*Authors: Ingrid Ljungvall, Jens Häggström*

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CARD-MVD — MAJOR enrichment; complete ACVIM staging criteria, EPIC trial data, all drug doses, LA rupture, IE. ✗ MISSING: dedicated Infective Endocarditis page (candidate).

#### Myxomatous Mitral Valve Disease (MMVD)

**Epidemiology:**
- Accounts for **>75%** of all cardiac disease in dogs.
- In CKCS: ~50% of dogs aged 6–7 years affected; **nearly 100% of CKCS aged >10 years** affected.
- Prevalence: higher in males at any given age; small-to-medium breeds (CKCS, Dachshund, Miniature Poodle, Yorkshire Terrier).

#### ACVIM MMVD Staging (Table 232.1) — COMPLETE CRITERIA

| Stage | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **A** | Higher than average risk for MMVD but **no apparent structural abnormality** (no audible murmur) at time of examination |
| **B1** | Preclinical MMVD with MR **not severe enough** to meet B2 criteria used for triggering medical treatment |
| **B2** | Preclinical MMVD with MR **and LA/LV enlargement** meeting **all** EPIC trial inclusion criteria: **murmur intensity ≥3/6** AND **LA/Ao (right-sided short-axis view, early diastole) ≥1.6** AND **LVIDDN ≥1.7** AND **breed-adjusted radiographic VHS >10.5** |
| **C** | MMVD severe enough to cause current or past clinical signs of CHF, **not refractory** to standard treatment |
| **D** | MMVD with CHF **refractory** to standard treatment — requiring **>8 mg/kg/day of furosemide** (or equivalent torsemide), administered concurrently with standard dosages of other medications |

**ACVIM B2 Echocardiographic Criteria (EPIC trial inclusion):**
- Murmur intensity **≥3/6**
- **LA/Ao ≥ 1.6** (right-sided short-axis view, early diastole) — separates B1 from B2
- **LVIDDN ≥ 1.7** (normalized LV internal diameter in diastole on M-mode)
- Breed-adjusted radiographic **VHS >10.5** (the table states 10.5; the text footnote references breed-adjusted VHS >10.5 for the breed in general, noting some sources state 11.5 for some breeds — see below)

**Additional radiographic VHS thresholds referenced in Ch 232:**
- LA/Ao **≥ 2.0** heralds CHF in development or already present.
- VHS radiographic score increasing is the highest prognostic variable for disease progression.

**Vertebral Heart Score (VHS) — referenced normal values:**
- Stage B1 dog: normal heart size (example in text: **VHS 10.2**; normal lung fields)
- Stage B2 early: mild cardiomegaly (**VHS 11.4**); normal lung fields
- Stage B2 late: more advanced cardiomegaly (**VHS 12.2**); tracheal elevation, prominent vasculature, no pulmonary edema
- Stage C: severe cardiomegaly (**VHS 14.6**); pulmonary congestion + interstitial edema

#### Clinical Monitoring Thresholds
- **Sleeping/resting respiratory rate (SRR/RRR)**: normal dogs and stage B dogs in thermoneutral, calm environment at home rarely exceed **30 breaths/min**. SRR/RRR consistently **>30 breaths/min** warrants further investigation for developing CHF.
- Clinic-measured respiratory rate unreliable (stress-elevated); SRR/RRR at home is preferred monitoring tool.

#### EPIC Trial (Ch 232 — Pimobendan in Preclinical B2 MMVD)
- International, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial (n=178 pimobendan, n=176 placebo).
- **Result**: Pimobendan in stage B2 MMVD prolonged time to primary endpoint (stage C or cardiac-related death).
- **Pimobendan median time to endpoint: 1228 days** (95% CI: 856 to NA).
- **Placebo median time to endpoint: 766 days** (95% CI: 667–875).
- P = **0.0038**.

#### QUEST Trial (Stage C MMVD)
- Pimobendan + diuretics (n=124) vs. benazepril + diuretics (n=128).
- **Pimobendan median survival: 267 days** (95% CI: 122–523).
- **Benazepril median survival: 140 days** (95% CI: 67–311).
- P = **0.0099**.

#### Management — Preclinical (Stage B)

**Stage B1**: Medical treatment **not recommended** — no therapy shown to have beneficial effects.

**Stage B2 — Pimobendan (only recommended drug):**
- Dose: **0.2–0.3 mg/kg PO q12h** (approved in many countries based on EPIC trial).
- Mechanism: calcium sensitization + phosphodiesterase inhibition → positive inotropic + vasodilatory ("inodilator").
- Reduces heart size (inotropic + vasodilatory effects) — presumed mechanism for benefit.
- ACEIs: **no evidence** they slow or prevent CHF progression at stage B. SVEP and VETPROOF trials (enalapril) and benazepril+spironolactone trial (DELAY study) — all failed to show delay in CHF onset vs. placebo.
- Beta-blockers (bisoprolol): large prospective trial **terminated prematurely for lack of efficacy** in stage B2 MMVD.

**Stage B2 — Owner instructions:**
- Count SRR; if consistently >30 breaths/min → seek evaluation.
- Strenuous exercise and high-sodium diets should be avoided in more advanced B2 dogs.
- Rechecks: every 6–12 months; more frequent if murmur has intensified from mild to moderate/severe.
- Previously B1 dogs: re-check with echo if murmur has increased to assess EPIC criteria fulfillment.

#### Management — Clinical (Stages C and D)

**Diuretic Treatment:**
- Loop diuretic (furosemide or torsemide) as first-line; dosage kept at minimum needed to keep dog free of CHF signs.
- Add spironolactone (alone or in combination with benazepril formulation) to loop diuretic.
- For refractory stage D (requiring >8 mg/kg/day furosemide equivalent): add hydrochlorothiazide for sequential nephron blockade.
- Mechanical treatment: abdominal paracentesis or thoracocentesis for severe ascites/pleural effusion causing respiratory distress.

**Pimobendan (stage C/D — first-line):**
- QUEST trial demonstrates superiority to ACEI + diuretics.
- Additional benefits: reduced heart rate, reduced pulmonary transit times, reduced free water retention.

**Digoxin**: Used almost exclusively as antiarrhythmic drug in dogs with MMVD at this stage (weak inotrope).

**Dobutamine**: For critically ill patients too sick to wait for oral inotropy (no clinical trial data in MMVD dogs).

**ACEIs (stage C/D)**: Recommended based on 1990s clinical trials; however, VALVE trial (ramipril added to furosemide + pimobendan) showed **no additional survival benefit** of adding ramipril; debate ongoing. ACEIs provide vasodilation and inhibit fluid retention, complementary to diuretics.

**Prognosis:**
- More than **50% of dogs that develop CHF due to MMVD do not survive more than 1 year** after CHF diagnosis.
- Worse outcome: worsened exercise tolerance, decreased appetite/cardiac cachexia, more severe mitral valve prolapse, increasing furosemide maintenance dose, increasing cardiac size/MR severity, worsened LV systolic function, decreasing serum creatinine, development of PH/syncope/atrial fibrillation/chordal rupture.

#### Complications

**Left Atrial Rupture:**
- Full-thickness rupture → hemopericardium → cardiac tamponade → often **sudden death**.
- Precipitants: trauma, excitement, exercise.
- Diagnosis: significant pericardial effusion + MMVD on echo.
- Treatment of atrial rupture with hemopericardium: **usually futile**. Immediate pericardiocentesis can be attempted (remove enough to alleviate tamponade without stimulating further bleeding). Emergency thoracotomy with pericardiectomy and closure: **generally not recommended** (poor prognosis).

**Chordal Rupture:**
- Suspect when acute severe left-sided CHF + PH in MMVD dog.
- Most important: rupture of first-order chordae attached to septal leaflet.
- Complete flail of one leaflet: medical treatment **usually futile**.
- Requires intensive care; longer time to stabilize than without chordal rupture.

**Pulmonary Hypertension (PH):**
- Common complication in longstanding severe MMVD; typically **mild to moderate**.
- Standard MMVD therapy + sildenafil if signs suggestive of PH (right-sided CHF, syncope with exertion) present even without pulmonary edema.

### Infective Endocarditis (IE) (Ch 232)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate for new DIS-CARD-IE page).

#### Occurrence
- Incidence: **~0.1% in dogs** in teaching hospital case series; true incidence likely higher.
- Much lower in cats. Risk: male sex, medium-to-large breed, middle-aged.
- Congenital predisposition: SAS, PDA associated with increased IE risk (some reports).
- Dogs with MMVD **not shown** to have higher IE risk despite dental exposure.

#### Common Organisms
Dogs and cats: Staphylococcus spp. (S. aureus, S. pseudintermedius), Streptococcus spp. (S. canis, S. bovis, beta-hemolytic), **Bartonella spp.** (common in some geographic regions), E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Corynebacterium spp.

#### Valves Affected
Mitral and aortic valves most common; aortic slightly less common than mitral overall. Tricuspid and pulmonic less often. Mural endocardium may also be affected (especially with aortic IE).

#### Clinical Features
- Fever (sustained or undulant) in **50–90%** of dogs.
- Lameness in **30–50%** (immune-mediated or septic polyarthritis, peripheral arterial thromboembolism).
- CHF from valve insufficiency in **~50%** of dogs.
- Arrhythmias (primarily ventricular) in **50–75%**.
- Thromboembolism in **~30–40%**; lungs most common site, then kidneys, distal aorta.

#### Diagnosis — Modified Duke Criteria Adapted for Dogs
**Definite IE** (histopathologic confirmation of vegetation OR):
- 2 major criteria, OR
- 1 major + 2 minor criteria

**Major criteria (dogs):**
- Positive echocardiographic: vegetative lesion, destructive lesion, abscess, new valvular insufficiency, **or AR greater than mild** (in absence of SAS, congenital aortic valvular malformation, severe systemic hypertension, or annuloaortic ectasia)
- Positive blood cultures: ≥2 positive cultures of organisms consistent with IE, or ≥3 positive cultures of common skin contaminants

**Minor criteria (dogs):** Fever; medium-to-large breed dog (>15 kg); SAS; vascular phenomena (arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, intracranial hemorrhage); immunological phenomena (glomerulonephritis, polyarthritis, vasculitis); Bartonella-positive PCR and/or titer >1:1024; positive blood culture not meeting major criteria; new/worsening murmur; chronic indwelling catheter; immunocompromised state; predisposing heart conditions (other than SAS); repeated non-sterile IV drug administration; echocardiogram consistent with IE but not meeting major criteria.

#### Management of IE

**Blood cultures**: 60–70% negative in dogs with IE. Collect aseptically.

**Initial empirical IV antibiotics (while awaiting culture results):**
- **Amoxicillin 20 mg/kg IV q8h** (beta-lactam, broad-spectrum)
- **+ Amikacin 20 mg/kg IV q24h** (aminoglycoside — for complete spectrum; **nephrotoxic — limited duration; avoid if CHF + furosemide** as concurrent furosemide enhances nephrotoxicity; support with IV fluids)
- Alternative to aminoglycosides (Gram-negative coverage): **enrofloxacin 5 mg/kg IV q12h** (q24h in cats) or **marbofloxacin 2 mg/kg IV q24h** (local resistance patterns may limit usefulness)

**Duration of therapy**: at least **6 weeks** is suggested (limited veterinary data).
**IV therapy**: 1–2 weeks intensively; then SC or PO when patient eating and hemodynamically stable.

**Prophylaxis** for cardiovascular interventional procedures (pacemaker implantation, balloon valvuloplasty, PDA/VSD closure): prophylactic antibiotics **1–2 hours before** and **6 hours after**. Amoxicillin is first choice; clindamycin as alternative.

#### IE Prognosis
- Worse: late diagnosis, late start of therapy; aortic valve involvement; valvular vegetation; Gram-negative infection (especially Bartonella spp. in aortic IE); cardiac and/or renal complications; septic embolization; thrombocytopenia; elevated ALP; hypoproteinemia; corticosteroids without antibiotics; inappropriate antibiotics; premature therapy termination.
- Better: isolated mitral valve involvement; Gram-positive organisms; integument-origin infections (skin abscesses, cellulitis, wound infections).
