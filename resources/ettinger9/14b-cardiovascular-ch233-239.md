### Ch 233 — Myocardial Diseases of Dogs

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CARD-DCM (DCM)  
> Worth enriching: occult-phase Holter/echo cutoffs; pimobendan dose in preclinical disease; ACEI dosing; taurine/carnitine supplementation; ARVC Boxer staging.

#### Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM) — Dog

**Definition / Phenotype**  
LV or biventricular systolic dysfunction + dilatation, not attributable to volume overload, shunts, valve disease, tachyarrhythmia, or systemic hypertension. (Ch 233)

**Breeds / Prevalence**  
Doberman Pinscher (DP): 10% prevalence ages 2–4 yr, 44% age >6 yr; Irish Wolfhound (IW): up to 25%; Great Dane: up to 36%; Newfoundland: ~10%.  
Median age of diagnosis (DP): ~6 years. Males show LV dilatation earlier; females more arrhythmic phenotype in DP. (Ch 233)

**Genetic testing (DP)**  
PDK4 and titin mutations tested through NC State Veterinary Cardiac Genetics Lab. Negative test does not exclude DCM; positive does not guarantee expression. Homozygotes at higher risk — not recommended for breeding. (Ch 233)

**Clinical Staging (Occult vs Clinical)**  
- **Occult (preclinical) phase**: Echocardiographic or ECG evidence without owner-perceived signs; may last months to years.  
- **Clinical phase**: CHF (left-sided pulmonary edema; right-sided effusions/ascites), syncope, sudden death.  
- Sudden death is first clinical sign in 25–30% of DP with DCM. (Ch 233)

**Echocardiographic Diagnosis — DP Screening Guidelines (ESVC/ESVC 2017 criteria)**  
- LV M-mode or Simpson's biplane measurements above breed-adjusted reference intervals.  
- Guidelines proposed involving major and minor criteria (not yet prospectively validated for all breeds). (Ch 233)

**Holter / ECG Criteria — Doberman Pinscher**  
- **>300 PVCs in 24 hours** on a single Holter recording → suggestive of DCM.  
- **Two Holter recordings within 1 year with 50–300 PVCs/24h each** → also suggestive. (Ch 233)  
- In DP, VA typically precede or co-occur with echo changes; Great Danes also commonly have VA. IW: AF in >70% preclinical; AF in >95% clinical. (Ch 233)

**Treatment — Preclinical DCM**  
- **Pimobendan**: median dose **0.24–0.26 mg/kg PO q12h, ~1 hour before feeding** — shown to prolong time to CHF/sudden death in DP (PROTECT study) and in IW. (Ch 233)  
- **Benazepril**: median dosage **0.5 mg/kg PO q24h** — significantly prolonged time to CHF/sudden death in DP (55–64% risk reduction). Author advocates ACEI in preclinical DCM unless contraindicated. (Ch 233)  
- **Taurine supplementation**: **30–50 mg/kg PO q12h** — initiate if taurine deficiency suspected/documented. (Ch 233)  
- **L-carnitine**: **70 mg/kg PO q8h** — may add in Cocker Spaniels and Boxers. (Ch 233)  
- AA therapy for significant VA (frequent PVCs with couplets/triplets, or VT with instantaneous HR >180 bpm): sotalol, mexiletine, amiodarone, or combination. Holter recheck within 2–3 weeks. (Ch 233)

**Treatment — Clinical DCM (CHF)**  
- **Furosemide** — first-line diuretic; IV or oral depending on severity. (Ch 233)  
- **ACEI** (enalapril or benazepril) — multiple veterinary trials showed improved QOL and survival. (Ch 233)  
- **Pimobendan** — part of standard therapy for CHF due to DCM; multiple placebo-controlled trials showed significantly longer survival. (Ch 233)  
- **Spironolactone** — may reduce morbidity/mortality; in DP with CHF, reduced risk of AF development. Use for persistent signs despite triple therapy (furosemide + ACEI + pimobendan) or as initial standard therapy. (Ch 233)  
- **Rate control in AF**: Target mean HR <125 bpm on Holter; in-hospital ECG target <140–150 bpm. Diltiazem + digoxin combination more efficacious than either alone for rate control. (Ch 233)

**Prognosis — DCM (Clinical Phase)**  
- Median survival time after CHF onset: **~19 weeks** (large retrospective).  
- 1-year survival: **28%**; 2-year survival: **14%**. (Ch 233)  
- 50% of dogs euthanized; 40% died from heart disease.  
- Worst survival: DP and Great Dane. Better survival: Cocker Spaniel. (Ch 233)

**Prognosis — Preclinical DCM**  
- Average time to CHF/sudden death: **~2 years (DP)**, **~4 years (IW)**. (Ch 233)

#### Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy (ARVC) — Boxer Dog

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — separate from DCM) — Boxer ARVC is a distinct entity.

**Definition**: Primary myocardial disease with partial-to-extensive replacement of RV myocardium with fatty/fibrofatty tissue, VA, and risk of syncope/sudden death. (Ch 233)

**Genetics**: Autosomal dominant with incomplete, age-related penetrance. Striatin gene deletion (8-bp) in some American Boxers. Ryanodine receptor (calstabin2 deficiency) also implicated. Homozygotes = more severe disease. (Ch 233)

**ECG / Holter Criteria for ARVC Diagnosis — Boxer**  
- PVCs of RV origin (LBBB morphology, positive deflection in leads II, III, aVF).  
- **>100 PVCs of RV origin per 24 hours** → suspicious for ARVC (in absence of other identifiable cause). Greater frequency + complexity = higher likelihood.  
- **>300 PVC/24h + increased complexity (couplets, triplets, R-on-T)**, or **>1000 PVC/24h** → candidate for AA therapy.  
- Day-to-day variability up to 80%; repeat Holter may be needed. (Ch 233)

**Treatment — ARVC**  
- **Immediate VT causing collapse**: IV lidocaine (Ch 230).  
- **Oral antiarrhythmics**: sotalol, mexiletine, combination of sotalol + mexiletine, or atenolol + mexiletine. (Ch 233)  
- Refractory: amiodarone or other combinations.  
- Therapeutic efficacy: **>80% reduction in VA number and complexity** on Holter within 2–3 weeks.  
- Omega-3 fatty acid supplementation as adjunct to AA therapy.  
- CHF (minority of cases): ACEI + pimobendan + diuretics + AA therapy. (Ch 233)

**Prognosis — ARVC**  
- Prospective study: **31% experienced sudden death**; median survival after identification of >300 PVC/24h = **just over 4 years**. Overall survival not significantly different from control Boxers (10–11 years). (Ch 233)  
- Retrospective (majority syncopal): MST **~1 year**; death risk within 1 year = **5× higher** with syncope or >10,000 PVCs/24h; **20× higher** with >200 runs VT/24h. (Ch 233)  
- English Bulldog ARVC: MST **8.3 months** (case series). (Ch 233)

#### Other Canine Myocardial Diseases

**Myocarditis**  
Etiologies: viral (parvovirus, distemper), protozoal (trypanosomiasis, leishmania, neospora, toxoplasma), bacterial (Bartonella, Borrelia, Leptospira, Ehrlichia), fungal (Blastomyces, Coccidioides), immune-mediated, non-specific. (Ch 233)  
Most common arrhythmias: VA and AV block (especially with trypanosomiasis). Elevated cTnI is common and useful — typically much higher than in cardiomyopathy/valve disease. Prognosis: generally poor long-term. (Ch 233)

**Canine HCM**  
Rare in dogs. Recent multicenter retrospective (68 dogs): 12% fatal; median survival 20 months. Small breed dogs now predominate (Shih Tzu, Chihuahua, terriers). Treatment of DLVOTO: atenolol (beta-blockade). (Ch 233)

**Atrial Myopathy (Persistent Atrial Standstill)**  
English Springer Spaniels and Labradors predisposed. ECG: bradycardia, no P waves, junctional or ventricular escape. Treatment: permanent pacemaker + medical management of CHF. Average survival with pacing: **>2 years**; CHF at time of pacing does not affect survival time. (Ch 233)

### Ch 234 — Feline Myocardial Diseases

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-HCM (feline HCM), ✓ DIS-CARD-RCM (RCM/unclassified CM)  
> Worth enriching: ACVIM 2020 staging complete (A/B1/B2/C/D); LA:Ao thresholds; complete drug table; clopidogrel dose; torsemide dose; pimobendan criteria in cats; telmisartan; rivaroxaban.

#### ACVIM 2020 Classification — Feline Cardiomyopathy Phenotypes

**Five phenotypes** diagnosed by echocardiography (ACVIM Consensus Statement 2020):
1. HCM phenotype — diffuse/regional increased LV wall thickness, non-dilated LV, absence of sufficient alternative cause.
2. RCM phenotype — myocardial form (relatively normal LV dims, LA or biatrial enlargement) or endomyocardial form (prominent endocardial scar bridging IVS and LV free wall).
3. DCM phenotype — LV systolic dysfunction, progressive LV dilation/volume increase, normal or reduced wall thickness.
4. ARVC phenotype — severe RA and RV dilation, often RV systolic dysfunction, RV wall thinning.
5. Nonspecific (NSCM) phenotype — does not fit any of the above 4. (Ch 234)

#### ACVIM 2020 Staging — Feline Cardiomyopathies

| Stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| Stage A | Cats predisposed to cardiomyopathy (at-risk breeds/genetics) without current disease |
| Stage B1 | Cardiomyopathy present, no CHF/ATE; normal atrial size or mild atrial enlargement; no other high-risk features |
| Stage B2 | Cardiomyopathy present, no CHF/ATE; **moderate or severe atrial enlargement** AND/OR other risk factors: gallop sound, arrhythmias, decreased LA function, extreme LVH, LV systolic dysfunction, spontaneous echocontrast, thrombus, regional wall motion abnormalities |
| Stage C | Cardiomyopathy with current or previous CHF/ATE |
| Stage D | Cardiomyopathy with refractory CHF |

(Ch 234)

**LA:Ao Reference Interval**: 0.5–1.2 (reference interval cited repeatedly in Ch 234; LA:Ao ≥2.0 = severe enlargement, associated with shorter survival). End-diastolic LA:Ao ≥2 was significantly associated with shorter time to cardiac death in RCM cats (Ch 234).

**LV wall thickness threshold for HCM diagnosis**: LVFW and/or IVS ≥6 mm at end-diastole (in absence of other sufficient cause). (Ch 234)

**Severe LVH threshold**: wall thickness ≥9.0 mm — independently associated with increased risk of cardiac death. (Ch 234)

#### HCM — Prevalence, Epidemiology

- Most common feline heart disease: estimated 15% prevalence in apparently healthy cats; increases markedly with age: 4.3% (6–12 months), 9.9% (1–3 yr), 18.6% (3–9 yr), 29.4% (≥9 yr). (Ch 234)  
- Maine Coon: MyBPC3-A31P mutation; prevalence of mutation 31–46% depending on region; RR for HCM = 9.9 (heterozygous), 35.5 (homozygous).  
- Ragdoll: MyBPC3-R820W mutation; prevalence 34%, 85% heterozygous.  
- Males overrepresented 70–79%; DSH predominate (65–70%). (Ch 234)

#### HCM — Prognosis

- Highly variable. Overall median survival from diagnosis: dependent on clinical stage.  
- Stage B (subclinical): median survival **>3617 days** (censored — most outlive study period) when LA size normal; **229 days** when LAE present. (Ch 234)  
- Stage C (CHF): median survival **~194–563 days** depending on study.  
- ATE at presentation: median survival **~184 days**. (Ch 234)  
- Syncope: median **654 days**. (Ch 234)  
- Ragdolls: significantly shorter survival than other breeds (median 19 days vs. 1297 days in one study). (Ch 234)  
- Homozygous MyBPC3-R820W Ragdolls: median age at cardiac death 5.7 years; vs >16.7 yr (heterozygous) and >15.2 yr (wild-type). (Ch 234)  
- Negative prognostic indicators: LAE (especially LA:Ao ≥2), severe LVH (≥9 mm), decreased systolic function, spontaneous echo contrast/thrombus, restrictive filling pattern, gallop sounds, arrhythmias. (Ch 234)

#### RCM — Prognosis

- Median survival (RCM myocardial form, excluding cats dying <24h): **667 days** (range 2–3710 days). All-cause mortality MST: **436 days**. (Ch 234)  
- Poor prognosis: LA:Ao ≥2 significantly associated with shorter time to cardiac death.  
- Biventricular failure RCM cats: MST **64 days** (95% CI = 8–120 days). (Ch 234)

#### DCM — Feline

- Rare primary disease since commercial food taurine supplementation. If primary: **taurine supplementation 250–500 mg/cat PO q12h**; clinical improvement 1–2 weeks; echo improvement 3–9 weeks. (Ch 234)  
- MST for feline DCM in retrospective study: **49 days** (very poor prognosis). (Ch 234)  
- Pimobendan improves survival in cats with non-taurine responsive DCM. (Ch 234)

**ACVIM Stage B (Treatment Summary)**  
- If treatment chosen over "watchful waiting": calcium channel blockers or RAAS antagonists now favored over atenolol, except with severe LVOTO or ventricular tachyarrhythmias.  
- Clopidogrel as first-line antithrombotic if moderate-to-severe LAE or other B2 risk factors. (Ch 234)

**ACVIM Stage C (Treatment Summary)**  
- Furosemide first-line (acute: parenteral 1–4 mg/kg; chronic: lowest effective oral dose).  
- Goal: resting/sleeping respiratory rate <30 breaths/minute.  
- If furosemide >6 mg/kg/day → switch to or add torsemide (0.1–0.2 mg/kg PO q24h, uptitrate).  
- Clopidogrel (first-line antithrombotic) with or without aspirin or rivaroxaban in very high-risk cats.  
- Other common additions: diltiazem, ACE inhibitors, pimobendan (if no significant LVOTO).  
- Pimobendan: consider for acute low CO (hypotension, hypothermia, bradycardia) when LVOTO trivial/absent. (Ch 234)

**ACVIM Stage D**  
- Add torsemide if furosemide insufficient.  
- Spironolactone 0.5–3 mg/kg q24h or 0.5–1.5 mg/kg q12h. (Caution: ulcerative facial dermatitis reported at 2 mg/kg PO q12h.)  
- Pimobendan for systolic dysfunction/poor CO in non-OHCM cases. (Ch 234)

### Ch 235 — Pericardial Diseases

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CARD-PERIC (Pericardial Effusion); ✓ DIS-NEO-HSA (HSA — note cardiac HSA is the primary ClinIQ driver for pericardial effusion)  
> Worth enriching: idiopathic pericarditis prognosis; MSTs for HSA, mesothelioma, chemodectoma; pericardiocentesis trigger.  
> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — Idiopathic (benign) Pericarditis as a distinct entity.

#### Epidemiology — Dog

Prevalence: 0.43% of dogs examined at referral hospital (~7% of dogs with cardiac disease signs). (Ch 235)

**Causes in 107 dogs**:
- Hemangiosarcoma (HSA): 33.6%
- Idiopathic pericarditis: 19.6%
- Mesothelioma: 14.0%
- Chemodectoma: 8.4%
- Thyroid gland adenocarcinoma: 5.6%
- Infective pericarditis: 4.7%
- Lymphoma: 2.8%
- Sarcoma: 1.8%
- Other (carcinomatosis, ruptured LA, foreign body, granuloma): <1% each

Neoplasia overall = 71% of cases. (Ch 235)

**HSA**: RA predilection (88% of RA masses were HSA); heart base HSA = 13% of heart base masses.  
Concurrent splenic HSA in ~30% of dogs with cardiac HSA. (Ch 235)  
Male, medium-to-large breed dogs (Golden Retrievers especially) overrepresented. Dogs with cardiac masses: mean age 9.7 yr; without masses: 7.9 yr. (Ch 235)

**Chemodectoma**: English Bulldogs, Boxers, Boston Terriers overrepresented (brachycephalic predisposition — chronic hypoxia-induced hyperplasia). (Ch 235)

**Idiopathic (Hemorrhagic) Pericarditis**  
Unknown cause; postulated viral or immune-mediated. ~50% resolve after single pericardiocentesis; remaining 50% have recurrent effusion requiring subtotal pericardectomy. Constrictive pericarditis is a possible chronic sequela. (Ch 235)

#### Pathophysiology — Cardiac Tamponade

Acute: rapid accumulation → tamponade at volumes as low as **50–150 mL** (20 kg dog).  
Chronic: slow accumulation → pericardium stretches to accommodate several hundred mL without hemodynamic compromise. (Ch 235)  
Mean arterial pressure maintained until intrapericardial pressure ~10 mm Hg in experimental model. (Ch 235)  
Right heart (thinner-walled) bears brunt → diastolic RA and RV collapse → decreased LV filling → decreased CO → cardiogenic shock. (Ch 235)

**ECG**: Electrical alternans in ~28% of cases; sinus tachycardia in ~28%. (Ch 235)  
**Radiography**: Globoid cardiomegaly with crisp margins — sensitivity only 41.9%, specificity 40% for cardiac tamponade in one study. VHS >10.7 sensitivity 77.6%, specificity 47.8%. (Ch 235)  
**Echocardiography**: Essential diagnostic test; mass detection sensitivity 82% (cardiologist), specificity 100%. RA mass sensitivity 82%; heart base mass 74%. (Ch 235)

#### Treatment and Prognosis

**Pericardiocentesis**  
Immediately lifesaving when cardiac tamponade present with hemodynamic compromise. If stable patient, defer until thorough echo performed. Do NOT delay if cardiogenic shock present. (Ch 235)

**Idiopathic Pericarditis**  
- Subtotal pericardectomy: **100% survival rate** over 3-year study — MST never reached.  
- Thoracoscopic pericardial window: disease-free interval **11.6 months**, MST **13.1 months** (inferior to subtotal pericardectomy). (Ch 235)

**Mesothelioma**  
- Subtotal pericardectomy: MST **10.3 months**.  
- Pericardial window: MST **8.6 months** (not significantly different from subtotal pericardectomy). (Ch 235)

**Chemodectoma (Heart Base Masses)**  
- Pericardectomy: MST **730 days** vs. **42 days** without pericardectomy. (Ch 235)  
- Second study (25 dogs): pericardectomy MST **661 days** vs. non-surgical **129 days**. (Ch 235)

**Cardiac HSA**  
- Pericardectomy alone: disappointing MSTs of **2.7–3.8 months** for combined neoplastic causes.  
- Right auricular HSA resection (23 dogs): MST with chemotherapy + surgery **175 days** vs. surgery alone **42 days**. (Ch 235)  
- Doxorubicin without surgery: extension in overall survival **116 days** (with dox) vs. **12 days** (without); only **14% of doxorubicin-treated dogs survived >6 months**. (Ch 235)  
- Alternative: doxorubicin ± other agents without resection: MST **139.5 days** (range 2–302 days). (Ch 235)

### Ch 236 — Systemic Hypertension

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-VASC-HYPERT (Systemic Hypertension)  
> **Critical to enrich**: ACVIM 2018 BP categories are essential — these are the definitive staging criteria. All drug doses confirmed/completed.

#### ACVIM 2018 Consensus Statement — BP Categories and TOD Risk

| Category | SBP (mm Hg) | TOD Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Normotensive** | <140 | Minimal |
| **Prehypertensive** | 140–159 | Low |
| **Hypertensive** | 160–179 | Moderate |
| **Severely hypertensive** | ≥180 | High |

**Treatment trigger**: SBP **>160 mm Hg** repeatedly with unlikely situational SH, or evidence of TOD. Ideal target: SBP **<140 mm Hg**; minimum acceptable target: SBP **≤160 mm Hg**. (Ch 236)

**Screening**: annually from 9 years of age; also for any animal with conditions associated with SH or clinical signs suggesting hypertensive TOD. (Ch 236)

#### Causes — Secondary Hypertension

- **Renal disease** (most common): CKD prevalence in cats with SH = 20–65%; AKI: ~59% cats, 75% dogs with SH.  
- **Hyperadrenocorticism**: 59–86% of HAC dogs; severe SH (SBP >180) in 46%.  
- **Primary hyperaldosteronism** (cats): >90% have SH (SBP >180 mm Hg) — resolves with adrenalectomy.  
- **Pheochromocytoma**: at least 50% of dogs present SH at some point.  
- **Hyperthyroidism** (cats): prevalence 5–25%.  
- **Diabetes mellitus**: 35–46% dogs; SBP tends to remain <160 mm Hg. (Ch 236)

**Prevalence**: 19.5% cats; 0.5–13% dogs. (Ch 236)

#### Target Organ Damage (TOD)

- **Ocular**: most common site of TOD. Retinal detachment, hemorrhage, edema, arteriolar tortuosity, hyphema. Hypertensive retinal lesions in 16% cats >8 years old; 50–100% of cats with SH show ocular lesions. (Ch 236)  
- **Cardiac**: LVH (74–85% of cats with SH); diastolic dysfunction; rarely LA enlargement or CHF from SH alone. (Ch 236)  
- **CNS**: hypertensive encephalopathy — seizures, altered mentation, blindness, vestibular/cerebellar ataxia; predilection for parietal/occipital lobes. Ischemic myelopathy in cats (C1–C5 most common, C2–C3 most frequent). (Ch 236)  
- **Renal**: hypertensive nephrosclerosis — arteriolar hyalinosis, glomerulosclerosis. (Ch 236)

**Hypertensive emergency approach**: Reduce BP 10% in first hour, 15% over next few hours. Sodium nitroprusside: start 1–2 mcg/kg/min IV CRI; max 10 mcg/kg/min. (Ch 236)

### Ch 237 — Heartworm Disease in Dogs and Cats (Dirofilaria immitis)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — **Heartworm Disease (Dirofilariasis) in dogs and cats**.  
> **Important note**: ClinIQ's lungworm page uses Angiostrongylus — this is a completely different parasitic pneumonia. D. immitis heartworm disease is entirely absent from ClinIQ and represents a major clinical gap.

#### AHS Clinical Classification — Dogs

The AHS categorizes clinical signs to guide treatment:
- **Mildly affected**: No clinical signs, OR cough only.
- **Moderately affected**: Cough AND/OR abnormal lung sounds AND exercise intolerance.
- **Severely affected**: Cough, exercise intolerance, abnormal lung sounds, dyspnea, syncope, ascites — medical therapy is key; adulticide options become more limited.
- **Caval syndrome**: Intracardiac worm burden causing hemolysis (anemia, hemoglobinuria) and hemodynamic instability — requires surgical worm extraction, not adulticide. (Ch 237)

#### AHS 2020 Recommended Heartworm Management Protocol (Dogs) — Table 237.1

| Day/Period | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Day 0 | Confirm positive Ag test + MF test. Apply EPA-registered mosquito repellent. **Begin exercise restriction** (stricter with more severe signs). If showing clinical signs: **prednisone 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h (week 1), then 0.5 mg/kg PO q24h (week 2), then 0.5 mg/kg PO q48h (weeks 3–4)**. |
| Day 1 | Administer heartworm preventive. If MF detected: pre-treat with antihistamine + glucocorticoid before preventive; observe ≥8 hours for reaction. |
| Days 1–28 | **Doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q12h × 4 weeks** (reduces lesions; disrupts heartworm transmission; eliminates Wolbachia). |
| Day 30 | Administer heartworm preventive + mosquito repellent. |
| Days 31–60 | Wait period (1 month after doxycycline to allow Wolbachia surface proteins to dissipate). |
| **Day 61** | Administer preventive. **First melarsomine injection: 2.5 mg/kg IM** (epaxial muscle). **Prednisone 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h (week 1), 0.5 mg/kg PO q24h (week 2), 0.5 mg/kg PO q48h (weeks 3–4)**. **Cage restriction; on leash when using yard.** |
| Day 90 | Administer preventive. **Second melarsomine injection: 2.5 mg/kg IM**. **Prednisone 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h → q24h → q48h** (same taper as above). |
| Day 91 | **Third melarsomine injection: 2.5 mg/kg IM**. Continue exercise restriction 6–8 weeks after last melarsomine. |
| Day 120 | Test for MF. If positive: treat with microfilaricide + retest in 4 weeks. Continue year-round prevention. |
| Day 365 | Antigen test 9 months after last melarsomine injection. If still Ag-positive: re-treat with doxycycline + 2 doses melarsomine 24h apart. |

Copyright American Heartworm Society, www.heartwormsociety.org, 2020. (Ch 237)

**Melarsomine details**:  
- Single injection: kills up to 90% of male worms, only 10% of female worms; not effective against microfilariae.  
- 2-phase/3-dose split protocol: **efficacy 98%** worm clearance; fewer complications; requires longer time frame and exercise restriction; higher cost.  
- 2-dose single-phase (2 injections 24h apart): standard alternative but lower efficacy.  
- Adverse effects: local irritation/pain/swelling/nodule at injection site; fever; hypersalivation; rarely paresis/paralysis if inadvertently near spinal cord; worm death → pulmonary embolization (exercise restriction essential). (Ch 237)

**Melarsomine is CONTRAINDICATED in cats** due to high rate of adverse reactions including pulmonary thromboembolism and death. (Ch 237)

**Non-arsenical Protocols (when melarsomine unavailable/not possible)**:  
- Ivermectin (6 mcg/kg/month PO) + doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q12–24h × 1 month: efficacy 73% worm clearance at 10 months; higher with subsequent melarsomine. Macrocyclic lactone alone NOT recommended as sole adulticidal therapy (prolonged vascular damage; risk of caval syndrome). (Ch 237)  
- Moxidectin (2.5 mg/kg topical with imidacloprid 10 mg/kg) + doxycycline → >95% efficacy by 12–18 months in most scenarios. (Ch 237)

**Doxycycline**: 10 mg/kg PO q12h × 4 weeks (or minocycline at same dose if doxycycline unavailable); 5 mg/kg PO q12h acceptable alternative if associated with GI signs. (Ch 237)

**Microfilaricide pre-treatment**: Dexamethasone **0.2 mg/kg IV** + diphenhydramine **2 mg/kg IM** ~1 hour before first preventive in dogs with high MF counts or when using milbemycin (500 mcg/kg — microfilaricidal dose — associated with more rapid kill). (Ch 237)

**Surgical caval worm extraction**: Indicated for caval syndrome (hemolysis + hemodynamic instability). Pre-op: prednisone, diphenhydramine 2 mg/kg IM, fluids, blood products, inotropes as needed. Optional: bronchodilator, heparin **75–150 IU/kg SC q8h** if thromboembolic concern. Extraction via jugular venotomy under fluoroscopic or echocardiographic guidance. (Ch 237)

**Prevention**: Macrocyclic lactone preventatives. Monthly oral ivermectin 6 mcg/kg; topical moxidectin + imidacloprid; milbemycin; selamectin — prevent larval development to infective stage. (Ch 237)

**Treatment in Cats**:  
- No adulticidal drug (melarsomine, thiacetarsamide) recommended — severe adverse reactions.  
- Medical management of clinical signs + surgical extraction when feasible.  
- Cats: feline heartworm associated respiratory disease (HARD); spontaneous resolution in some; palliative prednisolone for respiratory signs. (Ch 237)

### Ch 238 — Arterial Thromboembolic Disease (Feline ATE)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — **Feline Arterial Thromboembolism (saddle thrombus)** is a major clinical entity absent from ClinIQ.

#### Epidemiology

Prevalence: 0.26% cats in primary care; 0.6% at referral centers. Majority associated with heart disease (HCM phenotype most common). Noncardiac causes in up to 16% (protein-losing nephropathy, neoplasia [most commonly pulmonary], hyperthyroidism, protein-losing enteropathy). (Ch 238)

#### Clinical Presentation

**"Five Ps"** hallmark: **Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresis/Paralysis, Poikilothermia** (cool limbs). (Ch 238)  
- Bilateral pelvic limb ischemia (saddle thrombus at aortic bifurcation): **71–78%** of cases.  
- Thoracic limb involvement: **10–14%** (unilateral when it occurs). (Ch 238)

**Prognostic indicator at presentation**: Rectal temperature <37°C (<98.6°F) → <50% survival to discharge. (Ch 238)

**Box 238.1 — Prognostic Indicators**:  
- Better prognosis: partial limb motion; only one limb affected; early antiplatelet treatment.  
- Worse prognosis: hypothermia at presentation (<37°C/98.6°F); CHF at diagnosis; bradycardia. (Ch 238)

#### Treatment — Acute ATE (First 6 Hours)

**Analgesia (must be prioritized)**:  
- **Methadone 0.3 mg/kg IM or IV q4h** (IV preferred).  
- Ketamine or fentanyl IV CRI for more severe pain.  
- **Gabapentin 50–100 mg/cat** — adjunct for neuropathic pain and anxiolysis. (Ch 238)

**Empirical furosemide**: **1–2 mg/kg IM or IV** if heart failure suspected (IV preferred for rapid onset). (Ch 238)

**Antithrombotic Treatment — Acute ATE**:  
- **Clopidogrel**: first-line; administer as soon as oral medication tolerated. **18.75 mg/cat PO q24h**. Lifelong in most cases. (Ch 238)  
- **Low-molecular weight heparin (dalteparin)**: used frequently, no clinical trial evidence for survival improvement. Retrospective: 52 cats with dalteparin, max q12h frequency (potentially underdosed based on PK). (Ch 238)  
- **Rivaroxaban**: **1.25 mg/cat PO q12h** (PK study in healthy cats); add to clopidogrel in recurrent ATE cases. (Ch 238)  
- **Aspirin**: consensus advises against in cats with ATE (poor safety profile, inconsistent results); some cardiologists combine with clopidogrel without adverse events.  
- **Warfarin**: NOT recommended — severe adverse events, high-intensity monitoring. (Ch 238)  
- If combination therapy: clopidogrel + low-molecular weight heparin = first choice. (Ch 238)

**Thrombolytics (tPA, streptokinase)**:  
- No better outcome on population basis vs. standard antiplatelet therapy; tPA may help those presenting early (<6 hours); complications possible (ischemia-reperfusion injury). Not routinely recommended. (Ch 238)

#### Treatment — Subacute

**Buprenorphine**: **0.02 mg/kg q6h** after first 24 hours — likely adequate analgesia once methadone discontinued. (Ch 238)  
**Mirtazapine**: **2 mg/cat q24–48h** as appetite stimulant (to avoid IV fluid overload risk). (Ch 238)

#### Longer-Term Prognosis

- Historical (aspirin ± heparins): recurrence rate **25–47%**; MST **51–117 days**. (Ch 238)  
- With clopidogrel monotherapy: recurrence **~49%** (similar); but MST to cardiac death or recurrence: **~346 days**. (Ch 238)  
- Earlier referral center data: MST **~12 months** (almost 1 year). (Ch 238)

#### Prevention of ATE

- Best predictor of ATE: previous ATE.  
- Echo: reduced LAFS% (left atrial fractional shortening) = strongest predictor of ATE risk.  
- Clopidogrel **18.75 mg/cat PO q24h** — first-line for both primary and secondary ATE prevention (stage B2 cats especially). (Ch 238)  
- In cats with very poor LA function (LAFS% <15%): consider adding **pimobendan 1.25 mg/cat PO q12h** to improve LA function (off-label; no clinically significant LVOTO). (Ch 238)

### Ch 239 — Venous and Lymphatic Disorders

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — No coverage of venous or lymphatic disorders currently in ClinIQ.  
> Candidate diseases: (1) Lymphedema (primary and secondary), (2) Venous thrombosis/thrombophlebitis, (3) Pulmonary thromboembolism (cross-reference ch. 219), (4) Lymphangiosarcoma, (5) Chylothorax/lymphangiectasia (see also ch. 220/262), (6) Cranial vena cava syndrome.

#### Venous Diseases — Summary

**Venous thrombosis**: Low-flow conditions, fibrin + erythrocytes. Virchow's triad (stasis, endothelial disruption, hypercoagulable state). Indwelling catheters, pacemaker leads common causes. Associated with: nephrotic syndrome, HAC, IMHA, thrombocytosis, cardiac disease, sepsis, DIC, heartworm, neoplasia. AT deficiency = important co-factor. (Ch 239)

**Caval syndrome** (cranial vena cava): edema of neck, head, forelimbs, ventral cranial thorax; pleural effusion common. Causes: mediastinal masses, indwelling central venous catheters, transvenous pacemaker leads. (Ch 239)

**Therapy for venous obstruction**: If acute thrombus (<1 week, optimally <48h): tPA systemically or via selective catheterization. Venoplasty/venous stenting if thrombolysis fails. (Ch 239)

**Venous varicosis**: Tortuous, dilated veins. Rare in dogs and cats. Cutaneous phlebectasia: benign lesion in dogs with spontaneous or iatrogenic hyperadrenocorticism. (Ch 239)

**Phlebitis**: Local inflammation → venous intimal lesion → thrombophlebitis. Three forms: chemical (irritating drugs), physical (catheter trauma), microbial (infected fluids/catheter tip). Complications: sepsis, thromboembolic pneumonia, endocarditis. (Ch 239)

**Portal venous thrombosis**: Rare. Associated with hepatic disease or prothrombotic state (PLN, IMHA, HAC, splenic torsion). Signs: ascites, abdominal pain, acquired portosystemic shunting, hypovolemic shock. (Ch 239)

**Pulmonary thromboembolism**: Covered in ch. 219. In cats, 47% with necropsy-confirmed PTE had multiple predisposing disorders. Massive in-situ pulmonary thrombi reported in Cavalier King Charles Spaniels. (Ch 239)

**Venous malformations**: Persistence of the left cranial vena cava (PLCVC) — most common. Enters caudoventral right atrium at coronary sinus. Generally no clinical problem; case report of megaesophagus from incomplete PLCVC. (Ch 239)

#### Lymphatic Diseases

**Primary lymphedema**: Developmental abnormality. Hindlimbs of young dogs most common; may be transient (neonatal) or permanent. Autosomal dominant inheritance with variable expression in familial cases. Breeds: Bulldogs, Poodles, Old English Sheepdogs, Labrador Retrievers. Irish Wolfhound may be overrepresented in idiopathic lymphedema. (Ch 239)

**Secondary lymphedema**: More common than primary; due to neoplasia, surgery, trauma, parasites, radiation, infection. (Ch 239)

**Therapy for lymphedema**:  
- Treat underlying cause.  
- Antimicrobial therapy for infections.  
- Long-term heavy bandage application + physical therapy (comparable to compression stockings).  
- Topical skin care; intermittent antibiotics for secondary cellulitis.  
- Benzopyrones (e.g., rutin) — advocated but proof of efficacy lacking.  
- **Long-term diuretics contraindicated** (concentrate interstitial proteins → worsen tissue injury). (Ch 239)  
- Surgical options: lymphangioplasty, bridging procedures, shunts, omental transposition; subcutaneous tissue excision (staged). (Ch 239)

**Lymphangioma**: Benign tumor of small-caliber lymphatic vessels; large fluctuant masses in subcutaneous/fascial/mediastinal/hepatic/retroperitoneal spaces. Prognosis can be good with surgical excision/marsupialization/radiation; high recurrence risk. (Ch 239)

**Lymphangiosarcoma**: Rare malignant tumor. Medium-to-large breeds; affects young and old. Metastasis in one-third to half at diagnosis. Clinical signs: pitting edema of extremities, inguinal region, axilla, head/neck. Chylous effusions associated. Diagnosis by biopsy (IHC: PROX1 stain). Prognosis: guarded to poor. Incomplete surgery MST ~513 days; chemotherapy alone MST ~182 days; combination therapy MST ~574 days. (Ch 239)

**Lymphangitis**: Secondary to skin/mucosal/subcutaneous infections; bacterial/fungal. Treat with warm compresses, systemic antibiotics; culture/sensitivity for refractory cases. (Ch 239)
