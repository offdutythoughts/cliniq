### Ch 240 — Neurophysiology

**Key clinical physiology**

- Resting membrane potential: –65 mV (neuron); –65 to –90 mV (skeletal muscle). (Ch 240)
- Action potential: (a) resting → (b) depolarization (Na⁺ influx to +35–40 mV) → (c) repolarization (Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ exits). (Ch 240)
- Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase: 3 Na⁺ out per 2 K⁺ in → contributes to negative resting potential. (Ch 240)
- Saltatory conduction: depolarization "jumps" between nodes of Ranvier on myelinated axons → faster conduction. Myelin = oligodendrocytes (CNS), Schwann cells (PNS). (Ch 240)

**Synaptic transmission**
- Inhibitory neurotransmitters: GABA, glycine → Cl⁻ influx + K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarization (IPSP). (Ch 240)
- Excitatory: glutamate, acetylcholine → Na⁺ influx → EPSP. (Ch 240)
- Benzodiazepines and barbiturates bind GABA receptors → Cl⁻ influx → hyperpolarization → halt seizure propagation. (Ch 240)

**UMN vs LMN — foundational distinction**
- LMN cell bodies in ventral horn of spinal cord. Disease → decreased/absent tone, paresis/paralysis, decreased/absent reflexes. (Ch 240)
- UMN (pyramidal [corticospinal] + extrapyramidal [rubrospinal, vestibulospinal, reticulospinal]) normally inhibit LMNs. Disease → LMNs disinhibited → normal to increased tone, normal to increased reflexes + paresis/paralysis. (Ch 240)

### Ch 241 — Neurologic Examination and Neuroanatomic Diagnosis

**Five components of the neurologic examination**
1. Sensorium and behavior
2. Posture and gait
3. Postural reactions
4. Muscle mass, tone, spinal reflexes, cutaneous sensation, nociception
5. Cranial nerves (Ch 241)

**Modified Frankel Score (spinal cord lesion grading)**
- Grade 0: tetraplegia/paraplegia with no deep nociception
- Grade 1: tetraplegia/paraplegia with no superficial nociception
- Grade 2: tetraplegia/paraplegia with nociception
- Grade 3: nonambulatory tetraparesis or paraparesis
- Grade 4: ambulatory tetraparesis or paraparesis + GP ataxia
- Grade 5: spinal hyperesthesia only or no dysfunction (Ch 241)

**Three types of ataxia**
1. General proprioceptive (GP) ataxia: crossing limbs, scuffing, delayed swing phase; accompanies UMN paresis. (Ch 241)
2. Vestibular ataxia: head tilt, lean/fall to one side, abnormal nystagmus; proprioception preserved with peripheral lesion, UMN deficits with central lesion. (Ch 241)
3. Cerebellar ataxia: hypermetric "bursty" gait, strength preserved; head/neck intention tremor. (Ch 241)

**Decerebrate vs decerebellate posture**
- Decerebrate rigidity: opisthotonus + rigid extension of neck + all four limbs → midbrain or rostral cerebellar lesions. (Ch 241)
- Decerebellate rigidity: opisthotonus + extensor rigidity of limbs but hips flexed → severe cerebellar lesion. (Ch 241)

**Localization Framework — Five neuroanatomic regions**

**1. Prosencephalon (cerebrum/thalamus)**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sensorium/behavior | Seizures, abnormal behavior, propulsive activity, depression to coma |
| Posture/gait | Head turn; normal gait (unless peracute); propulsive circling ipsilateral; head pressing |
| Postural reactions | Contralateral deficits |
| Muscle mass/tone | Normal |
| Spinal reflexes | Normal |
| Cutaneous sensation | Contralateral (often facial/nasal) hypalgesia |
| Cranial nerves | Contralateral menace deficit ± abnormal PLR (optic chiasm/tract) |
| Other | Thirst, appetite, temperature dysregulation |

Key point: Animals with prosencephalic lesions typically have **normal gait** because rubrospinal and reticulospinal tracts are more caudal (midbrain, pons, medulla). Transient gait deficit may occur with peracute lesions (e.g., CVA). (Ch 241)

**2. Brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla)**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sensorium/behavior | Depression—coma |
| Posture/gait | UMN tetraparesis + GP deficits; vestibular ataxia (pontine/medullary lesions); opisthotonus (midbrain) |
| Postural reactions | Ipsilateral (pons/medulla); contralateral (rostral midbrain) |
| Cranial nerves | CN III–XII dysfunction (anisocoria, dropped jaw [V bilateral], facial hypalgesia [V], head tilt [VIII], nystagmus [VIII], facial paresis/paralysis [VII], dysphagia [IX, X], tongue paresis [XII]) |
| Other | Respiratory/cardiac abnormalities |

(Ch 241)

**3. Cerebellum**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sensorium/behavior | Unaffected |
| Posture/gait | Intention tremor head/neck/eyes; opisthotonus + extensor rigidity all limbs with hips flexed (severe, rostral); hypermetric/spastic gait with strength preserved; loss of balance |
| Postural reactions | Delayed then exaggerated (diffuse); ipsilateral limb (unilateral lesion) |
| Muscle mass/tone | May be exaggerated |
| Spinal reflexes | Normal to exaggerated |
| Cranial nerves | Menace deficit (ipsilateral); anisocoria (rare) |

Paradoxical head tilt: contralateral to lesion (flocculonodular lobe). Rebound phenomenon on head extension. (Ch 241)

**4. Spinal cord — Four functional regions**

| Lesion location | Thoracic limbs | Pelvic limbs | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1–C5 | UMN paresis; GP deficits; normal to ↑ withdrawal reflexes/tone* | UMN paresis; GP deficits; normal to ↑ reflexes/tone | Spastic tetraplegia; respiratory risk; UMN Horner's |
| C6–T2 | LMN paresis (short, choppy gait); ↓ withdrawal reflexes/tone | UMN paresis; GP deficits; long-strided; normal to ↑ reflexes/tone | Flaccid LMN TL paralysis; spastic PL paraplegia; respiratory risk; Horner's (T1–T3) |
| T3–L3 | Normal | UMN paresis; GP deficits; long-strided; normal to ↑ reflexes/tone | Spastic paraplegia; Schiff-Sherrington TL; possible paradoxical LMN PL (spinal shock) |
| L4–S3 | Normal | LMN paresis (short, choppy); GP deficits (white matter); ↓ patellar (L4–L6) or withdrawal reflexes (L6–S1) | Flaccid paraplegia PL + flaccid anus/tail + ↓ perineal sensation |

*C1–C5 lesions may show reduced thoracic limb withdrawal reflexes (Ch 241)

**Schiff-Sherrington syndrome**: Peracute T3–L3 lesion → thoracic limb spasticity due to loss of ascending inhibitory interneurons (border cells, L1–L7 dorsolateral ventral gray). Postural reactions remain normal in thoracic limbs (distinguishes from C1–C5 lesion). (Ch 241)

**5. LMN/Neuromuscular system**

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sensorium | Normal |
| Gait | Flaccid paresis ± exercise exacerbation |
| Postural reactions | Delayed/absent in affected limbs; normal with preserved voluntary motor |
| Muscle mass/tone | Decreased tone; neurogenic atrophy; pseudohypertrophy in myopathies |
| Spinal reflexes | Hyporeflexia through areflexia |
| Cutaneous sensation | Normal in pure LMN; hypalgesia if polyneuropathy with sensory component |

(Ch 241)

**Spinal reflexes — key reflexes**
- Patellar reflex: femoral nerve, L4–L7. Most reliable tendon reflex. Graded 0 (absent) to +4 (clonic). (Ch 241)
- Withdrawal-flexor TL: C6–T2. (Ch 241)
- Withdrawal-flexor PL: sciatic nerve, L6–S1. (Ch 241)
- Biceps reflex: musculocutaneous nerve, C6–C8. (Ch 241)
- Triceps reflex: radial nerve, C7–T2. (Ch 241)
- Perineal reflex: pudendal nerve, sacral + caudal segments. (Ch 241)
- Cutaneous trunci reflex: cutaneous sensory → ipsilateral spinal cord → relay cranially to C8 → both lateral thoracic nerves. Preserved 1–2 vertebral bodies caudal to transverse lesion. (Ch 241)
- Crossed extensor reflex: extension of contralateral limb during withdrawal reflex → indicative of UMN disease. (Ch 241)

**PLR localization table (right-sided lesion example)**

| Lesion | Right pupil resting size | Right PLR | Right menace |
|---|---|---|---|
| Right CN II | Normal to partial dilation | Light in right → neither constricts | Negative |
| Right CN III | Complete dilation | Light in right → only left constricts | Positive |
| Right retrobulbar | Complete dilation | Light in right → neither constricts | Negative |
| Right optic tract (thalamus) | Normal | Light in right → both constrict | Mild deficit |
| Right occipital cortex | Normal | Light in right → both constrict | Mild deficit |

(Ch 241)

**Cranial nerve dysfunction table**

| CN | Function | Clinical signs of dysfunction |
|---|---|---|
| I (Olfactory) | Smell | Anosmia |
| II (Optic) | Vision, PLR | Blindness, dilated/unresponsive pupils |
| III (Oculomotor) | Extraocular muscles, parasympathetic | Ventrolateral strabismus, ptosis, dilated pupils, ↓ PLR |
| IV (Trochlear) | Dorsal oblique | Dorsomedial strabismus (cat), lateral retinal vein deviation (dog) |
| V (Trigeminal) | Masticatory motor (mandibular), facial sensation | Masticatory muscle atrophy, dropped jaw (bilateral), ↓ facial/nasal sensation |
| VI (Abducent) | Lateral rectus, retractor bulbi | Medial strabismus |
| VII (Facial) | Facial expression, lacrimal, taste | Inability to close eyelid/move ear/retract lip, nasal philtrum deviation, dry eye |
| VIII (Vestibulocochlear) | Balance, hearing | Head tilt, vestibular ataxia, nystagmus, positional strabismus, deafness |
| IX (Glossopharyngeal) | Pharyngeal sensory/motor | ↓ gag reflex, dysphagia |
| X (Vagus) | Pharynx, larynx, viscera | ↓ gag reflex, dysphagia, laryngeal paralysis, megaesophagus |
| XI (Accessory) | Trapezius | Trapezius atrophy |
| XII (Hypoglossal) | Tongue | Atrophy/asymmetry tongue, inability to retract (bilateral) |

(Ch 241)

**Nociception assessment**: Hemostat pressure to digit → vocalization, head turn, or pupil dilation = positive (thalamic/cerebral recognition). Must distinguish from withdrawal reflex (spinal arc only). (Ch 241)

### Ch 242 — Inflammatory, Infectious, and Other Multifocal Brain Diseases (MUO/GME/NME)

> ClinIQ: ✓ MUO (DIS-NEU-MUE), GME (DIS-GME), NME/NLE (DIS-NEU-NME), Infectious Meningoencephalitis (DIS-NEU-MENINGITIS)

#### CSF analysis in inflammatory brain diseases (summary)

| Disease | Appearance | WBC/µL | Cell type | Protein (mg/dL) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bacterial | Clear to turbid | Mild to marked ↑ | Neutrophilic; monocytic | Increased, usually >100 |
| Viral | Clear | Normal to mild–marked ↑ | Lymphocytic; mononuclear; mixed; neutrophils (FIP) | Variable, usually <100; FIP >100 |
| Fungal | Clear to turbid | Mild to marked ↑ | Neutrophilic; mononuclear; eosinophils (Crypto); capsid/yeast sometimes | Increased variable |
| Protozoal | Clear | Mild to marked ↑ | Mononuclear mixed; eosinophils sometimes; tachyzoites sometimes | Variable increased |
| Rickettsial | Clear | Mild to moderate ↑ | Lymphocytic; mixed; morulae sometimes | Variable, usually <100 |
| Algal | Clear to xanthochromic | Marked ↑ | Eosinophilic; mixed; organisms sometimes | Increased, usually >100 |
| MUO | Clear to turbid | Mild to marked ↑ | Mononuclear; lymphocytic; mixed with neutrophils | Increased, variable |
| SRMA | Clear to xanthochromic | Moderate to marked ↑ | Neutrophilic; monocytic (chronic) | Increased, usually >100 |
| Eosinophilic meningoencephalitis | Clear | Moderate to marked ↑ | >50–90% eosinophilic | Increased to variable |

(Ch 242)

#### Bacterial meningoencephalitis

**Organisms**: Staphylococcus spp., Streptococcus, Klebsiella, Pasteurella spp., Actinomyces spp., Nocardia spp., Escherichia coli, anaerobes (Peptostreptococcus, Fusobacterium, Bacteroides). Gram-negative most common. (Ch 242)

**Treatment**: Antimicrobials crossing BBB. Best evidence for enrofloxacin, benzylpenicillin, and ampicillin in dogs. Also: broad-spectrum beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim-sulfadiazine, metronidazole, clindamycin. High mortality. (Ch 242)

#### Fungal meningoencephalitis

**Key organism**: Cryptococcus neoformans — most common fungal meningoencephalitis in dogs and cats. Route: inhalation → cribriform plate → olfactory bulb OR hematogenous. (Ch 242)

**Treatment**: Fluconazole 5 mg/kg PO q12h (preferred — BBB penetration; itraconazole does not penetrate). Voriconazole toxic to cats. Combination liposomal/lipid complex amphotericin B has better CNS penetration, less nephrotoxicity. Duration: months. Short-term corticosteroids (prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day × 1–2 weeks) significantly increases survival. (Ch 242)

**Prognosis**: Cryptococcosis remissions >1 year in 32% of 47 treated animals. Coccidioidomycosis: 82% alive >1 year with fluconazole. (Ch 242)

#### Protozoal (Neospora/Toxoplasmosis)

**Treatment**: Clindamycin 12.5 mg/kg PO q12h; trimethoprim/sulfonamide 15 mg/kg PO q12h with pyrimethamine 1 mg/kg PO q24h alone or in combination. Protocol: ≥4 weeks. (Ch 242)

#### Meningoencephalomyelitis of Unknown Origin (MUO)

**Definition**: Clinically diagnosed (MRI + CSF) non-infectious CNS inflammatory disease — encompasses GME, NME, NLE. Excludes SRMA (no overt CNS involvement). (Ch 242)

**GME**: Up to 25% of all canine inflammatory CNS disease. Multifocal (most common), focal, or ocular forms. Small, middle-aged, especially female dogs. (Ch 242)

**NME (Pug dog encephalitis)**: Breeds: Pug, Maltese, Chihuahua, Pekingese, West Highland White Terrier, Papillon, Shih Tzu, Brussels Griffon, Coton De Tulear, others. Mean age of onset: 2.5 years. Asymmetric cortical gray/white matter necrosis. (Ch 242)

**NLE**: Yorkshire Terrier, French Bulldog. White matter predilection; brainstem involved with cavitation. (Ch 242)

**Presumptive diagnosis criteria (Granger meta-analysis)**: Multifocal neurologic signs + CSF mononuclear pleocytosis + T2 hyperintense lesions on MRI. (Ch 242)

**CSF in GME**: Mild to moderate lymphocytic or mixed pleocytosis; some dogs have neutrophilic predominance; protein variably elevated. (Ch 242)

**CSF in NME (14 Pugs)**: Pleocytosis mean 120 cells/µL (lymphocytic 66%, mononuclear 17%, mixed 17%); mean protein 88.4 mg/dL. (Ch 242)

**IgG index**: (CSF IgG/serum IgG) × (serum albumin/CSF albumin); values ≥0.272 suggest increased intrathecal IgG production. (Ch 242)

**MRI — GME**: Multifocal/diffuse T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity irregular margins anywhere in CNS; variable enhancement. (Ch 242)
**MRI — NME**: Asymmetric, multifocal cortical gray/white matter; loss of gray/white demarcation; perilesional edema; T1 iso-to hypointense; T2 and FLAIR hyperintense. (Ch 242)
**MRI — NLE**: Subcortical white matter + brainstem; T1 hypointense; T2 and FLAIR hyperintense; cavitation + mild to absent enhancement → highly suggestive. (Ch 242)

**Treatment — MUO**

First-line: Corticosteroids (prednisone/prednisolone).
- Initial (until infectious diseases ruled out): anti-inflammatory 0.25–0.5 mg/kg PO daily.
- Then increase to immunosuppressive 2–4 mg/kg PO daily × 2–4 weeks.
- Taper every 4 weeks when clinical signs stabilize/improve.
- Eventually reduce to low-dose every other day.
(Ch 242)

**Cytosine arabinoside (cytarabine) protocol** — standard combined with prednisone:
- **SC bolus protocol**: 50 mg/m² SC q12h for 2 consecutive days; cycle repeated every 3–4 weeks for 3 cycles → interval extended by 1 week for 3 cycles at new interval → gradually extend to every 6 weeks and beyond. (Ch 242)
- **IV CRI protocol**: 200 mg/m² over 8 hours (or 24 hours in severe cases). Higher doses up to 600 mg/m² in severe cases. (Ch 242)
- **Combined protocol**: Initial IV CRI 200 mg/m² over 24 h → then 2-day SC 50 mg/m² q12h thereafter. (Ch 242)
- **SC alternative**: 50 mg/m² q2h for 4 doses on same day (pharmacokinetic study confirmed similar exposure). (Ch 242)

**Other second-line immunosuppressives**: Leflunomide, procarbazine, lomustine, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, COP (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisone), cyclosporine. Radiation therapy for focal GME lesions. (Ch 242)

**Monitoring**: Repeat CBCs and serum biochemistry regularly (risk: myelosuppression, hepatotoxicosis, GI disturbance). Combination CSF + MRI at 3-month recheck provides greater sensitivity for relapse prediction. (Ch 242)

**Prognosis — MUO**
- 15–30% of dogs with MUO die shortly after diagnosis.
- Negative prognostic indicators: multiple MRI lesions, mass effect, herniation, sulci effacement; decreased mentation at presentation; seizures; increased neutrophil % in CSF. Death within 7 days: associated with above factors.
- Focal forebrain lesions → significantly longer survival than multifocal or brainstem lesions.
- Seizures at presentation → significantly reduced survival.
- Median survival with corticosteroids alone: 28–602 days (wide range; one study 602 days).
- Median survival with corticosteroids + second-line agent: 240–590 days.
- Pugs with NE + anticonvulsant alone: similar survival to other MUO subsets.
- Spinal MUO: ~50% die or are euthanized regardless of therapy.
(Ch 242)

**Postencephalitic epilepsy** risk increased in dogs with MUO + seizures at early stage. (Ch 242)

### Ch 243 — Vascular Brain Diseases (Stroke)

> ClinIQ: ✓ CVA/Stroke (DIS-NEU-CVA)

**Definition**: CVA = rapidly developing focal (or global) brain dysfunction lasting >24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular origin. TIA: brief (<24 h), complete return of function. (Ch 243)

**Epidemiology**: Median age dogs 8 years, cats 12 years. Most strokes in dogs and cats are ischemic (emboli or local thrombosis). (Ch 243)

**Clinical signs by location**

*Forebrain CVA*: Abnormal mental status, seizures, contralateral postural reaction deficits, transient contralateral motor dysfunction, contralateral nasal hypalgesia + menace deficit, ipsilateral circling. Some develop chronic epilepsy. (Ch 243)

*Thalamic/midbrain CVA*: Contralateral or ipsilateral postural reaction deficits, contralateral menace deficit, ipsilateral head tilt/turn, ipsilateral circling, vestibular ataxia, pathological nystagmus, positional ventrolateral strabismus, anisocoria. (Ch 243)

*Cerebellar CVA*: Peracute head tilt, vestibular or cerebellar ataxia, intermittent opisthotonos, pathological nystagmus, ipsilateral menace deficits. (Ch 243)
- **Rostral cerebellar**: gait abnormalities (cerebellar ataxia ± hypermetria or acute onset non-ambulatory with decerebellate rigidity), head tilt, pathological nystagmus, ↓ menace, hopping deficits. **Short-term prognosis excellent; rapid improvement within first week.** (Ch 243)
- **Caudal cerebellar**: intermittent intention tremors, vestibular ataxia, vertical pathological nystagmus, paradoxical vestibular syndrome. (Ch 243)

**Most commonly affected artery in dogs**: Rostral cerebellar artery, followed by perforating arteries of caudal thalamus + rostral brainstem, cerebral striate arteries, middle cerebral artery, rostral cerebral artery. (Ch 243)

**Predisposing conditions**
- Dogs: comorbidity detected in 55%; chronic kidney disease (24%), hyperadrenocorticism (18%). (Ch 243)
- Cats: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, neoplasia, hyperthyroidism. (Ch 243)
- Predisposed breeds: Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, Greyhound. (Ch 243)
- Other causes: hypercoagulable states (protein-losing nephropathy, enteropathy, hyperadrenocorticism, IMHA, polycythemia vera), bacteremia, endocarditis, vasculitis, leishmaniasis, Cuterebra migration (cats), FCE, hypothyroidism/hyperlipidemia (atherosclerosis). (Ch 243)

**Diagnosis**
- CT: normal in early ischemia; hyperattenuating = acute hemorrhage. (Ch 243)
- MRI conventional: well-demarcated wedge-shaped lesion, primarily gray matter, T2 hyperintense, contrast enhancement variable. (Ch 243)
- DWI: best for hyperacute stroke — cytotoxic edema = diffusion impairment (hyperintense DWI, hypointense ADC). (Ch 243)
- SWI/GRE (T2*): hemorrhage = hypointense (signal void); hemosiderin paramagnetic. (Ch 243)
- High-dose corticosteroids are **contraindicated** for acute ischemic brain injury. (Ch 243)
- D-dimer within normal range does NOT rule out ischemic stroke in dogs. (Ch 243)

**Treatment**
- Head position: 30° elevation.
- Fluids: normo- to slightly hypervolemic; hypertonic saline and/or synthetic colloids initially.
- Mannitol: bolus over 15 minutes (NOT infusion) to obtain plasma-expanding effect; effect on brain edema begins ~15–30 minutes, lasts 2–8 hours. No clinical evidence it is contraindicated with intracranial hemorrhage. (Ch 243)
- Oxygen supplementation. (Ch 243)

**Prognosis**: Short-term prognosis good if not severely affected. Long-term prognosis in dogs good if no underlying disease found. Long-term prognosis in cats currently unknown. (Ch 243)

**Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs)**: Small punctate signal voids ≤5 mm on GRE/SWI. More common in older, smaller dogs. Associated with hypertension, DM, hypothyroidism, hyperadrenocorticism. Often incidental; associated with vestibular syndrome presentation + good prognosis. (Ch 243)

**Hypertensive encephalopathy**: Associated with acute (>30 mmHg from resting) or sustained (>180 mmHg systolic) elevation. Signs: forebrain signs (seizures, altered mentation, decreased consciousness, blindness) + caudal cranial fossa signs (vestibular/cerebellar ataxia, pathological nystagmus). MRI: bilateral symmetric white matter T2 hyperintensity (vasogenic edema). Signs may be **reversible** with BP normalization. (Ch 243)

### Ch 244 — Developmental, Degenerative and Metabolic Brain Diseases & Sleep Disorders

> ClinIQ: ✓ Brain Malformations (DIS-NEU-BRAINMAL), Cerebellar Hypoplasia (DIS-NEU-CEREHYPO), Cerebellar Abiotrophy (DIS-NEU-ABIOTROPHY), Thiamine Deficiency (DIS-NEU-THIAMINE)
> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidates): Hydrocephalus, Chiari-like Malformation/Syringomyelia, Lysosomal Storage Diseases, Narcolepsy, REM Sleep Behavior Disorder

**Neural tube defects**: Cranium bifidum, encephalocele, meningocele, craniofacial malformation. Sporadic; Burmese cats predisposed (mesodermal defects). (Ch 244)

**Disorders of forebrain induction**: Holoprosencephaly, arhinencephaly, corpus callosum agenesis (hallmark: hypodipsia/adipsia → hypernatremia episodes). (Ch 244)

**Disorders of neuronal migration — breed associations**
- Lissencephaly: Shih Tzu, Lhasa Apso, Pekingese, Australian Kelpie, Wire-haired Fox Terrier, Irish Setter, mixed breeds. (Ch 244)
- Polymicrogyria: Standard Poodle — occipital lobes; cortical blindness. (Ch 244)
- Cerebellar hypoplasia: Cats — feline panleukopenia virus in utero/neonatal. (Ch 244)
- Dandy-Walker syndrome: Cerebellar vermal agenesis + dilation of 4th ventricle + hydrocephalus; Eurasier Dog (VLDR). (Ch 244)

**Chiari-like Malformation (CM) and Syringohydromyelia (SM)**

Breeds: CKCS, Brussels Griffon. Mechanism: mismatch between caudal cranial fossa volume and contained brain → cerebellar herniation, medullary kinking, hydrocephalus, obstructive CSF flow. (Ch 244)

*Clinical signs*: Neuropathic pain (excessive scratching ear/neck/shoulder with hindpaw, vocalization, facial rubbing, postural pain on lifting/rising/jumping), thoracic/pelvic limb GP ataxia, postural reaction deficits. Size and asymmetry of syrinx = strongest predictors of pain. Up to 70% CKCS and 24% Brussels Griffons with CM/SM on MRI show no signs. (Ch 244)

*Treatment*: Gabapentin for neuropathic pain. **Pregabalin 13–19 mg/kg PO q12h** — small randomized placebo-controlled trial showed significant reduction of neuropathic pain in CKCSs with SM. Drugs to reduce CSF production. Surgery to restore craniospinal compliance; recurrence common. (Ch 244)

**Hydrocephalus**

*Definition*: Active distension of ventricular system related to inadequate passage of CSF from production to absorption. Ventriculomegaly = incidental imaging finding without clinical signs. (Ch 244)

*Threshold for clinical hydrocephalus*: Ventricle/brain index >0.6 (MRI) differentiates internal hydrocephalus from ventriculomegaly. (Ch 244)

*Clinical signs*: Behavioral/mentation changes, ataxia, visual deficits, seizures; dome-shaped calvaria, fontanelle; ventrolateral strabismus (orbital malformation); lateralized signs with intraventricular foramen obstruction. 30% of dogs >2 years at diagnosis. (Ch 244)

*Medical treatment*: Furosemide, acetazolamide, or omeprazole ± corticosteroids to decrease CSF production. Mannitol for acute decompensation (transient benefit). (Ch 244)

*Surgical treatment*: Ventriculoperitoneal shunt — 72% improvement within median 4 months (retrospective study 30 dogs, 6 cats). Complications first 6 months: shunt obstruction, migration, infection, mechanical failure, overshunting. (Ch 244)

**Degenerative brain diseases — breed-specific summary (key selected)**

| Disease | Breeds | Clinical signs |
|---|---|---|
| Hypomyelinogenesis | Dalmatian, Springer Spaniel (PLP1, X-linked = "shaking pup"), Samoyed, Chow Chow, Weimaraner | Bouncing gait, pendular nystagmus, tremor, cerebellar ataxia |
| Leukodystrophies | Various | Ataxia, slow/rapid progressive para-to tetraparesis, ± tremors, spasticity → recumbency |
| Myelinolysis | Afghan Hound, Miniature Poodle, Dutch Kooiker | Rapidly progressive para-to tetraparesis, ataxia, death from respiratory failure |
| Cerebellar degeneration (multiple forms) | Many breeds | Cerebellar ataxia, intention tremor, nystagmus |
| Canine degenerative myelopathy (SOD1) | Many breeds | GP ataxia with para-to tetraparesis, ↓ spinal reflexes |
| Neuroaxonal dystrophy | Rottweiler (VSP11), Papillon (PLA2G6), others | Dysmetria, GP/cerebellar ataxia, ± tremor |
| Multiple systems degeneration | Kerry Blue Terrier, Chinese Crested | Cerebellar ataxia → basal ganglia degeneration → festination → euthanasia by 1–2 years |

(Ch 244)

**Necrotizing polioencephalomyelopathies (brain thiamine transport disorder)**
- Yorkshire Terrier (SLC19A3.1): Acute seizures; altered mentation; dysphagia; central blindness; hypermetria; facial hypalgesia; tetraparesis.
- Alaskan Husky (SLC19A3.1): Chronic progressive seizures.
- MRI: Bilateral symmetrical cavitated malacic lesions in thalamus; smaller lesions in striatum, brainstem. (Ch 244)

**Lysosomal Storage Diseases (LSDs)**
Broad category of enzyme deficiency diseases causing substrate accumulation in lysosomes. Cerebellar signs often first. Progressive to UMN weakness, behavioral abnormalities, seizures. (Ch 244)

*Notable LSD examples*:
- Globoid cell leukodystrophy (Krabbe): West Highland White Terrier, Cairn Terrier. Psychosine toxic to oligodendrocytes/Schwann cells. (Ch 244)
- Neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses (Batten disease, NCLs): Progressive visual impairment → cognitive/motor decline → seizures → premature death. (Ch 244)
- Lafora's disease: Myoclonic epilepsy; Beagle, Basset Hound, Miniature/Standard Poodle, Wirehaired Dachshund, Pembroke Welsh Corgi, Chihuahua; cats (DSH). (Ch 244)

**Metabolic encephalopathies (inborn errors)**

*L-2-hydroxyglutaric aciduria*: Yorkshire Terrier, American Staffordshire Terrier (L2HGDH), WHWT. Ataxia, dementia, tremor, seizures 6 months–7 years. MRI: symmetric gray matter hyperintensity. (Ch 244)

*Cobalamin deficiency*: Giant Schnauzer, Australian Shepherd (AMN), Beagle, Border Collie (CUBN). Altered mentation, seizures, inappetence, hyperammonemia, blood dyscrasia. ↑ urine MMA + ↓ serum cobalamin. Signs resolve with parenteral cyanocobalamin. (Ch 244)

#### Sleep Disorders

**REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD)**: Pathological loss of REM atonia → violent limb/facial movements, air chewing, aggressive behavior, vocalizations during sleep. Arousal quick and complete (contrast with seizures). Seen in dogs and cats. Common sequel to tetanus in dogs. Also reported in young adult Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retrievers with caudate nuclei necrosis and axonal dystrophy. **Treatment**: Potassium bromide 44 mg/kg PO q24h → decreases severity/frequency in 78% of treated dogs. (Ch 244)

**Narcolepsy**
- *Familial*: Doberman Pinscher, Labrador Retriever, Dachshund — autosomal recessive, defect in HCRTR2 gene (hypocretin receptor 2). Onset as early as 4 weeks; plateau at 6 months. CSF hypocretin levels NORMAL (receptor defect, not ligand deficiency). (Ch 244)
- *Sporadic/acquired*: CSF hypocretin very low or absent. Secondary to hypothalamic neoplasms, head trauma, encephalitis. (Ch 244)
- *Clinical signs*: Excessive daytime sleepiness, immediate-onset REM sleep after emotional trigger (eating, exercise, play); cataplexy = periods of acute muscular hypotonia lasting up to 20 minutes. (Ch 244)
- *Diagnosis*: Food-elicited cataplexy test (FECT). Physostigmine salicylate 0.025–0.1 mg/kg IV → increases cataplectic frequency within 15 minutes (narcoleptic dogs) vs no effect (normal dogs). DNA testing for familial cases. (Ch 244)
- *Treatment*:
  - Imipramine 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q8–12h (monitor: sedation, anticholinergic effects [tachycardia, hyperexcitability, tremor])
  - Clomipramine 1–3 mg/kg PO q12–24h (monitor: emesis, diarrhea, sedation, anticholinergic)
  - Desipramine 3 mg/kg PO q12h
  - Venlafaxine 2.5 mg/kg PO q12h (no cholinergic side-effects)
  - Methylphenidate 0.25–0.5 mg/kg PO q24h (monitor: seizures, excitement, tachycardia, tremor)
  - Yohimbine 0.045 mg/kg IV (monitor: seizures, excitement, ptyalism)
  - Author first-line: start imipramine at lowest dose, titrate up.
(Ch 244)

**Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome (CDS)**: Not explicitly covered as a separate entity in Chapter 244 (mentioned only in passing re: CMBs in Ch 243).
> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — CDS not given its own chapter; brief references only.

### Ch 245 — Neoplastic Brain Diseases

> ClinIQ: ✓ Intracranial Neoplasia (DIS-NEU-BRAINTUM / DIS-BRAIN-NEO)

**Epidemiology**
- Estimated incidences: dogs 14.5/100,000; cats 3.5/100,000. Intracranial neoplasms in 2.0–4.5% of dogs and 2% of cats at necropsy. (Ch 245)
- Most brain tumors in middle-aged to older animals (majority >5 years in dogs). (Ch 245)

**Primary brain tumors (PBT) — dogs**
- Meningiomas 45%, gliomas 40%, choroid plexus tumors (CPTs) 5% → ~90% of PBTs. (Ch 245)
- Median ages: meningiomas 10.5 years, gliomas 8 years, CPTs 5.5 years. (Ch 245)
- Predisposed breeds — meningioma: Golden Retriever, Boxer, Miniature Schnauzer, Rat Terrier. (Ch 245)
- Predisposed breeds — gliomas: Boxer, Boston Terrier, Bullmastiff, English/French Bulldogs (brachycephalic). Chromosome 26 locus (DENR, CAMKK2, P2RX7 variants). (Ch 245)
- ~50% of all canine intracranial tumors are SBTs: hemangiosarcoma (29–35%), pituitary tumors (11–25%), lymphoma (12–20%), metastatic carcinomas (11–20%). (Ch 245)

**Primary brain tumors (PBT) — cats**
- ~70% of feline intracranial tumors are PBTs; >50% are meningiomas. (Ch 245)
- Lymphoma (50%) + pituitary tumors (30%) = majority of SBTs in cats. (Ch 245)
- Median age: meningioma 11 years; neuroepithelial tumors and lymphoma 7–8 years. (Ch 245)
- Neutered male domestic shorthair cats predisposed to gliomas. (Ch 245)

**Clinical signs**
- Seizures most common in dogs (50–70% of forebrain tumors). New onset seizure >5 years old → high clinical suspicion. (Ch 245)
- Brain tumor — structural epilepsy risk increased with: frontal lobe tumor, falcine/subtentorial herniation, marked contrast enhancement. (Ch 245)
- Cats: personality/behavioral changes most common (16–67%); seizures in ~25%. (Ch 245)
- Signs of ICH/herniation: depressed consciousness (stupor to coma), anisocoria, ↓/absent PLR, decerebrate/decerebellate rigidity, nystagmus, cardiorespiratory alterations. (Ch 245)
- CTH (caudal transtentorial herniation): compresses midbrain → oculomotor nucleus → strabismus + mydriatic pupil ipsilateral to herniation + ↓ consciousness. (Ch 245)
- FMH (foramen magnum herniation): cerebellar vermis caudal displacement → death, nystagmus, head tilt, gag deficits, tongue weakness, altered respiratory rhythms. (Ch 245)
- 64–86% of dogs/cats with CTH or FMH have no clinical signs. (Ch 245)

### Ch 246 — Traumatic Brain Disease

> ClinIQ: ✓ Head Trauma (DIS-NEU-HEADTRAUMA)

**Normal ICP**: 5–12 mm Hg. (Ch 246)

**Cerebral perfusion pressure = MAP − ICP.** CBF held constant when MAP = 50–150 mmHg. (Ch 246)

**Cushing's reflex**: Severe ↑ ICP → ↓ CBF → ↑ brain CO₂ → ↑ sympathetic tone → ↑ systemic blood pressure → baroreceptors detect → ↑ vagal tone → bradycardia. (Ch 246)

**Secondary injury**: Depleted ATP, massive glutamate release, calcium/sodium influx, cytotoxic edema, arachidonic acid cascade, free radicals, inflammatory mediators. Exacerbated by hypoventilation/hypercapnia, hypoxemia, hypotension (<40 mmHg MAP), hyperglycemia. (Ch 246)

**Modified Glasgow Coma Scale (MGCS)**

Three components assessed: Consciousness, Brainstem reflexes, Motor activity.

*Consciousness levels*: Alert → Obtunded/demented → Stuporous → Comatose. (Ch 246)

*Key brainstem reflexes*:
1. Oculocephalic reflex (physiologic nystagmus — absent in brainstem injury)
2. Pupillary light reflex + pupil size (miosis = thalamic/sympathetic injury; mydriasis = midbrain or transtentorial herniation)
(Ch 246)

*Motor activity*: Ambulatory → Nonambulatory + voluntary movement → Recumbent + rigidity or ↓ extensor tone.
- Constant extensor rigidity + opisthotonus = brainstem OR cerebellar injury; key difference: cerebellar injury has **normal consciousness**.
(Ch 246)

**MGCS score >8 = at least 50% chance of survival; survival improves linearly with increasing MGCS.** MGCS correlated with outcome at 3 days, 1 month, 6 months. (Ch 246)

**MRI injury severity score I–IV**: Higher scores → negative outcomes. Midline shift = negative prognostic indicator. (Ch 246)

**Prognosis**: Mild-moderate injuries → typically good without long-term deficits. Severe injury → higher mortality + post-injury deficits/seizures (but overall incidence of post-traumatic seizures low in dogs and cats). (Ch 246)

### Ch 247 — Epilepsy

> ClinIQ: ✓ Idiopathic Epilepsy (DIS-WK-EPILEPSY)

**Definition** (ILAE): Epilepsy = disease of brain characterized by enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures; clinically = 2+ unprovoked seizures at least 24 hours apart. (Ch 247)

**Classification**
1. **Idiopathic epilepsy (IE)**: 2+ unprovoked seizures; known or presumed genetic predisposition; functional neuronal defect; >50% of canine epilepsy. (Ch 247)
2. **Structural epilepsy**: Recurrent seizures caused by congenital or acquired brain pathology (neoplastic, inflammatory, cerebrovascular, anomalous, degenerative). 17–45% of dogs in general population. (Ch 247)
3. **Epilepsy of unknown cause**: Does not fulfill criteria for structural or IE; no thorough workup or findings inconclusive. (Ch 247)

**IVETF Tiered Approach to diagnosis of IE in dogs**

| Tier | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **Tier I** | ≥2 unprovoked seizures ≥24 hours apart; seizure onset 6 months – 6 years; normal interictal physical and neurological exam; no significant minimum database abnormalities (CBC, biochemistry, fasting bile acid/ammonia, urinalysis); family history of IE supports |
| **Tier II** | Tier I PLUS unremarkable fasting and postprandial bile acids; no brain MRI abnormalities associated with seizures; unremarkable CSF analysis |
| **Tier III** | Tiers I + II PLUS ictal or interictal EEG abnormalities characteristic for seizure disorders |

(Ch 247)

**Age associations**: Dogs 6 months–6 years → significantly more likely IE than structural. Dogs >6 years (especially >7 years) → 71–88% structural cause. (Ch 247)

**Interictal exam**: Neurological abnormalities in 90% of dogs with confirmed structural epilepsy; 33% of structural epilepsy dogs have normal interictal exam. (Ch 247)

**Breed predisposition for IE**: Australian Shepherd Dog, Beagle, Belgian Shepherd, Belgian Terveuren, Bernese Mountain Dog, Border Collie, Border Terrier, CKCS, Dalmatian, English Springer Spaniel, Finnish Spitz, Golden Retriever, Hungarian Vizsla, Irish Wolfhound, Italian Spinone, Keeshond, Labrador Retriever, Lagotto Romagnolo, Petit Basset Griffon Vendéen, Rhodesian Ridgeback, Shetland Sheepdog, Standard Poodle. (Ch 247)

**Prevalence of IE in general canine population**: 0.62–0.75%. Breed-specific rates: Labrador Retriever 3%, Irish Wolfhound 18%. (Ch 247)

**Indications to initiate ASD therapy** (ACVIM + IVETF consensus):
1. ≥2 seizures within 6-month period
2. History of cluster seizures or status epilepticus (≥5 minutes or repeated seizures with incomplete recovery)
3. Postictal signs prolonged (>24 hours), severe, and/or unusual
4. Increasing seizure frequency/duration/severity over 3 interictal periods
(Ch 247)

**Antiseizure drugs — dogs**

| Drug | Recommended oral starting dose | Time to steady state | Adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phenobarbital | 2.5–3 mg/kg q12h | 14–21 days | Sedation, ataxia, polyphagia, PU/PD, hyperexcitability, hepatotoxicosis, blood dyscrasias, dermatosis |
| Bromide | 30 mg/kg q24h | 100–200 days | Sedation, ataxia, polyphagia, PU/PD, inappetence, vomiting, diarrhea, pancreatitis, bromism (ataxia, weakness, altered mentation) |
| Imepitoin | 10 mg/kg q12h* | 2 days | Sedation, ataxia, polyphagia, PU/PD, hyperexcitability, aggression |
| Levetiracetam (IR) | 20 mg/kg q8h | 1 day | Sedation, ataxia, behavioral changes, vomiting, diarrhea |
| Levetiracetam (XR) | 30 mg/kg q12h | 1 day | Same |
| Zonisamide | 5–10 mg/kg q12h** | 5 days | Sedation, ataxia, inappetence, weight loss, vomiting, diarrhea |

*When used as add-on with phenobarbital: 5 mg/kg q12h.
**Higher end when co-administered with phenobarbital. (Ch 247)

**Antiseizure drugs — cats**

| Drug | Recommended oral starting dose | Adverse effects |
|---|---|---|
| Phenobarbital | 1.5–2.5 mg/kg q12h | Sedation, ataxia, polyphagia, weight gain, PU/PD, anorexia, blood dyscrasias, pseudolymphoma, pruritus |
| Imepitoin | 30 mg/kg q12h | Sedation, ataxia, hypersalivation, inappetence, vomiting |
| Levetiracetam (IR) | 20 mg/kg q8h | Sedation, ataxia, hypersalivation, inappetence |
| Zonisamide | 2.5 mg/kg q12h | Sedation, ataxia, inappetence, vomiting, diarrhea |

**Bromide NOT recommended in cats** — risk of life-threatening allergic airway disease. (Ch 247)

**ACVIM monotherapy recommendations**: Phenobarbital and imepitoin = high (likely effective); bromide = moderate (most likely effective); levetiracetam and zonisamide = low (may not be effective). (Ch 247)

**Feline first choice**: Phenobarbital (meta-analysis 2018); levetiracetam superior to phenobarbital for feline audiogenic reflex seizures (randomized controlled study). (Ch 247)

**Therapeutic drug monitoring (phenobarbital)**: Target serum concentration: **15–40 µg/mL** (reference range used in multiple chapters). Measure at steady state (14–21 days), when seizures persist, when toxicity suspected, and at 6–12 month intervals. (Ch 247)

*Note: The 15–40 µg/mL reference range is the standard veterinary therapeutic range cited throughout the text and confirmed by pharmacokinetic studies.*

**Bromide therapeutic range**: Established (referenced in text though specific range not restated in Ch 247; standard: dogs 1–2 mg/mL; higher with monotherapy). (Ch 247)

**Dosage adjustment formula**: New dosage (mg/kg) = current dosage (mg/kg) × (desired serum concentration ÷ measured serum concentration). (Ch 247)

**Treatment success (IVETF)**: Seizure freedom for time exceeding 3× longest pre-treatment interseizure interval AND minimum 3 months. **Partial success**: Prevention of cluster/status; <1 seizure/3 months; reduced seizure severity. (Ch 247)

**Drug-resistant epilepsy**: ~30% prevalence in dogs and cats with epilepsy. Defined as failure of ≥2 tolerated and appropriate ASD trials. (Ch 247)

**Acute management in home — rescue therapy**

*Rectal diazepam*: 1 mg/kg rectally at onset; up to 3 times over 24 hours. Increase to 2 mg/kg in dogs receiving phenobarbital (phenobarbital increases diazepam metabolism). Dispense in original tinted glass vial (inactivated by light, adheres to plastic). (Ch 247)

*Intranasal midazolam*: 0.2 mg/kg via atomizer. Randomized multicenter clinical trial: superior to rectal diazepam for short-term management of status epilepticus in dogs. (Ch 247)

*Pulse levetiracetam (home setting)*: Initial 60 mg/kg PO (after seizure or at pre-ictal signs onset), then 20 mg/kg q8h until seizure-free 48 hours. (Ch 247)

**Prognosis — IE**: Many dogs/cats with IE expect near-normal lifespan. Remission (~1 year seizure-free): ~15% dogs, ~45% cats with IE. Cluster seizures and status epilepticus negatively influence survival. Border Collies, Australian Shepherd Dogs, Boxers, German Shepherd Dogs more likely to suffer cluster seizures. (Ch 247)

**Feline-specific syndromes**
- Temporal lobe epilepsy-like syndrome: focal seizures with orofacial involvement (salivation, facial twitching, lip smacking, chewing, licking, swallowing) + motor arrest; hippocampal changes on MRI or histopathology. (Ch 247)
- Feline audiogenic reflex seizures: Geriatric cats (median onset 15 years); myoclonic → tonic-clonic; triggered by high-frequency sounds; hearing impairment/deafness in 50%; levetiracetam preferred. (Ch 247)

### Ch 248 — Vestibular Disease

> ClinIQ: ✓ Idiopathic Vestibular (DIS-NEU-IDVEST), Congenital Vestibular (DIS-NEU-CONGVEST)

**Central vs. Peripheral Vestibular Disease — Differentiation Table**

| Clinical sign | Central vestibular | Peripheral vestibular |
|---|---|---|
| Head tilt | Ipsi- or contralateral | Ipsilateral |
| Mental status | Normal, obtunded, stupor, coma | Normal, may be disoriented |
| Conscious proprioception | Reduced/absent ipsilateral to lesion | Normal |
| Paresis | Possible | No |
| Nystagmus | Horizontal, rotatory, **vertical**, **changing directions** | Horizontal or rotatory only |
| Positional strabismus | Yes | Yes |
| Horner's syndrome | Rarely | Frequently |
| Cranial nerve deficits | CN V, VII, IX, X, XII | CN VII only |
| Hypermetria, tremor | If cerebellar involvement | No |

(Ch 248)

**Key nystagmus rules**:
- Fast phase **away from lesion** = peripheral vestibular.
- Fast phase toward OR away from lesion = central vestibular.
- **Vertical nystagmus** or **changing-direction nystagmus** = central.
- Spontaneous nystagmus >66/minute: 95% specificity and 85% sensitivity for peripheral vestibular lesion. (Ch 248)

**Paradoxical vestibular syndrome**: Head tilt contralateral to lesion → lesion in flocculonodular lobe, fastigial nucleus, or caudal cerebellar peduncle → excessive ipsilateral extensor tone → lean/tilt away from lesion. Ipsilateral hypermetria, truncal sway, tremor common. Proprioception reduced ipsilateral to lesion (confirms side). (Ch 248)

**Differential diagnosis table — central vs. peripheral**

| Etiology | Central | Peripheral |
|---|---|---|
| Vascular | Infarction, septic emboli, hemorrhage | — |
| Inflammatory/infectious | MUO (GME, NLE, NME); CDV; Neospora/Toxoplasma; fungal; bacterial; rickettsial | Otitis media/interna; nasopharyngeal polyps |
| Traumatic | Head trauma | Head trauma |
| Anomaly | Intracranial arachnoid cyst; Chiari-like malformation | Congenital vestibular disease |
| Metabolic/toxic | Metronidazole intoxication; thiamine deficiency | Hypothyroidism; aminoglycosides; chlorhexidine |
| Idiopathic | — | Idiopathic (geriatric) vestibular disease |
| Neoplastic | Primary or metastatic brain tumor | Neoplasia of middle/inner ear; MPNST |
| Degenerative | Neurodegenerative disease; storage diseases | — |

(Ch 248)

**Congenital vestibular disease**: Dog breeds: German Shepherd, Doberman, Smooth Haired Fox Terrier, English Cocker Spaniel. Cat breeds: Siamese, Burmese, Tonkinese. Bilateral: Akitas, Beagles. Onset 3–12 weeks. No effective treatment; visual/proprioceptive compensation. Deafness permanent. (Ch 248)

**Hypothyroidism and vestibular disease**: Can cause peripheral AND central vestibular disease. Central: arteriosclerosis-associated CNS vascular accidents. Myxedematous deposits compress CN VII + CN VIII in internal acoustic meatus → peripheral. (Ch 248)

**Idiopathic/geriatric vestibular syndrome**:
- Peracute onset, often severe.
- Unilateral (occasionally bilateral — especially cats).
- Etiology unknown; disturbance in endolymphatic production/flow. Cats in northeastern USA may be associated with Cuterebra migration.
- Nausea and vomiting common.
- Facial nerve involvement described in recent study (challenges prior teaching that facial paralysis absent).
- **Treatment**: Supportive — fluids, antiemetics: maropitant (NK-1 antagonist), metoclopramide (D2 antagonist), ondansetron (5-HT3 antagonist) may provide additional benefit. Physical rehabilitation important to stimulate CNS compensation.
- **Prognosis**: Improves within 2–3 days; resolves within 2–4 weeks. Residual head tilt possible.
(Ch 248)

**Metronidazole toxicosis**:
- Doses of 60 mg/kg/day × 3–14 days commonly reported to cause toxicosis; doses as low as 30 mg/kg/day incriminated. (Ch 248)
- Dogs: Acute to subacute bilateral central vestibular signs, symmetrical or asymmetrical generalized ataxia, positional **vertical nystagmus**. Cerebellar involvement: hypermetria + intention tremors. (Ch 248)
- Cats: Forebrain disturbances — seizures, blindness, ataxia (rather than vestibular signs). (Ch 248)
- Mechanism: Interaction with GABA receptors in cerebellum and vestibular nuclei. (Ch 248)
- **Treatment**: Drug discontinuation + supportive care. **Diazepam 0.5 mg/kg IV, then 0.5 mg/kg PO q8h × 3 days** → shortens recovery to 1–3 days (vs 2–3 weeks without). (Ch 248)

**Thiamine deficiency** (Ch 248 cross-ref):
- Initial signs: anorexia, lethargy.
- Later: vestibular signs (usually bilateral), pupillary dilation, marked ventroflexion of head/neck.
- **Treatment**: **Thiamine 12.5–50 mg daily, IM, SC, or PO** → rapid improvement possible; residual signs may persist. (Ch 248)

### Ch 249 — Cranial Neuropathies

> ClinIQ: ✓ Horner's Syndrome (DIS-NEU-HORNERS)
> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidates): Idiopathic Trigeminal Neuropathy, Idiopathic Facial Paralysis (as distinct entries beyond current coverage)

**General principles**: Cranial neuropathy = dysfunction of any CN anywhere along its anatomic course. Idiopathic cranial neuropathy = exclusion diagnosis. Most idiopathic cranial mononeuropathies are self-limiting. (Ch 249)

**Optic neuritis (CN II)**:
- Bilateral acute vision loss + mydriatic unresponsive pupils. Optic disc swelling on fundoscopy (not always present). (Ch 249)
- ERG: NORMAL in optic neuritis (distinguishes from retinal disease; SARDS = extinguished ERG). (Ch 249)
- Causes: CDV, mycotic, protozoal, GME, neoplasms (meningioma, glioma, lymphoma, pituitary). (Ch 249)
- Idiopathic: systemic immunosuppressive prednisone. Prognosis for visual recovery: guarded to poor. (Ch 249)

**Idiopathic trigeminal neuritis (ITN)**:
- Most common cause of dropped jaw in dogs. (Ch 249)
- Primarily mandibular motor dysfunction; trigeminal sensory deficits in ~1/3 of dogs; concurrent facial nerve paralysis, masticatory muscle atrophy, or Horner's occasionally. (Ch 249)
- Treatment: supportive care + assisted feeding. Affected dogs usually recover within **2–4 weeks**. (Ch 249)
- MRI: Normal nerve signal/morphology OR diffusely enlarged T2-hyperintense nerves. (Ch 249)

**Trigeminal nerve sheath tumor (NST)**:
- Common cause of unilateral neurogenic masticatory muscle atrophy. Facial hypalgesia ± paresthesia (facial rubbing). Horner's syndrome. (Ch 249)
- Treatment: surgical debulking, radiotherapy, glucocorticoids. **Stereotactic RT: median survival 1–2 years.** (Ch 249)

**Cavernous sinus syndrome (CSS) and orbital fissure syndrome (OFS)**:
- Dysfunction of ≥2 CNs: III, IV, VI, ophthalmic or maxillary branch of V. Signs: external + internal ophthalmoplegia, reduced corneal sensitivity, neurotrophic keratitis, ptosis. (Ch 249)
- Most common causes: primary or metastatic neoplasms. (Ch 249)

**Idiopathic facial nerve paralysis**:
- Idiopathic in 75% of dogs and 25% of cats. (Ch 249)
- 60% unilateral, 40% bilateral. (Ch 249)
- MRI: commonly associated with various segments of facial nerve enhancement. Recovery less likely or prolonged if enhancement present. (Ch 249)
- Animals that recover usually do so within **3–8 weeks**. (Ch 249)

**Horner's syndrome (HS)**:
- Three-neuron oculosympathetic pathway: 1st order (rostral brainstem → cervical spinal cord; cell bodies T1–T3) → 2nd order (vagosympathetic trunk) → 3rd order (cranial cervical ganglion → eye/adnexa). (Ch 249)
- Clinical signs: miosis, ptosis, protrusion of third eyelid, enophthalmos. (Ch 249)
- Topical phenylephrine test: mydriasis within 20 minutes = post-ganglionic lesion; >20 minutes = pre-ganglionic. (Ch 249)
- **Idiopathic HS**: Most cases; mostly post-ganglionic (recent data). **Golden Retrievers predisposed.** Resolves in many dogs within **3–4 months** — mainly aesthetic problem unless bilateral. (Ch 249)

**Deafness (CN VIII cochlear)**:
- Most common cause of congenital hereditary sensorineural deafness: white coat color (piebald and merle genes). (Ch 249)
- Acquired sensorineural: presbycusis (age-related) OR ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides). (Ch 249)
- Diagnosis: brainstem auditory evoked response (BAER) testing. (Ch 249)

**Megaesophagus (vagus/accessory nerve)**:
- Most canine cases acquired and idiopathic. Must rule out: polyneuropathies, polymyopathies, myasthenia gravis, endocrinopathies (hypoadrenocorticism, hypothyroidism), intoxications (lead, organophosphate, thallium), brainstem disease. (Ch 249)
- Treatment: directed at underlying cause + assisted feeding (Bailey chair, gastrostomy tube). Prognosis highly variable by etiology. (Ch 249)

**Laryngeal paralysis**:
- In older, large-breed dogs: often initial manifestation of progressive, degenerative polyneuropathy. (Ch 249)
- Signs: dysphonia, inspiratory stridor, respiratory distress. (Ch 249)
- Causes: hypothyroidism, trauma to recurrent laryngeal nerve, neoplasia, lead intoxication, brainstem disease. (Ch 249)
