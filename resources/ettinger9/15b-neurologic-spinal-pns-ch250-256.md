#### Cervical Spondylomyelopathy (CSM / "Wobbler Syndrome")

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-CCSM / DIS-NEU-WOBBLER — enrichment: disc-associated vs osseous forms, corticosteroid dose, surgical outcomes

**Spinal localization:** Cervical (C1–C5 and C6–T2 region). Two-engine gait: choppy short-strided thoracic limbs, wide-based abducted pelvic limb gait with longer stride. Postural reaction deficits present. Thoracic limb flexor (withdrawal) reflex decreased with normal-to-increased extensor tone. Pelvic limb reflexes normal to increased. Cervical hyperesthesia not a prominent feature.

**Forms:**
- **Disc-associated CSM:** Middle-aged large-breed dogs (mostly Doberman Pinschers). IVD protrusion at C5-6 and C6-7 with or without hypertrophy of dorsal longitudinal ligament or ligamentum flavum.
- **Osseous CSM:** Young adult giant-breed dogs (especially Great Danes). Compression from malformation/proliferation of vertebral arch (dorsally), articular processes (dorsolaterally), or pedicles (laterally).

**Diagnosis:** Advanced imaging required — MRI gold standard. MRI detects spinal cord signal changes in ~60% of dogs. CT myelography preferred over plain CT. Survey radiographs used only as screening.

**Treatment:**
- Medical: Exercise restriction is most important. Body harness instead of neck collar. **Corticosteroids: prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q12–24h, tapering over 2–4 weeks.** Omeprazole often co-prescribed for GI protection. ~50% improve with medical management.
- Surgical: ~70–80% of dogs improve with surgery, though many deteriorate within months to 2–3 years post-op. >30 surgical techniques described; no single technique is clearly superior.

#### Degenerative Lumbosacral Stenosis (DLSS)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-DLSS — enrichment: epidural steroid injection outcomes, surgical success rates, continence prognosis

**Spinal localization:** L4–S3 (cauda equina). Most commonly L7–S1 intervertebral disc. No spinal cord at L7–S1 level in dogs; femoral nerve not affected, so true paraplegia does not occur.

**Clinical signs:** Lumbosacral pain is chief sign. Pelvic limb weakness without proprioceptive ataxia; "stiff" gait; sometimes unilateral or bilateral lameness (can mimic orthopedic disease). Decreased flexor reflex (especially poor hock flexion); normal patellar reflex (occasionally "pseudohyperreflexia"). Late-stage: urinary and/or fecal incontinence.

**Diagnosis:** Signalment: middle-aged to older, large-breed dogs (GSD predisposed genetically). Survey radiographs first (rule out discospondylitis, neoplasm, trauma). Definitive: CT and MRI (primarily MRI). Critical caveat: compression on imaging does not correlate with severity — must match imaging to history and neurologic exam.

**Treatment:**
- Medical: Enforced rest, short walks, NSAIDs or prednisone (not both), gabapentin, body-weight reduction. Success rate 55% in referral population (higher in general population).
- Epidural corticosteroid injection: Improved outcome in 79% of dogs in one retrospective study using fluoroscopic guidance.
- Surgical: Dorsal laminectomy at L7–S1 ± facetectomy ± stabilization. Success rate 66.7–95%. Fecal/urinary incontinence resolves in only 13–45% of surgical cases (failure to resolve in 55–87%).

#### Extradural Synovial Cysts (ESCs)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — extradural synovial/ganglion juxtafacet cysts

Two cyst types: synovial (synovial lining containing fluid) and ganglion (myxoid material, no specific lining). Combined term "juxtafacet cysts." Most frequent locations: lumbosacral and cervical regions. Median age 8 years in large-breed dogs. MRI diagnosis of choice (sensitivity 90% in humans; 70% CT). MRI: well-circumscribed extradural masses, hyperintense T2. CSF: albuminocytologic dissociation typical. All reported surgical cases had positive outcomes.

---

#### Spinal Arachnoid Diverticula (SAD)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — spinal arachnoid diverticulum/cyst; note Pug predisposition and connection to syringomyelia

Focal dilations of subarachnoid space causing progressive compressive myelopathy. Previously called "arachnoid cysts" (a misnomer — not closed cavities). ~55% cervical (large-breed dogs), ~45% thoracolumbar (small-breed dogs). Most common sites: C2–C3, T9–T13. Male predisposition 2:1 to 4:1. Median age 2.5 years (range 2.5–13 years). Pugs: older median age (59 months), associated with concurrent constrictive myelopathy.

**Diagnosis:** MRI modality of choice. Post-myelogram CT demonstrates contrast-filled teardrop-shaped expansions. ~20% of dogs show albuminocytologic dissociation; ~10% mild mononuclear pleocytosis.

**Treatment/Prognosis:**
- Medical (glucocorticoid therapy): Some cases stable for long periods.
- Surgical: Fenestration with durotomy ± closure, durectomy, marsupialization, or shunting device. 82% surgically treated experienced long-term neurological improvement; 30% medically managed improved. **Better prognosis: age <3 years and duration <4 months.** Pugs have poor long-term prognosis after surgery for thoracolumbar SAD (85% experience late recurrence).

#### Spondylosis Deformans

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (not a clinical-decision candidate by itself — no neurologic signs)

Very common degenerative process, essentially no clinical significance by itself. Osteophytes arise from vertebral endplate periphery; sclerotic endplates. Does not cause neurological signs; does not warrant further work-up unless concurrent neurologic disease present. Prevalence 18.1% (2041-dog retrospective).

---

#### Disseminated Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis (DISH)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate for incidental/workup context)

Systemic ossification affecting axial and appendicular skeleton. Flowing ossification along ventrolateral vertebral column. Prevalence 3.8% (78/20,141 dogs). Most common at T6–T10 and L2–L6. Can cause foraminal stenosis leading to nerve root compression. Distinguished from spondylosis by signal intensity on MRI: DISH bone has signal equal to vertebral bone marrow on T1 and T2; spondylosis osteophytes are hypointense relative to marrow.

#### Degenerative Myelopathy (DM)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING as distinct disease page — **strong candidate for new entry**. SOD1 genetic test is key enrichment.

**Etiology/Pathogenesis:** Primarily thoracolumbar (T3–L3) spinal cord of medium-to large-breed dogs. Also called canine degenerative radiculomyelopathy (CDRM). Incompletely penetrant autosomal mode of inheritance identified with **mutation in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene**. Neural degeneration: "gain of toxic function" of mutant SOD1. Primary central axonopathy, not Wallerian degeneration. Primarily affects dorsal aspect of lateral funiculi and medial portion of dorsal funiculi.

**Clinical signs:** Chronic thoracolumbar (T3–L3) myelopathy. Begins with mild proprioceptive ataxia and paraparesis, initially possibly asymmetric, slowly progressive. **No spinal hyperesthesia — this is a key diagnostic feature** distinguishing DM from compressive myelopathies. Spinal reflexes pelvic limbs: normal to hyperreflexive. Decreased to absent patellar reflexes in ~10–15% (sensory, not LMN disturbance). Extensor tone always increased in early phase. Progression: 6–12 months to non-ambulatory in large breeds. If kept alive, progresses to LMN deficits, tetraparesis, dysphonia, dysphagia, and ultimately hypoventilation.

**Diagnosis:**
- Mean age of onset: usually ≥5 years; mean age 9 years in large breeds; mean 11.2 years in Pembroke Welsh Corgis.
- Most common breeds: German Shepherd Dogs, Boxers; also Pembroke Welsh Corgis (female predominance 1.6:1 in Corgis).
- Diagnosis by **exclusion of other myelopathies**. MRI typically normal; some concurrent mild disc protrusions possible. Must rule out intramedullary lesions/neoplasms with MRI.
- **DNA test based on SOD1 mutation is commercially available.** Homozygotes for the mutation are at risk for DM; heterozygotes are carriers unlikely to develop clinical DM.
- CSF: normal or increased protein with normal cell count (albuminocytologic dissociation). Phosphorylated neurofilament heavy (pNF-H) elevated in CSF in all DM stages — proposed biomarker.
- Corticosteroid trial strategy: DM dogs will NOT improve; dogs with compressive myelopathy often do improve. Can be used to differentiate.

**Treatment/Prognosis:**
- No pharmacological therapy alters disease course (vitamin supplementation, glucocorticoids, aminocaproic acid, N-acetylcysteine — all showed no benefit).
- **Only physical therapy has been shown to alter long-term course of DM** (Ch 256 reference).
- One retrospective study: dogs with intense physical therapy (walking, PROM, massage, hydrotherapy) survived mean 255 days; moderate regimen 130 days; no physical therapy 55 days.
- Long-term prognosis poor. Average time from diagnosis to death in Pembroke Welsh Corgis: **1.25 years.**

#### Intervertebral Disc Disease (IVDD)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-IVDD / DIS-NEU-IVDD — significant enrichment: IVDD grading, DPP thresholds, recovery %

**Classification:**
- **Hansen Type I IVDD:** Herniation of nucleus pulposus through annular fibers and extrusion of nuclear material into spinal canal. Typically chondroid disc degeneration. Acute onset. Predominantly chondrodystrophic breeds; also Doberman, Labrador.
- **Hansen Type II IVDD:** Annular protrusion caused by shifting of central nuclear material. Late chondroid degeneration. Chronic compression. Older non-chondrodystrophic breeds.
- **ANNPE (Acute Non-Compressive Nucleus Pulposus Extrusion):** "Type III," missile disc, high-velocity low volume extrusion. Typically associated with trauma or strenuous exercise. Often severe, strongly asymmetric (90% of cases). No pain after immediate incident. Differentiated from FCE by MRI.
- **HNPE (Hydrated Nucleus Pulposus Extrusion):** Formerly intraspinal or discal cyst. Most common in cervical region. Non-painful severe cervical myelopathy; non-ambulatory tetraparesis most common; bilateral symmetrical signs. Excellent prognosis with or without surgery.

---

##### Cervical IVDD

**Epidemiology:** Accounts for 14–25% of IVD disorders in dogs. Chondrodystrophic small breeds (Dachshunds, Toy Poodles, Beagles) predominate; large breeds (Labradors, Dalmatians, Dobermans) up to 24% of cases. Mean age 6.3–8.6 years.

**Location:** Small breeds: 80% at C2–C4, 44–59% at C2–C3 alone. Large breeds: mostly C6–C7.

**Clinical signs:** Cervical pain up to 60% (no deficits) to ~90% overall. Ambulatory tetraparesis in up to 42%; non-ambulatory tetraparesis 11–22%; tetraplegia 2–7%. Nerve root signature in 22–50%. Thoracic limb reflex loss usually implies C6–T2 lesion, but 36% of dogs with reduced thoracic limb reflexes actually have C1–C5 lesion.

**Diagnosis:** Radiographic accuracy for disc site localization: 35%. MRI is gold standard.

**Treatment:**
- Conservative: Strict cage rest 3–4 weeks; body harness; anti-inflammatory medications; analgesics; gabapentin 10–20 mg/kg PO q8h for foraminal/nerve root involvement; muscle relaxants (diazepam, methocarbamol).
- Recurrence rates with conservative management: 33–36% or more. One study: 49% successful outcome, 33% recurred, 18% therapeutic failure.
- Surgical: Ventral slot decompression — complete recovery in up to 90% at 1 month, 98% at 12 months. Large breeds with caudal cervical disc: only 66% success. Recurrence rate post-surgery ~5–10%, mean time to recurrence 91 days. Significant adverse events in up to 10% (neurologic deterioration, respiratory difficulty, hemorrhage).

---

##### Thoracolumbar IVDD

**Localization:** Hansen Type I most common at thoracolumbar junction (T12–T13 to L1–L2). Most common in chondrodystrophic breeds. Large breeds: most common at L1–L2; German Shepherd Dogs sometimes T1–T5.

**Neurologic grading (Modified Frankel / Grade system — as referenced in text):**
The text discusses degrees of dysfunction ranging from spinal pain only to paraplegia with/without pain perception. The critical decision point is deep pain perception (DPP):

| Neurologic Status | Recovery with Conservative Rx | Recovery with Surgical Rx |
|---|---|---|
| Ambulatory paraparetic | 72.5% | 98.4% |
| Non-ambulatory paraparetic | 79.8% | 93% |
| Paraplegic with intact DPP | 56% | 93% |
| Paraplegic WITHOUT DPP | 22.4% | 61% |

Data from 502 dogs (Ch 250).

**Deep Pain Perception (DPP) — prognostic significance:**
- Most important prognostic indicator for functional recovery.
- **"Absence of pain perception for longer than 48 hours does not seem to negatively impact outcome any more than absence for less than 48 hours"** — but decompressive surgery should be performed as early as possible.
- Poor prognosis if pain perception does not return within **2 to 4 weeks** from time of surgery.

**Myelomalacia:** Mean incidence focal/ascending-descending myelomalacia: **13.9%** in dogs with acute thoracolumbar IVDD and loss of nociception. Possibly higher in French Bulldogs. pNF-H biomarker has high sensitivity and specificity for PMM identification.

**Conservative management indications:** First-time spinal pain only; mild-to-moderate paraparesis; financial constraints (only reason for non-surgical management of recumbent patient). Cage rest 3–4 weeks + pain relief (anti-inflammatories, opioids, muscle relaxants). Recurrence rates 30–50% in minimally affected ambulatory dogs; usually within 6 months to 1 year.

**Surgical management indications:** Spinal pain/paresis unresponsive to medical therapy; recurrence or progression; paraplegia with or without intact DPP.
- Corticosteroids/high-dose methylprednisolone sodium succinate: **Do NOT improve outcome in surgical IVDD; associated with higher GI/UTI complications, increased hospital stay and cost.**
- Fenestration recommended along with decompression to minimize future disc extrusions.
- Recurrence post-surgery: 2–42%; time to recurrence usually 1 month to 2 years.

**ANNPE features (differentiating from FCE):**
- Acute severe myelopathy after trauma/vigorous exercise.
- 90% asymmetric presentation.
- Disc space narrowing visible on radiographs.
- MRI: abnormal signal within epidural space; cord heterogeneously hyperintense.
- Small volume — not creating permanent compression; may not be visible on MRI.
- Requires MRI to differentiate from FCE.

#### Atlantoaxial (AA) Instability

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-AA / DIS-NEU-AAI — enrichment: diagnostic measurements, conservative vs surgical outcomes, perioperative mortality

**Etiology:** Congenital or developmental anomalies most common. Dens dysplasia (34%), hypoplasia/aplasia (46%), dorsal angulation, separation of dens, absence of transverse ligament. ~24% of affected dogs have normal dens. Small breeds: Yorkshire Terriers, Chihuahuas, Miniature Poodles, Pomeranians, Pekingese. Traumatic AA subluxation can occur in any breed.

**Clinical signs:** Neck pain in most traumatic cases and 30–60% of congenital cases. Neurological deficits range from mild postural reaction abnormalities (56%) to tetraplegia (10%); gait dysfunction reported in up to 94%. Evidence of neck pain most common in most dogs. Rare tetraplegia cases can progress to respiratory compromise.

**Diagnosis:**
- Survey radiographs: increased space and angle (>10°) between dorsal lamina of atlas and dorsal spinous process of axis. Assess dens on ventrodorsal and oblique views.
- CT: assists with dens conformation, fracture detection, surgical implant planning.
- MRI: caudal fossa abnormalities, craniocervical junction anomalies, spinal cord lesions (hemorrhage, edema, syringomyelia).

**Treatment:**
- Conservative: Strict cage confinement 6 weeks + analgesia + rigid cervical brace (must come over head rostral to ears, extend caudally to sternum level). **Good long-term outcome in 38% (10/26) cases managed conservatively.** Dogs affected <30 days significantly more likely to have good outcome with conservative management vs >30 days.
- Surgical: Permanent AA fusion. **Perioperative mortality 10–30%.** Age of onset <24 months associated with greater odds of successful first surgery. Duration ≤10 months and lesser clinical severity associated with greater odds of successful final outcome.

#### Congenital Spinal Column Malformations

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — especially for brachycephalic breeds)

**Hemivertebrae:** French and English Bulldogs, Boston Terriers most affected. Up to 78–94% of French Bulldogs have hemivertebrae (usually incidental). Subtypes: butterfly vertebra, wedged vertebra, dorsal hemivertebra. T4–T9 most common location. Kyphosis >35° and step lesions associated with higher likelihood of neurological signs. Pugs have different subtypes (wedged vertebra) and more often associated with clinical signs. Surgical realignment/stabilization recommended over laminectomy alone; good to guarded prognosis.

**Caudal Articular Process Dysplasia (CAD):** French Bulldogs 70.4%, English Bulldogs 84.1%, Pugs 97% have CAD on CT. Most affected: T10–T13 caudal articular processes. Can lead to constrictive myelopathy (meningeal fibrosis) in Pugs — intradural adhesions between arachnoid and pia causing cord constriction. MRI: irregular subarachnoid space, linear hypointense bands, spinal cord distortion, bilateral V-shaped ventrolateral extradural lesions. Urinary/fecal incontinence resolves in <50% post-surgery.

---

#### Meningomyelitis

> ClinIQ: refer to Ch 242 for full coverage — only spinal-specific entities here

Rare without concurrent encephalitis. Review of 220 dogs: only 41 had focal spinal cord involvement. CDV and protozoa most common infectious causes. SRMA most commonly recognized probable non-infectious inflammatory disease.

#### Steroid-Responsive Meningitis-Arteritis (SRMA)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — important differential for young dogs with cervical pain

**Etiology:** Immunological cause suspected — vasculitis. Elevated IgA in CSF and serum, increased CSF and blood B-cell/T-cell ratios, elevated IL-6 and IL-8. High CD11a expression on PMNs promotes neutrophil passage into subarachnoid space.

**Signalment:** Young adults (8–18 months old); Beagles, Bernese Mountain Dogs, Boxers, German Wirehaired/Shorthaired Pointers, Border Collies, Jack Russell Terriers, Weimaraners, Golden Retrievers, Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retrievers, other medium-to-large breeds. Usually febrile and hyperesthetic with cervical rigidity and anorexia.

**Diagnosis:**
- CBC: marked peripheral neutrophilia with left shift.
- CSF: marked neutrophilic pleocytosis and protein elevation; **cell counts >100 cells/mcL common**; neutrophils non-degenerative.
- IgA elevated in CSF and serum (sensitivity 91%, specificity 78% in 311 dogs). Non-specific.
- Serum CRP significantly elevated; APPs above normal in all SRMA dogs; useful for monitoring remission (3-month serum CRP and CSF levels predict remission with high sensitivity).
- MRI: often normal; may reveal meningeal contrast enhancement, paraspinal muscle T2/STIR hyperintensity, perineal tissue signal changes.

**Treatment/Prognosis:**
- Immunosuppressive dosages of corticosteroids (Ch 144). **Up to 80% long-term remission** with early intensive corticosteroid therapy.
- Relapses in 16–47.5% of cases (including after 2 years of age). Approximately one-third relapse.
- Refractory: cytosine arabinoside or azathioprine (Ch 169). Surgical decompression if epidural hemorrhage.

#### Discospondylitis

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-DISCO — enrichment: antibiotic duration, brucellosis workup, fungal treatment

**Etiology:** Infection of IVD and adjacent vertebral endplates. Most common etiological agents: coagulase-positive Staphylococcus spp. (S. pseudintermedius or S. aureus). Others: Streptococcus, E. coli, Actinomyces, Brucella canis, Aspergillus spp. Young German Shepherd Dog bitches and Hungarian Vizslas predisposed to aspergillosis. Young Basset Hounds: tuberculosis. Hematogenous spread or direct inoculation.

**Most commonly affected sites:** L7–S1, caudal cervical, mid-thoracic, thoracolumbar spine.

**Diagnosis:**
- Elevated serum CRP; blood and urine cultures positive in up to 75% and 50% of cases respectively.
- Serologic testing for brucellosis required in all cases (up to 10% positive). Standard test: blood culture (bacteremia intermittent — may miss positive animals).
- Definitive: spinal radiographs — narrowing of disc space, endplate irregularity to gross lysis. Radiographic change may not be evident in first 2–4 weeks.
- CT: subtle endplate erosion, paravertebral soft-tissue swelling.
- MRI: increased T2/STIR signal, decreased T1 signal in disc and paravertebral tissues; contrast enhancement of endplates in 88% of sites; endplate erosion and STIR hyperintense, T2-hypointense bone marrow.

**Treatment:**
- Initial: potentiated amoxicillin or cephalexin, cage rest, analgesics. IV antibiotics for severe neurological compromise or sepsis.
- Duration: **8–16 weeks recommended**, though one 513-case study found mean duration of 54 weeks.
- Resolution of pain/fever expected within 5 days; complete neurological resolution may take 2–3 months.
- **Brucellosis:** Minimum 6-week course of doxycycline + rifampin ± gentamicin, combined with castration after 2 weeks of preoperative antibiotics.
- **Aspergillus spp.:** Itraconazole 5 mg/kg PO q24h, fluconazole, or voriconazole — long-term reports of success lacking; chronic recurrence likely.
- Surgical decompression: reserved for refractory cases or severe deficits with no improvement in 3–5 days.
- Prognosis generally good unless etiology is fungal, multiple lesions, vertebral fracture/subluxation, or concurrent endocarditis.

#### Spinal Cord Injury — Mechanisms and General Principles

> ClinIQ: refer to DIS-NEU-SPFX for fracture/luxation

Primary impact injury causes minimal immediate structural damage; most functional consequences arise from secondary cascade (impaired microcirculation, cellular apoptosis, necrosis) over several days (up to ~7 days in experimental models). Vasogenic edema is the main mechanism by which corticosteroids reduce compression-associated deficits — reduces blood vessel permeability.

**Corticosteroid controversy:** Current consensus: **corticosteroids are NOT helpful in ameliorating acute spinal cord insults** (traumatic or vascular). High-dose methylprednisolone sodium succinate initially showed benefit in humans (NASCIS-2) but this has since been disputed and is associated with adverse effects (GI disturbance, immunosuppression, muscle mass reduction). Corticosteroids may benefit compression-associated deficits (vasogenic edema reduction) but NOT primary spinal cord contusion or vascular lesions.

**Spinal shock:** Acute severe spinal cord injury can cause depression of reflex arcs caudal to lesion for ~24 hours or more. Avoid mislocalizing during this period; use cutaneous trunci (panniculus) reflex and painful palpation sites.

---

#### Vertebral Fracture-Luxation

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-SPFX — enrichment: three-column model, DPP as primary prognostic indicator

**Three-column model for stability:**
- Ventral column: ventral half of vertebral bodies + annulus (ventral) + ventral longitudinal ligament.
- Middle column: dorsal vertebral bodies + dorsal annulus + dorsal longitudinal ligament + pedicles.
- Dorsal column: laminae + spinous processes + articular processes + all associated ligaments.
- **If two of three columns interrupted: instability → strong indication for internal fixation.**

**Immediate management:** Secure animal to rigid board; avoid excessive movement; take radiographs of entire vertebral column (multiple lesions possible). Maintain normal blood pressure (critical for spinal cord integrity). ABC priorities before neurologic injuries.

**Surgical timing:** Carry out surgery as soon as possible (reduces mechanical injury and alleviates compression); patient must be stable enough for general anesthesia.

**Prognosis — primary indicator: deep pain sensation:**
- Cervical region: almost always retain DPP; those that don't usually die before presentation (respiratory compromise).
- Thoracolumbar with DPP intact: usually recover remarkably good function.
- **Thoracolumbar with loss of DPP: very guarded; few reported cases recover ability to walk.**
- Sacral cord (situated in L5–L6 vertebrae): ambulatory but pain/continence problems; bilateral LMN destruction → permanent incontinence.
- Full recovery can require ≥10 days to show ANY change; complete recovery may take 3 months or more.

#### Fibrocartilaginous Embolism (FCE)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-FCE — enrichment: MRI differentiation from ANNPE, intumescence prognosis, recovery timeline

**Pathogenesis:** Occlusion of spinal cord arterial supply by chondroid material (from intervertebral disc nucleus), causing spinal cord infarction. In hours after initial lesion, edema may develop causing mild worsening.

**Signalment:** More common in non-chondrodystrophic breeds; Miniature Schnauzer possibly at increased risk. Middle-aged to older animals. Feline FCE: higher prevalence in cranial cervical cord of cats (possibly associated with hyaline degeneration of ventral spinal artery and systemic hypertension).

**Classic presentation:**
- Instantaneous onset; often at exercise.
- Animal may cry out or simply collapse and is immediately neurologically impaired.
- **By time of veterinary presentation: typically NO pain (pathognomonic if combined with lateralized signs).**
- Often highly lateralized — striking mismatch between motor and sensory deficits.
- Gradual regression of signs over subsequent days.

**Diagnosis:**
- MRI is required (only modality that can reveal spinal cord parenchymal disease).
- Classic FCE MRI: small, often one-quadrant hyperintensity on T2; iso- or hypointense on T1. Many lesions show large T2/STIR hyperintense regions.
- Definitively diagnosed only at post mortem; most clinical diagnoses are presumptive.
- Evaluate for hypo- and hypercoagulable states (ch. 170) — hematomyelia and spinal cord thrombosis can produce virtually indistinguishable signs.

**Treatment:** No specific treatment. Supportive care: physical rehabilitation (walking assistance, underwater treadmill — but NOT passive range of motion due to possible adverse effects in spinal cord disease). Management of incontinence.

**Prognosis:**
- **T3–L3 or C1–C5 lesions with intact DPP: good outlook.**
- **Loss of DPP or large lesions affecting intumescences (C6–T2 or L4–S3): more guarded** — less scope for plastic responses if grey matter destroyed.
- Sacral cord lesions: persistent fecal/urinary incontinence likely.
- Allow **up to 3 months** to appreciate full extent of recovery.
- Some mild-to-moderately severe lateralized cases show considerable improvement within ~48 hours.

#### ANNPE (Acute Non-Compressive Nucleus Pulposus Extrusion)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-ANNPE — enrichment: differentiation from FCE

Seen in both traumatic (external trauma) and non-traumatic (vigorous exercise) settings. Collapsed disc spaces visible on radiographs (distinguish from FCE). MRI: abnormal signal in epidural space; cord heterogeneously hyperintense; nucleus extrusion may spread along epidural space and may even penetrate dura. Clinical signs often severe and asymmetric (90%); MRI required to differentiate from FCE.

---

#### Spinal Neoplasia

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-SPINEO (meningioma), DIS-NEU-PNST/DIS-PNST (nerve sheath tumors) — enrichment: compartment classification, breed-specific tumors

**Classification by location:**
1. **Extradural:** Most common. Osteosarcoma (large dogs), hemangiosarcoma (Golden Retrievers), fibrosarcoma, lymphoma, liposarcoma, multiple myeloma. Often associated with bone damage and pathological fractures. Metastases often present at diagnosis. Pain prominent. Generally **poor prognosis** — often malignant and may have metastases.
2. **Intradural/Extramedullary:** Meningiomas and nerve sheath tumors. Slow-growing. Meningiomas especially common at C1–C2 in dogs (particularly Boxers). Both tend to invade locally. Long-term (>12 months) disease-free interval possible with surgical resection of nerve sheath tumors alone. Meningioma surgical excision: alleviation of signs for 12 months or more (frequently regrow after incomplete excision). **Nerve sheath tumors worse prognosis** due to difficulty achieving complete excision.
3. **Intramedullary:** Less common. Astrocytoma, ependymoma. Variable histologic behavior — some well-circumscribed and excisable. Nephroblastoma: close to thoracolumbar junction of juvenile dogs (often German Shepherd Dogs); benign histology but locally invasive; surgical excision with good outcomes in some cases.

**Lymphoma:** Reasonably common spinal lesion in dogs; leading cause of spinal lesions in adult cats.

**Diagnosis challenge:** High variability between readers in correctly identifying compartment of intradural masses — recommendations for treatment may vary widely.

### Ch 252 — Peripheral Neuropathies
(Mariani CL)

---

#### Anatomy, Clinical Presentation, and Diagnostic Approach

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-POLYNEURO — enrichment: key electrodiagnostic tests, distinguishing NMJ vs nerve

PNS includes motor, sensory, and autonomic fibers. **Key clinical signs of LMN/PNS disease:** paresis, hypotonia, muscle atrophy, depressed or absent segmental spinal reflexes. EMG and nerve conduction studies (MNCV, SNCV) are the most useful diagnostic tests. F wave evaluation: proximal nerve and ventral nerve roots. SNCV and cord dorsum: sensory nerves and dorsal nerve roots. Muscle and nerve biopsies: confirm and characterize abnormalities.

---

#### Metabolic Neuropathies

**Diabetic Neuropathy:**
- Primarily in cats; rarely in dogs. Plantigrade stance frequent in cats. Paraparesis progressing in some cases to tetraparesis.
- Electrophysiology: MNCV, SNCV, F wave, cord dorsum all abnormal even with subtle EMG changes. Demyelination predominates.
- Treatment: glycemic control — improved control typically results in improvement or resolution of signs.

**Hypothyroid Neuropathy:**
- Both axonal damage and demyelination. Paresis involving 1–4 limbs; cranial nerve deficits (facial paresis, vestibular signs, trigeminal dysfunction — often unilateral, multiple nerves).
- May be associated with megaesophagus and laryngeal paralysis (causal role tenuous).
- Diagnosis: reduced free and total T4, elevated TSH. EMG: spontaneous activity; reduced MNCV and SNCV. CSF: albuminocytologic dissociation.
- Treatment: levothyroxine — clinical response begins within several days, complete by **3–8 weeks**. Cranial nerve deficits, megaesophagus, laryngeal paralysis: less likely to improve.

---

#### Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors (PNST)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-PNST / DIS-PNST — enrichment: brachial plexus location, MRI features, prognosis

Most common in large-breed dogs; rare in cats. Commonly arise from brachial plexus. Malignant characteristics (locally invasive, grow proximally up nerves and nerve roots). Chief signs: lameness and muscle atrophy (often the only signs for extended periods). Horner's syndrome with caudal brachial plexus involvement. Trigeminal nerve sheath tumors: most common cranial nerve neoplasm → unilateral masticatory muscle atrophy.

**Diagnosis:** EMG denervation potentials; MRI/CT for visualization; ultrasound for fine-needle aspiration.

**Treatment:** Surgical removal (often requires amputation + laminectomy with nerve root sectioning). Despite aggressive therapy, many tumors recur (tendency to grow proximally through multiple nerve roots).

---

#### Acute Polyradiculoneuritis

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-POLYRADIC — enrichment: raccoon exposure, IVIG trial, corticosteroid contraindication

**Clinical signs:** Acute ascending flaccid tetraparesis. Typically progress rapidly over **48–72 hours** (may continue up to 10 days). Pelvic limbs usually first, then thoracic. Severe cases involve cranial nerves and respiratory musculature → hypoventilation. Segmental spinal reflexes: reduced to absent. Sensation intact; many animals appear hyperesthetic.

**Background:** First recognized ~7–10 days after raccoon contact ("coonhound paralysis"). Identical signs seen without raccoon exposure. Immune-mediated (similarities to Guillain-Barré syndrome); shared antigens between inciting stimulus and peripheral nerve tissue. Serological, electrophysiological, and histopathological evidence support immune-mediated pathogenesis.

**Diagnosis:**
- Characteristic clinical signs and course.
- Differentials: tick paralysis, botulism, elapid envenomation (all NMJ diseases).
- Electrodiagnostics: early onset spontaneous activity; abnormalities on MNCV and F wave.
- CSF lumbar cistern: may reveal albuminocytologic dissociation.

**Treatment:**
- Primarily supportive — most animals recover if given sufficient time.
- Recovery time: weeks to 6 months in rare cases.
- **Corticosteroids NOT beneficial; worsens muscle atrophy and predisposes to secondary infections.**
- IVIG may shorten recovery time (one study, Ch 252 ref 48) — requires further study.
- Mechanical ventilation may be required for respiratory muscle involvement.
- **Recovery: most animals recover with supportive care (Video 252.4).**

#### Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (CIDP)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-POLYNEURO — enrichment: canine/feline CIDP features

Immune-mediated; histopathologically shows demyelination. Insidious onset, slowly progressive. Pelvic limbs first, then thoracic. Relapsing-remitting course in some. MNCV consistently decreased, often with conduction block. Many animals respond to **corticosteroids at immunosuppressive dosages**. Other modalities (IVIG, plasma exchange, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide) used in humans but not well studied in veterinary patients.

#### Geriatric-Onset Laryngeal Paralysis and Polyneuropathy (GOLPP)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — very common clinical entity

Idiopathic laryngeal paralysis in older dogs: one manifestation of more generalized peripheral neuropathy. Also called laryngeal paralysis-polyneuropathy complex. Medium-to-large-breed dogs; Labrador Retrievers particularly overrepresented. Treatment: arytenoid lateralization (improves breathing but together with esophageal dysfunction predisposes to aspiration).

### Ch 253 — Neuromuscular Junction Disorders
(Moore SA)

---

#### Tick Paralysis

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-NEU-TICKPARAL — enrichment: American vs Australian forms, treatment distinctions

**Mechanism:** Neurotoxin from salivary glands of engorged female ticks (Dermacentor sp. in North America, Ixodes sp. in Australia) — interferes with ACh release from presynaptic terminal via calcium-mediated mechanism.

**American tick paralysis (Dermacentor sp.):**
- Dogs commonly affected; cats relatively resistant.
- Clinical signs begin **5–9 days after tick attachment** — acute, rapidly progressive ascending flaccid paresis → tetraplegia over **12–72 hours**.
- Segmental spinal reflexes severely diminished all four limbs; decreased muscle tone.
- Cranial nerve deficits uncommon; autonomic, sensory, and sphincter abnormalities do NOT occur.
- Respiratory paralysis may require mechanical ventilation.
- **Tick removal → rapid improvement in signs, usually within hours.**

**Australian tick paralysis (Ixodes holocyclus):**
- Cats AND dogs affected.
- More severe; additional signs: autonomic dysfunction, micturition disorders, congestive heart failure (diastolic dysfunction), pulmonary edema, aspiration pneumonia, hypoventilation.
- Pupillary dilation common in cats and often in advanced canine cases.
- Unilateral cranial nerve deficits and Horner's syndrome reported.
- **Clinical condition may continue to decline for several days AFTER tick removal** (unlike American form).
- Treatment: tick removal + supportive care. **Phenoxybenzamine and acepromazine** for autonomic signs in Australian form.
- **Glucocorticoids NOT routinely used; antibiotic therapy not helpful.**

---

#### Botulism

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — important LMN disease differential

**Pathogenesis:** Acute diffuse LMN disease from Clostridium botulinum neurotoxins. Ingestion of preformed toxin in spoiled/uncooked meat; rarely toxico-infection. **Botulinum neurotoxin type-C (BoNT-C) most commonly associated with disease in dogs.** Naturally occurring botulism extremely rare in cats. Mechanism: irreversible enzymatic cleavage of SNARE proteins → blocks ACh release at presynaptic terminals of skeletal muscle and cholinergic autonomic synapses.

**Clinical signs:** Acute progressive tetraparesis with autonomic signs (ileus, tachy- or bradycardia, mydriasis, urinary retention). Cranial nerve deficits: decreased palpebral reflex, megaesophagus, diminished pupillary light reflexes. Severity/progression varies with toxin quantity.

**Diagnosis:** Usually presumptive (history + clinical exam). Definitive: demonstration of BoNT in blood, feces, stomach contents, or food source. Routine bloodwork normal. Electrodiagnostics: **incremental increases in CMAP with high-rate stimulation (presynaptic NMJ disease pattern).**

**Distinguishing features vs tick paralysis and polyradiculoneuritis:**
- Botulism/tick paralysis: NMJ diseases (presynaptic); normal EMG spontaneous activity; abnormal incremental CMAP.
- Polyradiculoneuritis: PNS disease; early spontaneous activity on EMG; abnormal MNCV and F wave.

**Treatment:**
- Supportive (primary).
- Antibiotics generally NOT indicated (exception: suspected toxico-infection or secondary aspiration).
- **Aminoglycosides and ampicillin potentiate neuromuscular blockade — AVOID.**
- Antitoxin: most available products do not contain anti-BoNT-C antibodies — generally NOT useful for dogs. Test injection required if given (equine origin → anaphylaxis risk).
- **Prognosis: excellent if adequately supported.** Spontaneous recovery over **1–4 weeks.**

#### Elapid Snake Envenomation

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — geographically relevant for AU/Americas)

Mechanism: either postsynaptic nondepolarizing NMJ blockade (binds AChR) or presynaptic inhibition of ACh release. Signs within hours: flaccid tetraparesis/tetraplegia, hypotonia, reduced reflexes, hypoventilation; cranial nerve signs (ptyalism, dysphagia, dysphonia, facial paresis). **Hemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria in a substantial proportion of dogs — NOT a feature of other diffuse LMN diseases** (useful differentiator). Recovery in 1–2 weeks with supportive care. Tiger snake and Mexican coral snake antivenins cross-protective against North American coral snake venom.

#### Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

> ClinIQ: ✓ DIS-WK-MG — significant enrichment: diagnostic criteria, anti-AChR titer, pyridostigmine dosing, spontaneous remission rates

**Pathophysiology:** Acquired autoimmune disorder — autoantibodies against nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (AChR) on postsynaptic muscle terminal → destruction of AChR → reduced neuromuscular transmission → skeletal muscle weakness exacerbated by activity. Terminology clarification: "congenital MG" now renamed **Congenital Myasthenic Syndrome (CMS)** — MG reserved for autoimmune conditions only.

**Signalment:** Bimodal age distribution — **<4 years of age or >9 years of age.** Overrepresented dog breeds: Akita, German Shorthaired Pointer, Chihuahua, German Shepherd Dog, Golden Retriever. Familial predisposition: Newfoundland and Great Dane. Cats: Abyssinian and Somali overrepresented.

**Forms:**
1. **Generalized MG** (most common): Diffuse appendicular muscle weakness. Normal neuro exam at rest. Activity-induced weakness: choppy stilted gait first noted in pelvic limbs, then thoracic. Megaesophagus in **84% of dogs** and **40% of cats** with generalized MG. Ventroflexion of neck in both species (more common in cats).
2. **Focal MG:** Weakness of isolated muscle groups (ocular, facial, esophageal, pharyngeal, laryngeal). No generalized appendicular weakness. Presenting complaints: regurgitation, dysphagia, dysphonia, or strabismus.
3. **Fulminant MG:** Rapid onset severe diffuse weakness; non-ambulatory tetraparesis/lateral recumbency; frequent regurgitation and aspiration pneumonia common; respiratory muscle weakness; urinary retention. Thymoma strongly associated.

**Comorbidities:** Thymoma prevalence **3.4% in dogs and up to 52% in cats**. Also associated with hypothyroidism, hypoadrenocorticism, polymyositis, masticatory myositis.

**Diagnosis:**

*Anti-AChR autoantibody titer (gold standard):*
- **Species-specific anti-AChR autoantibodies detect approximately 98% of canine MG cases.**
- Antibody levels highly variable between patients; **do NOT correlate with clinical severity**.
- Within patients: decrease in antibodies correlates with clinical improvement — can be followed to assess remission.
- May be falsely negative early in disease or if corticosteroids administered prior to testing.
- **Retesting recommended in 1–2 months** if either scenario suspected.
- Small number of patients have true seronegative MG (autoantibodies against other NMJ proteins).

*Edrophonium (Tensilon) test / neostigmine:*
- Short-acting anticholinesterase; no longer available in some countries — neostigmine may substitute.
- Inhibits AChE → ACh accumulation in NMJ → improved neuromuscular transmission.
- Animals with MG often show improvement shortly after receiving drug (lasting minutes with edrophonium, hours with neostigmine).
- **NOT completely specific for MG** — some other NMJ diseases may also improve; not all MG animals respond.
- **Risk of cholinergic crisis** (worsened weakness, salivation, tremors, vomiting, bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, respiratory distress) — more common with neostigmine.
- **Atropine and equipment for intubation/respiratory support must be available.**

*Electrodiagnostics:* Repetitive nerve stimulation testing — decrement response. Supports diagnosis in seronegative cases.

*Thoracic radiography/CT:* Evaluate for thymoma and aspiration pneumonia.

**Treatment:**

*AChE inhibitors (mainstay of symptomatic therapy):*
- **Pyridostigmine bromide (oral):** First-line oral agent. Must be carefully titrated to effect. **Narrow therapeutic window.** Significant side-effects include bradycardia, hypersalivation, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle cramping, weakness.
- **Neostigmine (intramuscular):** Used only in hospitalized animals in acute crisis or profound regurgitation.
- Signs of excessive anticholinergic drug administration may be difficult to distinguish from uncontrolled MG — careful monitoring required at therapy initiation.

*Immunosuppressive therapy:*
- Goal: reduce autoantibody production against AChR.
- Agents used: **cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), azathioprine** (Ch 144, 169).
- MMF vs pyridostigmine alone: **no difference in survival time or remission rate** between groups in recent study.
- **Corticosteroids:** Effective but use with caution — may potentiate muscle weakness, exacerbate signs in some patients (gradual dose increase recommended to avoid this complication). Avoid in aspiration pneumonia.
- IVIG and therapeutic plasma exchange: supported by small case series and efficacy in other immune-mediated diseases; neither thoroughly evaluated in controlled veterinary study.

**Prognosis/Remission:**
- **Long-term spontaneous remission rate as high as 88% in dogs with MG.**
- **Spontaneous remission NOT a characteristic of feline MG.**

#### Congenital Myasthenic Syndrome (CMS)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — distinct from acquired MG)

AChR deficiency in most breeds (documented in Jack Russell Terriers, Springer Spaniels, Miniature Dachshunds). Clinical signs begin ~6–9 weeks of age: generalized limb and cervical muscle weakness worse with activity; megaesophagus variably present. **Anti-AChR autoantibodies NOT useful — definitive diagnosis requires muscle biopsy for AChR quantification.**

Treatment: pyridostigmine often produces improvement; long-term response variable. Exceptions with more favorable outcomes: CMS in Miniature Dachshunds (signs resolved by 6 months) and Gammel Dansk Honsehund dogs (stable with mild clinical signs).

---

#### Exercise-Induced Collapse (EIC) of Labrador Retrievers

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — common Labrador-specific condition)

Not NMJ disease; mentioned in context. Heritable condition associated with **autosomal recessively inherited mutation (Arg256Leu)** in gene encoding dynamin 1 (DNM1). First episode usually before 2 years of age. Ataxia, pelvic limb weakness, collapse during/after strenuous activity; thoracic limbs can progress. Recovery with rest within 30 minutes; severe episodes can result in death. Genetic test commercially available. **No cure; excellent prognosis if refrains from triggering activities.**

### Ch 254 — Autonomic Nervous System Disorders
(Harkin KR)

---

#### Dysautonomia

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate) — strong candidate for new entry

**Etiology/Pathogenesis:** Idiopathic neurodegenerative disorder of dogs, cats, horses, and rabbits. Peracute to subacute disease course. **Hallmark histology: chromatolytic degeneration of neurons in autonomic ganglia with minimal to no inflammation.** Similar changes in ventral horn of spinal cord and brainstem nuclei. Toxicoinfectious etiology suspected (possible Clostridium botulinum type C/D association).

**Signalment:** Generally affects young dogs and cats.

**Clinical signs:**
- Dilated and nonresponsive pupils; poor anal tone; vomiting/regurgitation; dysuria.
- **Heart rate: 90–120 bpm in dogs, 120–150 bpm in cats** — with characteristic **lack of heart rate variability** (rarely varies >1–2 bpm even with forced exercise).
- Dry mucous membranes (eyes, nose, oral cavity) — xerostomia, dry eyes → corneal ulceration risk.
- GI dysfunction: vomiting, regurgitation, megaesophagus, generalized ileus, focal distal ileal impaction, constipation, diarrhea, poor anal tone.
- **Unique: vomitus comes out through nose in addition to mouth** (failure of laryngeal/pharyngeal function).
- Urinary: dysuria with retention and poor sphincter/urethral tone (easy to express), OR small percentage with retention and increased tone (impedes expression).
- Weak LV systolic function — LV % fractional shortening can fall to single digits without compensatory tachycardia.
- Weakness may be from poor cardiac output or from diffuse LMN paralysis.

**Diagnostic algorithm (Figure 254.1):**
1. Confirm absence of heart rate variability: walk test in dogs.
2. Evaluate tear production: Schirmer tear test strips — **commonly <5 mm/min** (reference: dogs 21±4 mm/min; cats 9–34 mm/min [median 18 mm/min]).
3. **Pilocarpine testing (0.05%, 1–2 drops topically):** Normal — no response over 60 minutes. Dysautonomia — **miosis in <60 minutes** (most by 30–45 minutes). Not performed if miotic or if PLR present; must be performed BEFORE atropine testing.
4. **Atropine response test (0.06 mg/kg SC):** Normal — heart rate increase ≥40 bpm. Dysautonomia — **no change in HR.** Not performed if HR >140 bpm (dogs) or >160 bpm (cats), or if heart rate variability present.
5. Thoracic radiographs: assess for megaesophagus and aspiration pneumonia.
6. **If megaesophagus or aspiration pneumonia present: consider euthanasia** — survival is rare.
7. Echocardiogram: measure LV fractional shortening in dogs (not needed in cats — ventricular systolic dysfunction not demonstrated in feline dysautonomia).

**Supportive therapy (Table 254.2):**

| Problem | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Decreased LV systolic function on echo | **Pimobendan 0.3 mg/kg PO q12h** |
| Vomiting (in-hospital) | Maropitant 1–2 mg/kg SQ or IV q12–24h; metoclopramide 1–2 mg/kg/24h IV CRI (often combined) |
| Vomiting (at-home) | Metoclopramide 0.1–0.2 mg/kg PO q8h; clarithromycin 7.5–12.5 mg/kg PO q12h (often combined) |
| Recurrent distal ileal obstruction | Intestinal resection and anastomosis |
| Urinary retention with decreased sphincter tone | Manual expression 6–8 times/day or more |
| Urinary retention with increased sphincter tone | **Tamsulosin 25–75 mcg/kg PO q12–24h** + manual expression 6–8 times/day |
| Photophobia | Pilocarpine 0.05–0.1% OU in the morning; artificial tears |
| Nasal congestion | 1–2 mL of 0.9% saline in each nostril q6–8h |

- Recovery of voluntary urinary bladder function, appetite, and normal GI motility may take **2–3 months**.
- Bethanechol NOT recommended (owners may reduce manual expression frequency → chronic bladder distension → UTI).
- Long-term survival with minimal residual dysautonomia is possible in both dogs and cats.

### Ch 255 — Unique Feline Neurologic Disorders
(Beltran E, De Risio L)

---

#### Cerebellar Hypoplasia (Feline)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — common feline condition, distinct from canine presentation)

Caused by perinatal infection with feline panleukopenia virus (FPV) during last 3 weeks of gestation or first 3 weeks of life → destruction of external germinal layer → granloprival cerebellar hypoplasia. Vaccination with modified-live FPV vaccine during pregnancy is CONTRAINDICATED (causes same disease). Clinical signs: cerebellar ataxia, intention tremor, menace response deficits, nystagmus. **Clinical signs remain stable or improve** (compensation possible). No treatment; MRI may show cerebellar atrophy. Good quality of life possible.

---

#### Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP) — Neurologic Form

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate for feline neurologic FIP)

Up to 38% of FIP cases show neurological signs. Non-effusive (dry) form predominates in CNS involvement. Usually pedigree cats from multi-cat environments, typically <4 years of age. Three distinct CNS clinical syndromes: central vestibular syndrome, multifocal CNS disease, T3–L3 myelopathy.

**Diagnosis:** MRI findings: obstructive hydrocephalus, ventricular dilation, periventricular/ependymal/meningeal contrast enhancement. Up to 37% of histologically confirmed neurologic FIP has normal MRI. CSF: **marked neutrophilic to pyogranulomatous pleocytosis (nucleated cell count >100 cells/L) and protein >200 mg/dL (>2 g/L)** — high viscosity may impair collection. RT-PCR for FCoV RNA and immunostaining for FCoV antigen in CSF: useful antemortem support for diagnosis.

**Treatment/Prognosis:** Currently inevitably fatal. Corticosteroids provide palliative relief; some cats survive only days to months. Antiviral compounds (GS-441524) show promising results in non-neurologic FIP; neurologic FIP was excluded from major trials; one report of positive response in 4 cats with naturally occurring neurologic FIP.

#### Feline Ischemic Myelopathy

Higher prevalence of acute vascular lesions in cranial cervical spinal cord of cats — possibly associated with hyaline degeneration of ventral spinal artery itself associated with systemic hypertension.

#### Feline Hippocampal Necrosis (FHN) and Feline Limbic Encephalitis (FLE)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — unique feline neuroimmunological entity)

FHN associated with different causes including FLE (VGKC/LGI1 antibodies), neurotoxicosis, and structural brain disorders. Hippocampus susceptible to epileptic seizures and very epileptogenic. Cats: interictal behavioral changes (vocalization, aggression, anxiety) + recurrent focal seizures with orofacial automatism (hypersalivation, lip smacking, facial twitching, chewing, licking, swallowing) → generalized tonic-clonic. MRI: bilateral hippocampal hyperintensity (T2), hypointensity (T1), marked enhancement post-contrast. Testing CSF and serum for LGI1 and VGKC antibodies indicated but not widely available. Treatment: antiepileptic medications + immunosuppressive therapy. Prognosis guarded.

---

#### Feline Orofacial Pain Syndrome (FOPS)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate)

Pain disorder with oral discomfort and tongue mutilation. High prevalence in Burmese cats (inherited component). Acute-severe form: young kittens at permanent teeth eruption — self-limiting within weeks. Chronic-episodic form: adult cats with paroxysmal orofacial pain, often triggered by oral movements; periodontal disease is predisposing factor. Treatment: analgesia, gabapentin, phenobarbital, mitigating environmental stressors, treating dental triggers. Dental intervention may worsen FOPS — refer to veterinary dentistry specialist.

---

#### Feline Hyperesthesia Syndrome (FHS)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate)

Poorly understood disorder: episodes of cutaneous trunci muscle contraction (skin rippling over lumbar area), excessive vocalization, overgrooming/self-trauma to tail. May be triggered by petting; occurs when anxious or stressed. More common in Siamese, Burmese, Himalayan, Abyssinian cats; onset typically 1–4 years. Diagnosis by exclusion (dermatologic, spinal, renal disorders). Treatment: multimodal including gabapentin and phenobarbital or topiramate + environmental stressor management.

### Ch 256 — Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation
(Levine D, Millis D)

---

> ClinIQ: Cross-reference from DM, IVDD, FCE, polyradiculoneuritis — most relevant content is the DM physical therapy survival data (already cited in Ch 250)

**Key modalities:**
- **Cryotherapy:** After acute tissue trauma; reduce inflammation, swelling, pain.
- **Thermotherapy (superficial heat):** Chronic conditions — increase connective tissue extensibility; decrease pain.
- **Therapeutic Ultrasound (TUS):** Deep heating (up to 5 cm). 1 MHz for 3–5 cm depth; 3.3 MHz for 1–2 cm depth.
- **Electrical Stimulation (ES):** Muscle strengthening; pain reduction via sensory nerve stimulation. Most dogs tolerate well.
- **Photobiomodulation (PBM)/Therapeutic Laser:** Modulates mitochondrial respiration, ATP synthesis; wound/joint healing; muscle regeneration; pain control. Class IIIb (5–500 mW) or Class IV (>500 mW).
- **Extracorporeal Shockwave Therapy (ESWT):** High-intensity sound waves; indications include osteoarthritis, delayed union fractures, post-op TPLO recovery, medial shoulder instability, biceps/supraspinatus tendinitis.
- **Aquatic Therapy (UWTM):** Underwater treadmill and swimming; core to neurological rehabilitation. NOT appropriate to use passive range-of-motion exercises in patients with spinal cord disease (adverse effects; see Ch 251 FCE section).
- **Therapeutic Exercise:** Most common and valuable modality — improve active ROM, flexibility, muscle mass/strength, balance, endurance.

**Neurological rehabilitation key principle:** For FCE and other spinal cord diseases, passive ROM exercises may be contraindicated; walking assistance and underwater treadmill preferred.

**Degenerative Myelopathy:** Physical therapy the ONLY treatment shown to alter long-term disease course. See Ch 250 for survival data.
