### Ch 266 — Pathophysiology of Liver Disease (Gow)

#### Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE)

**Definition / Grade Scale**
HE is a clinical diagnosis based on hepatic disease + compatible neurological signs with other causes excluded. Grade scale (Veterinary modification of West Haven):
- Grade 0: Asymptomatic
- Grade I: Mild decrease in mobility, apathy or both
- Grade II: Severe apathy, mild ataxia
- Grade III: Combination of hypersalivation, severe ataxia, head pressing, blindness, circling
- Grade IV: Stupor/coma, seizures

Hyperammonemia reinforces suspicion but absence does not exclude HE. No laboratory test accurately predicts HE severity. (Ch 266)

**HE triggers (dogs/cats):** ammonia (GI origin — glutamine catabolism, bacterial urease), systemic inflammation, Mn excess, hypokalemia (esp. cats), alkalosis, hyponatremia, high-protein meals, GI hemorrhage, infection/UTI.

**Management of HE Crisis:**
- Avoid lactated Ringer's (lactate requires hepatic metabolism)
- Warm isotonic cleansing enemas to remove urease bacteria
- After colonic evacuation: retention enema with lactulose 1:3 parts water, 10 mL/kg via Foley, retain 30–60 min, repeat as needed
- Correct hypokalemia, dehydration; IV fluids to enhance renal ammonia excretion
- Treat infection/UTI as precipitant

**HE Drug Doses (Table 266.3):**
| Drug | Protocol | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lactulose PO | 0.5 mL/kg q8–12h; titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day | Osmotic diarrhea if overdosed |
| Lactulose enema | 1 part lactulose:3 parts warm water; 10 mL/kg, Foley, retain 30–60 min | After colonic evacuation |
| Lactitol PO | 0.5 mL/kg q12h; titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day | |
| Lactose (milk/yoghurt) | Empirical to effect 2–3 soft stools/day | Suggested in cat; lactase deficiency prevalence unknown |
| Neomycin | 20 mg/kg PO q12h | Oto- and nephrotoxicosis if absorbed |
| Amoxicillin | 10 mg/kg PO q8–12h | |
| Potentiated amoxicillin | 12.5–25 mg/kg PO q12h | Potential hypersensitivity, diarrhea |
| Ampicillin | 10–20 mg/kg PO q6–8h | |
| Metronidazole | 7.5 mg/kg PO q8–12h | Half standard dose — reduced 50% due to hepatic metabolism |
| Rifaximin | 5 mg/kg PO q12–24h | Nonabsorbable; expense limits use; limited anecdotal use in dogs with HE |

**Nutrition for HE (chronic):**
- Protein restriction no longer recommended long-term (causes muscle catabolism → worsens ammonia buffering)
- Target: 18–22% protein DM (dogs), 30–35% protein DM (cats); dairy or vegetable proteins preferred over meat
- Cats have higher protein requirements; cats with lipidosis require high-protein diets

**Portal Hypertension (PH) Management:**
- Spironolactone: 1–2 mg/kg PO q24h (aldosterone antagonist; full effect 1–2 weeks; also antifibrotic)
- Furosemide: 1–2 mg/kg PO q12h if spironolactone insufficient or rapid reduction needed
- Goal: reduce body weight 0.5–1.5% per day; monitor daily weight, abdominal girth, PCV, total solids, creatinine, electrolytes

**Jaundice thresholds:**
- Plasma: detectable >1 mg/dL (>17 µmol/L) bilirubin
- Sclera: >1.5 mg/dL (>26 µmol/L)
- Mucosa: >2.0–2.5 mg/dL (>34–43 µmol/L)

**Coagulation — Biopsy Risk (ACVIM consensus):**
High risk for biopsy hemorrhage: platelets <50,000/µL; PT or aPTT >1.5× ULN; fibrinogen <100 mg/dL (<1 g/L); vWF activity <50%; BMBT >5 min; PCV <30%

**Portal Vein Thrombosis treatment:** Heparin or warfarin for 6 months in acute PVT. Low-molecular-weight heparin and fondaparinux are standard of care in humans. (Ch 266)

### Ch 267 — Congenital Vascular Liver Diseases (PSS, MVD/PVH) (Weisse & Berent)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-HEP-PSS (Portosystemic Shunt) — see enrichments below

**Classification:**
1. Macrovascular PSS — intrahepatic (IHPSS, ~25–33% in dogs) or extrahepatic (EHPSS, 66–75%)
2. Primary Portal Vein Hypoplasia (PVH):
   - With PH (= NCPH, noncirrhotic portal hypertension) — Doberman overrepresented (27%)
   - Without PH (formerly MVD) — microangiopathy
3. Hepatic Arteriovenous Malformations (HAVM) — rare; portal hypertension, ascites in 75%

**Congenital PSS prevalence:** 0.18% all dogs; 0.05% mixed-breed dogs. Cats: EHPSS more common. (Ch 267)

**Breed predispositions:**
- EHPSS small breeds: Yorkshire Terrier (OR >35 vs all others), Maltese, Miniature Schnauzer, Pug, Havanese, Dandie Dinmont
- IHPSS large breeds: Irish Wolfhound, Labrador, Golden Retriever, Australian Cattle Dog
- NCPH: Doberman Pinschers (27% of NCPH cases), most <4 yrs, >10 kg

**Protein C for differentiating MVD from PSS:**
Plasma protein C >70% in 88% of dogs with MVD (PVH without PH); low in dogs with macroscopic PSS. (Ch 267)

**SBA (Serum Bile Acids):**
- 12-hour fasting + 2-hour postprandial SBA = test of choice for PSS evaluation
- Postprandial SBAs 100% sensitive for PSS in dogs and cats (paired samples)
- SBAs unreliable when there is cholestatic jaundice → use ammonia instead
- False-low SBAs: delayed intestinal absorption, lack of GB contraction, malabsorption, delayed gastric emptying
- SBA can remain elevated after shunt attenuation and should not be used solely to assess surgical outcome (Ch 267)

**Blood Ammonia:**
- Less sensitive than SBAs (62–88% of PSS dogs abnormal at baseline fasting)
- 6-hr postprandial ammonia more sensitive (91%) than fasting (62%) in dogs with PSS
- ATT + fasting SBAs combined: highest specificity (97%), PPV 97% for PSS diagnosis
- Plasma must be separated and analyzed within 20 minutes of collection (Ch 267)

**Scintigraphy shunt fractions:**
- Normal: <15% of portal blood bypasses liver
- PSS: usually >60–80% shunt fraction; some cats lower (52% had lower fractions)

**Medical Management (Table 267.1):**
| Disorder | Therapy |
|---|---|
| Bacterial byproduct reduction | Cleansing enemas: warm water or 30% lactulose at 5–10 mL/kg |
| | Oral lactulose: 0.5–1 mL/kg PO q6–8h to produce 2–3 soft stools/day |
| | Metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg IV or PO q12h |
| | Ampicillin 22 mg/kg IV q6h |
| | Neomycin 22 mg/kg PO q8h (avoid if GI bleeding, ulcerations, or kidney disease) |
| Coagulopathy (with bleeding) | Fresh frozen plasma 10–15 mL/kg over 2–3h |
| | Vitamin K1 1.5–2 mg/kg SC or IM q12h × 3 doses, then q24h |
| GI ulceration | Famotidine 0.5–1 mg/kg IV or PO q12h |
| | Omeprazole 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q12–24h |
| | Esomeprazole 0.5 mg/kg IV q12–24h |
| | Misoprostol 2–3 mcg/kg PO q12h |
| | Sucralfate 1 g/25 kg PO q8h |
| Seizure control | Avoid benzodiazepines* |
| | Phenobarbital 4 mg/kg IV q3–6h × 4 doses |
| | KBr (avoid in cats due to bronchospasm): loading 400–600 mg/kg/day divided over 1–5 days PO with food; maintenance 20–30 mg/kg PO q24h |
| | Propofol 1–3.5 mg/kg IV bolus, then CRI 0.01–0.25 mg/kg/min* |
| | Levetiracetam 20 mg/kg (up to 60 mg/kg) PO or IV q8h (*no evidence-based support) |
| Cerebral edema | Mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg slow IV bolus over 20–30 min |
| | Flumazenil 0.02 mg/kg IV to effect (benzodiazepine antagonist for HE-induced coma) |
| Nutritional support | Normal (not high) protein: 18–22% for dogs, 30–35% for cats (DM basis); dairy/vegetable proteins preferred |
| | Vitamin B complex 1 mL/L IV fluid therapy |
| Hepatoprotective therapy (chronic/uncorrectable) | SAMe 17–22 mg/kg/day PO |
| | Ursodeoxycholic acid 10–15 mg/kg/day PO |
| | Vitamin E 15 IU/kg/day PO |
| | Silymarin (milk thistle) 3–7 mg/kg PO q8h |
| | L-carnitine 250–500 mg/day PO (cats) |

*Controversial.

**Pre-operative seizure prophylaxis:** Levetiracetam 20 mg/kg PO q8h for ≥24h preoperatively. Evidence that it prevents post-attenuation seizures is conflicting (not clearly confirmed in more recent studies). (Ch 267)

**Post-attenuation seizure management:** Propofol 1–3.5 mg/kg IV bolus then CRI 0.1–0.2 mg/kg/min (or higher). Acepromazine 0.005–0.01 mg/kg IV (low dose) has been used for postprocedural seizures (contrary to earlier concerns re: seizure threshold). (Ch 267)

**Surgical outcomes for PSS:**
- EHPSS with ameroid constrictor: complications 7–20%; mortality 0–17%; good-excellent outcomes up to 94%; estimated MST 152 months
- EHPSS with cellophane band: complication rate ~10–13%; mortality 3–9%; good-excellent ~84%
- IHPSS surgical: complication 29–77%; mortality 6–23% suture ligation; 0–9% ameroid constrictor
- Medical management only: >50% euthanized within 10 months; ~33% survive long-term (MST 56.9 months in survivors)

**Medical-only prognosis:** Dogs medically managed with cPSS — 24/27 (88%) died or euthanized vs 21/97 (21.6%) surgically treated in one prospective study. Surgical/interventional attenuation recommended when possible. (Ch 267)

### Ch 268 — Congenital Ductal Plate Abnormalities (Kortum)

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Congenital Hepatic Fibrosis / Ductal Plate Malformation / Caroli Syndrome (candidate)
> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Polycystic Liver Disease (candidate)

**Spectrum of Ductal Plate Malformations (DPMs):**
- Choledochal cysts (extrahepatic/large ducts) — reported mainly in cats; most are fusiform type I, up to 10 cm
- Caroli disease/syndrome (large intrahepatic ducts) — characterized by segmental dilation of large intrahepatic bile ducts; "central dot sign" on CT pathognomonic in people; described in dogs and cats age 6 months–6 years
- Polycystic Liver Disease (PLD) — medium intrahepatic ducts; PKD1 mutation in Persian cats (36–49% ADPKD prevalence); PLD in <10% of cats with ADPKD
- Congenital Hepatic Fibrosis (CHF) — small intrahepatic ducts; progressive periportal-to-bridging fibrosis with portal vein hypoplasia; portal hypertension common; reported in dogs (Boxers overrepresented in one study with DPM spectrum), cats 2 months–2 years; median survival in Boxers: 7.5–8.8 years
- Von Meyenburg Complexes (VMC) — benign biliary hamartomas; incidental in dogs/cats; no treatment needed; US monitoring considered for rare malignant transformation

**Choledochal cyst treatment (cats):** Surgical excision ± biliary stenting; medical management with antibiotics, hepatic antioxidants, choleretics for poor surgical candidates. Prognosis guarded due to recurrent biliary infection risk. (Ch 268)

**Caroli/CHF treatment:** Ursodeoxycholic acid + antibiotics to manage cholangitis/cholelithiasis. Value of hepatic antioxidants/choleretics is unknown in animals. Portal hypertension managed per standard protocols. Liver transplantation in humans with advanced CHF. (Ch 268)

### Ch 269 — Acute Toxic and Other Parenchymal Liver Disease (McCallum)

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Acute Toxic Hepatopathy (candidate: distinct from chronic hepatitis entry)

**ALF definition (veterinary):**
Acute clinical signs of severe hepatocellular injury + hyperbilirubinemia + coagulopathy (PT >1.5× upper reference interval). Encephalopathy not required for diagnosis in dogs/cats. (Ch 269)

**Common causes by category (Table 269.1):**
- Drugs: phenobarbital, zonisamide, carprofen, NSAIDs, acetaminophen, amoxicillin-clavulanate, sulfonamides, lomustine, azathioprine, ketoconazole/azoles, amiodarone, diazepam (cats), methimazole (cats), tetracyclines
- Toxins: xylitol (dogs), aflatoxins, cycad palm (cycasin), blue-green algae (microcystins), Amanita phalloides, heavy metals, copper
- Infectious: Leptospira spp., CAV-1, Toxoplasma (cats)
- Neoplasia: lymphoma (most common), histiocytic sarcoma, massive HCC, round cell tumors
- Metabolic: hepatic lipidosis (cats), acute copper toxicosis (dogs)

**ALT half-life:** ~40–61 hours in dog; ~3.5 hours in cat. >50% decrease within 1 half-life = good prognostic indicator. (Ch 269)

**Acetaminophen toxicosis:**
- Dogs: hepatotoxicosis >100 mg/kg; methemoglobinemia >200 mg/kg
- Cats: no safe dose (absent UGT1A6 glucuronidation enzyme)
- Treatment: NAC (replenishes GSH), SAMe, ascorbic acid 30 mg/kg SQ/IV q6–8h for methemoglobinemia in dogs. Decontamination: emesis + activated charcoal within 1–4 hours. NAC effective up to 48 hours after ingestion. (Ch 269)

**Azathioprine hepatotoxicosis:** Reversible dose-dependent DILI in 15% of dogs after median 2 weeks; monitor biochemistries first 4 weeks. GSH depletion potentiates — consider SAMe or NAC supplementation. German Shepherd Dogs overrepresented. (Ch 269)

**Lomustine hepatotoxicosis:** Increased liver enzymes in up to 86% at therapeutic doses; clinical hepatotoxicosis only 3–6%; after median 4 doses, 11 weeks. Combination of SAMe + silymarin shown to decrease incidence and severity — administer concurrently. (Ch 269)

**Amiodarone:** Dose-dependent toxicosis in up to 45% of dogs after 6 days to 8 months. Resolves with drug discontinuation. (Ch 269)

**Amanita phalloides treatment:** Penicillin and silymarin (antidotes; inhibit hepatocyte amatoxin uptake). Silymarin 50 mg/kg IV at 5 hours and 24 hours after intoxication was shown to prevent hepatic injury experimentally in dogs. Survival ~26% in one case series. (Ch 269)

**Sulfonamide hepatotoxicosis:** Develops 5–36 days (mean 12 days) after exposure. Hepatopathy associated with decreased survival (46% recovery) vs sulfonamide hypersensitivity without hepatopathy (89% recovery). Discontinuation usually leads to resolution. (Ch 269)

**Xylitol:** Dogs >100 mg/kg → acute hypoglycemia + hepatotoxicosis. Cats: appear not to develop hepatotoxicosis experimentally. Treatment: dextrose even if not yet hypoglycemic (hepatoprotective); SAMe + NAC. (Ch 269)

**Blue-green algae (microcystins):** Cholestyramine may bind microcystins and disrupt enterohepatic recirculation. (Ch 269)

**General ALF Supportive Care:**
- IV fluids: 0.9% saline preferred over lactated Ringer's (lactate requires hepatic metabolism, may worsen lactic acidosis and cerebral hyperemia)
- Hypotension: norepinephrine, vasopressin (vasopressors); hydrocortisone for CIRCI
- Hypoglycemia: dextrose supplementation
- Antiemetic: maropitant 0.5 mg/kg SQ q24h
- Vitamin K1 prophylactic: 0.5–1.5 mg/kg IM/SQ q12h × 3 days if cholestasis or bleeding tendency
- FFP 5–15 mL/kg if active hemorrhage or invasive procedure planned
- Mannitol for ICH: 0.5–1 g/kg IV over 20 minutes
- Propofol for seizures and ICH reduction (short half-life, anticonvulsant properties)

**Hepatoprotectants for ALD:**
- NAC: 140 mg/kg IV once, then 70 mg/kg IV q6–8h (thiol donor, GSH replenishment, free radical scavenger; IV preferred — no GI side effects; effective even for non-acetaminophen toxicosis)
- UDCA: 10–15 mg/kg PO q24h (cytoprotective; treats cholestasis)
- Silymarin: 10 mg/kg PO q24h with phosphatidylcholine (hepatoprotective, antifibrotic; flavonoid from milk thistle)
- SAMe: 20 mg/kg PO q24h (augments hepatocyte GSH; improves membrane fluidity)
- Vitamin E: 10–15 IU/kg PO q24h (antioxidant; inhibits lipid peroxidation)

**Leptospirosis treatment:** Parenteral potentiated amoxicillin during initial hospitalization + 2-week course of doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q24h to eliminate renal carrier state. (Ch 269)

**ALF Prognosis — negative prognostic indicators (dogs):**
Hyperbilirubinemia, hypoalbuminemia, hypocholesterolemia, hypoglycemia, elevated PT + aPTT, decreased protein C + antithrombin, thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, ascites, granular cylindruria. Hyperphosphatemia (poor regeneration) negative; hypophosphatemia (intracellular shift with regeneration) positive. (Ch 269)

### Ch 270 — Chronic Hepatitis in Dogs (Lidbury)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-HEP-CHRONHEP — see copper thresholds and dose enrichments below

**Definition:** Histologically: hepatocellular apoptosis/necrosis + mononuclear or mixed inflammatory infiltrate + regeneration + fibrosis. (Ch 270)

**Prevalence:** CH identified in 12% of all dogs necropsied for any reason (UK study). Median age 7.2 years (range very wide). Copper-associated CH accounts for about one-third of primary hepatitis cases. (Ch 270)

**Breed predispositions:** American Cocker Spaniel, Cairn Terrier, Dalmatian, Doberman Pinscher, English Cocker Spaniel, English Springer Spaniel, Great Dane, Labrador Retriever, Samoyed. (Ch 270)

**Copper Thresholds for Chelation (ACVIM consensus):**
- **Chelation indicated:** hepatic copper >1000 mcg/g dry weight
- **Chelation considered:** hepatic copper 600–1000 mcg/g dry weight, depending on presence, grade, and localization of histologic copper staining
- Histologic copper score ≥3/5 typically supports chelation initiation
- Normal reference: copper accumulation not significant if histologic copper score <3/5
- Copper quantification by atomic absorption spectroscopy (expressed on dry weight basis) or inductively-coupled plasma mass spectrometry (wet weight, then converted)
- Regenerative nodules contain less copper than surrounding liver — contribute to sampling discordance (Ch 270)

**Chelating Agents — Doses:**
- **D-penicillamine (drug of choice):** 10–15 mg/kg PO q12h before feeding. Common GI side effects (vomiting, diarrhea, anorexia) — give with small food, antiemetic, or reduce dose temporarily. Rare: glomerulopathy, dermatological lesions → discontinue. Not licensed for small animals.
- **Trientine (if D-penicillamine not tolerated):** 10–15 mg/kg PO q12h before feeding. Less published experience; not recommended in most recent ACVIM consensus statement.
- **Ammonium tetrathiomolybdate:** reduces hepatic copper in dogs; appears well-tolerated; further studies needed before routine use.
- **Zinc acetate (after cessation of D-penicillamine):** 5–10 mg/kg PO q12h — decreases copper absorption. Monitor plasma zinc to avoid toxicity.
- **Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine):** 10–25 mg per dog PO q24h concurrent with D-penicillamine (as used for Wilson's disease in humans). D-penicillamine and zinc should not be given simultaneously. (Ch 270)

**Chelation duration:** Usually months; 6–10 months adequate for most Labrador Retrievers (considerable interindividual variation). Bedlington Terriers may need lifelong therapy. Monitor ALT monthly; treat ≥1 month after ALT returns to normal; then consider repeat biopsy with copper quantification. (Ch 270)

**Diet:** Copper-restricted commercial liver support diet during treatment. Continued throughout chelation. (Ch 270)

**Immune-mediated CH — Immunomodulatory Therapy:**
- **Prednisolone:** 1–2 mg/kg/day PO, then gradually tapered. High doses cause vacuolar hepatopathy and other adverse effects. ALT may improve but often doesn't fully normalize.
- **Modified cyclosporine:** 5–10 mg/kg/day PO; retrospective study of 48 dogs showed 79% remission rate (ALT return to normal); median dose at remission 7.9 mg/kg/day; median time to remission 2.5 months. GI signs 38%, gingival hyperplasia 25%.
- **Mycophenolate mofetil:** 10 mg/kg PO q12h
- **Azathioprine:** 2 mg/kg PO q48h (Ch 270)

**Cytoprotective Agents (Table 270.2):**
| Agent | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SAMe (S-adenosylmethionine) | 20 mg/kg PO q24h | Give ≥1h before feeding; antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, modulates apoptosis |
| Silymarin | 3–6 mg/kg PO q24h combined with phosphatidylcholine | Phosphatidylcholine increases absorption; antioxidant, immunomodulator, antifibrotic, choleretic |
| Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol acetate) | 10–15 IU/kg PO q24h | Antioxidant; give with food |
| Ursodeoxycholic acid | 10–15 mg/kg PO q24h | Choleretic, displaces hydrophobic bile acids, antiapoptotic; indicated in cholestasis or concurrent biliary infection |

**Managing HE in chronic CH:**
- Lactulose: 1–3 mL/10 kg PO q6–8h; adjust to produce 3–4 soft stools/day
- Metronidazole: 7.5 mg/kg PO q8–12h (lower dosage due to hepatic metabolism)
- Ampicillin / amoxicillin / amoxicillin-clavulanate: standard doses (Ch 270)

**Managing Ascites in CH:**
- Avoid high sodium diets
- Spironolactone: 1 mg/kg PO q24h (initial); increase incrementally to 2 mg/kg q12h if needed
- Furosemide: 1 mg/kg PO q12h (add if spironolactone insufficient)
- Monitor body weight, girth, electrolytes, renal values, serum protein
- Paracentesis if dyspnea or discomfort (Ch 270)

**Managing GI signs:**
- Omeprazole (PPI): 1 mg/kg PO q12h
- Maropitant: 1 mg/kg IV/SQ/PO (antiemetic)
- Ondansetron: 0.2 mg/kg IV q12h or 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q12h (Ch 270)

**Prognosis CH:**
Mean survival 561 days in 364 dogs (ACVIM consensus data). Negative prognostic factors: ascites, advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis, prolonged bleeding times, jaundice (hyperbilirubinemia). Lobular dissecting hepatitis: median survival 0.7 months (very poor). Copper-associated CH: excellent prognosis with early chelation. (Ch 270)

### Ch 271 — Chronic Hepatic Diseases in Cats (Boland & Beatty)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-HEP-CHOLANGITIS (Cholangitis/Cholangiohepatitis Cat) — see enrichments

#### Lymphocytic Cholangitis (Cat)

**Definition/Prevalence:** Chronic progressive immune-mediated inflammatory process centered on biliary tree. Accounts for 6.8–20% of feline liver biopsy diagnoses. Probably immune-mediated; bacterial infection suggested as trigger but not confirmed in most cats. (Ch 271)

**Clinical signs:** Gradual weight loss, polyphagia or anorexia, vomiting, lethargy, PU/PD over months to years. Often bright on PE. Jaundice, hepatomegaly, ascites may be present. (Ch 271)

**Labs:** Marked hyperglobulinemia + modest ALT/ALP elevation. Ascites: high protein, increased globulins, small lymphocytes, non-degenerate neutrophils — can mimic FIP. Normal US does not exclude lymphocytic cholangitis. (Ch 271)

**Diagnosis:** Liver biopsy required. Histology: infiltrating aggregates of small lymphocytes into portal region + portal fibrosis + biliary ductular proliferation. Differentiated from lymphoma by: bile duct targeting, ductopenia, peribildary fibrosis, portal B-cell aggregates, portal lipogranulomas, polyclonal T-cell receptor rearrangements. (Ch 271)

**Treatment:**
- Immunosuppressive prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg PO q24h (longer survival than ursodeoxycholic acid alone in one study)
- Ursodeoxycholic acid 10–15 mg/kg PO q24h
- Therapeutic abdominocentesis as needed
- Antibiotics only if bacterial culture supports this
- Prognosis with treatment: good in some reports; median/mean survival 26–36 months. (Ch 271)

#### Hepatic Amyloidosis (Cat)

**Species/breed:** >90% Siamese and related breeds; familial; cats usually <5 years old. (Ch 271)

**Presentation:** Spontaneous hepatic rupture → acute hemoabdomen; episodic intra-abdominal hemorrhage; sudden death. Diagnosis: cytology or biopsy — apple-green birefringence on Congo-red staining under polarized light. (Ch 271)

**Treatment/prognosis:** Poor — survival days to weeks; rare cases months. Cage rest + vitamin K1 supplementation. Colchicine used but safety and efficacy uncertain in cats. (Ch 271)

#### Primary Copper-Associated Hepatopathy (PCH) in Cats

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Feline Primary Copper Hepatopathy (candidate)

**Copper threshold in cats:**
- All cats with PCH had hepatic copper >**700 mcg/g dry matter basis (DMB)**
- All cats without hepatobiliary disease had hepatic copper **<190 mcg/g DMB**
- Secondary copper hepatopathy: copper accumulates periportal (not centrilobular as in primary) (Ch 271)

**Treatment (cats with PCH):**
- Penicillamine 10–15 mg/kg PO q12h
- Elemental zinc 2–4 mg PO q12–24h
- Antioxidants: SAMe, silybin, vitamin E
- Copper-restricted diet if hepatic insufficiency indicated
- Penicillamine-induced hemolytic anemia reported in cats (discontinue if occurs)
- Survival for several years possible with reduced clinical signs and histopathological improvement; prognosis depends on early diagnosis (Ch 271)

#### Chronic Cholangitis Associated with Liver Flukes (Cat)

**Treatment:** Praziquantel — dosages used range from 20–40 mg/kg SQ, IM, or PO as a single dose, or q24h × 3–5 days. (No consensus on dosage/interval.) Concurrent bacterial cholangitis: treat with appropriate antibiotics based on culture. (Ch 271)

### Ch 272 — Infectious Biliary Tract and Gallbladder Disease (O'Neill)

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Infectious Cholecystitis / Neutrophilic Cholangitis as distinct entry (candidate)

**Neutrophilic cholangitis (NC):** Most common cholangitis subtype; occurs commonly in cats, less commonly in dogs (reflects anatomical differences in biliary tract). ~50% of cases have bactibilia confirmed (69% in one large recent case series). (Ch 272)

**Bacteria isolated:** E. coli, Enterococcus spp., Bacteroides spp., Clostridium spp. (enteric organisms — support ascending or portal hematogenous route). (Ch 272)

**Concurrent disease (cats):** Pancreatitis 50–90%, IBD 40–90%, both 30–50%. Dogs with NC commonly have concurrent cholecystitis. (Ch 272)

**US Normal Values:**
- Gallbladder wall: feline 1 mm, canine 3 mm (newer study suggests 1.3 mm may be more appropriate in dogs)
- Common bile duct diameter: 4 mm

**Cholecystocentesis complication rate:** 2–7% (dogs and cats); complications usually transient (mild bile leakage or hemorrhage). Rare: bile peritonitis, GB rupture, cardiopulmonary arrest. (Ch 272)

**Treatment of NC / CC:**
- **Antimicrobials:** amoxicillin-clavulanate ± metronidazole as initial broad-spectrum choice. Duration recommended: **6–8 weeks** (should be based on response to treatment, with US + liver enzyme + bilirubin monitoring). Culture and sensitivity from bile mandatory.
- Cholecystectomy: indicated for cholelithiasis; should be considered with severe or progressive GB changes; linked to improved survival in some chronic NC cases.
- UDCA + antioxidants (SAMe, silymarin, vitamin E): good theoretical arguments; no confirming data.
- Management of concurrent pancreatitis and IBD essential. (Ch 272)

**Prognosis NC:** Survival times of several years in dogs and cats discharged from hospital. Worst outcomes with biliary tract rupture and bile peritonitis. (Ch 272)

### Ch 273 — Non-infectious Biliary Tract & Gallbladder Disease (Jaffey)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-HEP-MUCOCELE (Gallbladder Mucocele/Biliary Obstruction) — see enrichments

#### Gallbladder Mucocele (GBM)

**Epidemiology:** Most common extrahepatic biliary disease in dogs. Median age 10 years (range 1.5–17). Breed predispositions: Shetland Sheepdog, Cocker Spaniel, Miniature Schnauzer, Border Terrier, Pomeranian, Beagle. Extremely rare in cats. (Ch 273)

**Concurrent conditions:** High incidence of endocrinopathies (hyperadrenocorticism, hypothyroidism), hyperlipidemia, hyperleptinemia, decreased vitamin D. Imidacloprid administration linked in Shetland Sheepdogs. (Ch 273)

**US Classification:**
Six types of GBMs based on ultrasonographic appearance (Figure 273.2):
- Type I: organized, gravity-independent, echogenic debris >30% of GB lumen
- Type II: echogenic debris with partial stellate strands adhered to GB wall
- Type III: stellate pattern
- Type IV: stellate + kiwi pattern combination
- Type V: kiwi pattern with echogenic debris
- Type VI: classic kiwi pattern (gravity-independent hyperechoic material + hypoechoic rim sign)
- Gallbladder rupture in ~20% of dogs with clinical signs due to GBM
- Lower GBM types (I–III) can be as clinically significant as higher types (Ch 273)

**Diagnosis:** Contrast-enhanced US reported 100% sensitivity and specificity for GB wall rupture (not widely available). CT superior to US for GB perforation in humans. (Ch 273)

**Medical Therapy:**
- Low-fat diet
- UDCA (ursodeoxycholic acid) — choleretic; reduces bile acid hydrophobicity
- SAMe — choleretic effects via GSH-mediated bile salt independent canalicular bile formation
- Broad-spectrum antimicrobials if infection suspected (~16% of culture-positive cases); not routine without evidence of infection
- Rigorous management of concurrent endocrinopathies (may cause GBM regression in some cases — 2 dogs with hypothyroidism had complete GBM resolution with medical therapy alone)
- If GBM fails to resolve within **2–4 months** of medical therapy + adequate endocrinopathy control → prophylactic cholecystectomy recommended
- If not pursuing surgery: serial US every **3–6 months** (Ch 273)

**Surgery:** Cholecystectomy recommended in dogs with biliary tract signs + moderate-to-severe clinicopathologic derangements or suspicion of EHBDO or GB wall necrosis. Delaying surgery risks rupture, bile peritonitis, sepsis, MODS, death. (Ch 273)

**Prognosis:**
- In-hospital mortality: ~20% (clinical GBM) vs <5% (subclinical)
- Negative prognostic indicators: higher US GBM type, elevated ALP, postoperative hypotension, increasing postoperative serum lactate, owner recognition of jaundice, concurrent hyperadrenocorticism, Pomeranian breed, increasing age, increasing bilirubin, presence of clinical signs or GB rupture (Ch 273)

**Hyperbilirubinemia as negative prognostic indicator in GBM:** Documented in one study. (Ch 266 cites this for gallbladder mucocele)

#### Cholelithiasis

**Prevalence dogs:** 0.97–13%. Usually pigment stones (calcium bilirubinate ± carbonate, phosphate, free fatty acids) vs cholesterol stones in humans. Most dogs/cats subclinical; complications include EHBDO, rupture, bile peritonitis, cholecystitis, cholangitis. (Ch 273)

**Surgery:** Cholecystectomy for cholecystolithiasis and choledocholithiasis with patent CBD. Biliary-enteric diversion if CBD patency cannot be established. Cholecystotomy to remove choleliths avoided (risk of recurrence and ongoing complications). (Ch 273)

**Medical (subclinical):** UDCA + SAMe + low-fat diet + manage underlying triggers; pigment stones unlikely to dissolve (one case report of successful resolution with UDCA). (Ch 273)

#### Biliary Neoplasia

**Dog:** Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma predominates; 94% of 65 dogs with intrahepatic biliary neoplasia had intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. Gallbladder neuroendocrine tumors and carcinomas less frequent. (Ch 273)

**Cat:** Bile duct adenomas >50% of all feline primary hepatobiliary tumors. Diffuse intraperitoneal metastasis and carcinomatosis in ~70% of cats with bile duct carcinomas. (Ch 273)

**Gallbladder neuroendocrine tumor prognosis (dogs):** Median survival 1358 days (range 432–1834 days) after cholecystectomy including those with metastasis. (Ch 273)

**Cholangiocarcinoma prognosis:** Biologically aggressive. Metastatic rates 90% in dogs; 80% cats. Survival after surgery for massive bile duct carcinoma: majority die within 6 months (local recurrence or metastasis). (Ch 273)

#### Destructive Cholangitis

**Prevalence:** Very rare in dogs (only 9 cases reported in 20 years); not yet reported in cats. Characterized by destruction and loss of interlobular bile ducts ≥50% of small portal tracts. Idiopathic or possibly drug reactions (sulphonamides, amoxicillin-clavulanate), viral infections, immune-mediated. Only known cause of intrahepatic cholestasis producing acholic feces in companion animals. (Ch 273)

**Treatment:** Discontinue all possible causal drugs. High-dose UDCA + immunosuppression (as used in humans) — used successfully in one dog case report. Recovery possible but limited information. (Ch 273)

### Ch 274 — Vacuolar Hepatopathies (Kuzi & Watson)

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Vacuolar/Steroid Hepatopathy (candidate)

#### Steroid Hepatopathy (SH) / Glycogen-Associated VH

**Causes:** Exogenous or endogenous glucocorticoids; also various acute/chronic diseases (chronic stress-induced hypercortisolemia). Vacuolation begins centrilobular (zone 3), becomes generalized when chronic. Considered benign but should prompt search for primary disease. (Ch 274)

**Labs:** Dramatic increases in ALP activity (most common finding); ALP increases > ALT. Diffusely hyperechoic liver or hypoechoic hepatic nodules <3 cm on US moderately predictive of VH. Cytology: clear well-delineated vacuoles (lipid) vs indistinct vacuoles (glycogen). (Ch 274)

#### Glycogen-like VH of Scottish Terriers

**Special considerations:** Female and male predisposed equally; median age at HCC development ≥8 years. HCC in 35% of Scottish Terriers with progressive vacuolar hepatopathy. Adrenal glands enlarged in only 26%. Gallbladder mucocele in 16%. NOT hyperA (cortisol assays usually normal); poor response to hyperA treatments (severe side-effects with mitotane/ketoconazole; trilostane ineffective). Current recommendation: SAMe supplementation (reduced glutathione in other VH) + regular US monitoring. (Ch 274)

#### Feline Hepatic Lipidosis (FHL)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-HEP-LIPIDOSIS — see enrichments

**Pathophysiology:** Anorexia >2–14 days → NEB → increased FFA mobilization (decreased LPL activity + increased hormone-sensitive lipase) → TAG accumulation in hepatocytes → intrahepatic cholestasis → liver failure. Cats have limited ability to adapt protein/fat metabolic pathways. L-carnitine and n-3 long-chain PUFA deficiencies contribute. (Ch 274)

**Signalment:** Median 7 years (range 1.5–16); possible female overrepresentation; neutered indoor cats on dry diets overrepresented. 50–95% have concurrent conditions (DM, pancreatitis, inflammatory hepatobiliary disease, GI disease, neoplasia). Up to 28% with no identifiable cause. (Ch 274)

**Clinical Signs/Labs:**
- ALP elevated in 100%; hyperbilirubinemia 97.2%
- GGT commonly within reference intervals (74.6%); increased GGT → search for primary hepatobiliary disease
- Hypertriglyceridemia 84.6%; mild-moderate ALT elevation 88.7%; CK elevation 48.2%
- Liver function abnormalities (hypoalbuminemia, hypocholesterolemia, hypoglycemia, decreased BUN) in 40%
- Coagulation abnormalities common (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, FDP, PIVKA); rarely clinically relevant bleeding
- Severe bleeding during biopsy: thrombocytopenia <80,000/mL or aPTT >1.5× ULN (Ch 274)

**Treatment:**

*Enteral Feeding (mainstay):*
- Early enteral feeding reduces mortality from up to 90% to ≤40%
- Temporary nasogastric tube initially → esophageal or gastric tube preferred for non-liquid diets and medication administration
- Nasogastric tube also permits gastric suctioning in cats with gastric ileus
- Return to voluntary food intake: 12–16 days or longer
- Avoid benzodiazepines in anesthetic regimens (may worsen HE)

*Diet:*
- High-protein, high-caloric-density diets
- L-carnitine supplementation: **250 mg/day PO** (lowers plasma FFA concentrations; may be insufficient in catabolic state)
- Start at 25–33% of RER, divided into frequent meals or infusion, gradually reaching RER over 3–4 days (prevents refeeding syndrome)
- "Illness factor" not recommended (may lead to overfeeding, hyperglycemia, GI and hepatic dysfunction)
- No proven benefit from other supplementations (taurine, arginine)

*Supportive:*
- Antiemetics: maropitant + ondansetron for nausea/vomiting
- Electrolyte monitoring and correction: hypokalemia → worsens HE; hypomagnesemia → refractory hypokalemia; hypophosphatemia → severe hemolysis
- Vitamin B12 (cobalamin): **250 mcg/cat q7 days SQ**
- Thiamine: **100 mg/day PO or SQ**
- Vitamin K1: **1 mg/kg SQ q24h × 3–5 days** (reduce bleeding risk)
- SAMe: **20 mg/kg PO q24h on empty stomach** (antioxidant)
- Vitamin E: **100 IU PO q24h** (antioxidant)
- Ursodeoxycholic acid: **unlikely beneficial and probably should be avoided** in FHL (Ch 274)

*HE in FHL cats:*
- Reduced-protein diet not recommended; instead reduce amount fed + lactulose + smaller frequent meals
- Prokinetics (ranitidine, cisapride) if ileus develops
- Dose corrections needed for drugs metabolized by liver (e.g., maropitant) (Ch 274)

**Prognosis FHL:**
Reported mortality rates 12–70%; 38% in one recent study. Negative prognostic markers at presentation: older age, low serum protein and/or cholesterol, elevated CK. During hospitalization: worsening hypoalbuminemia, hyperammonemia or hyperbilirubinemia, electrolyte depletion, hypotension, neurological signs, → death; decreased BHBA and bilirubin concentrations during hospitalization → survival. Median hospitalization 5 days (range 1–18). (Ch 274)

#### Superficial Necrolytic Dermatitis (SND, Hepatocutaneous Syndrome)

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — SND/Hepatocutaneous Syndrome (candidate)

Uncommon hepatopathy; most common in older small-breed dogs (median 8–10 years); associated with DM (25–40%), glucagonomas, or phenobarbital therapy. Characteristic "honeycomb" or "Swiss cheese" US appearance with variably sized hypoechoic nodules surrounded by hyperechoic borders. Treatment: identify/address primary cause; ample protein supplementation; zinc + essential fatty acids; weekly parenteral amino acid treatment. Poor prognosis; euthanasia often within 6 months. Avoid corticosteroids (risk of precipitating DM). (Ch 274)

### Ch 275 — Neoplasia of the Liver and Biliary Tree (Bexfield)

> **ClinIQ:** ✗ MISSING — Hepatic/Biliary Neoplasia (candidate)

**Prevalence:** Primary liver tumors 0.6–1.5% of all tumors in dogs; 1–3% (up to 7% of nonhematopoietic tumors) in cats. Metastatic tumors ~3× more frequent than primary in dogs. Benign more common in cats. Peak incidence 10–12 years. (Ch 275)

**Primary tumor type frequency (dogs):** HCC 77% of all primary hepatic tumors; bile duct tumors 9%; hepatic neuroendocrine tumors 3%.

**Primary tumor type frequency (cats):** Bile duct adenomas historically >50% (but recent immunohistochemistry-based study found no adenomas among 61 feline tumors). Hematopoietic tumors most common in UK (histopathology data).

**Nodular hyperplasia:** Common in 15–60% of older dogs; 1% of histopathology submissions in cats. Must be distinguished from malignancy — histology required.

**HCC morphology (dogs):** 53–83% massive; 16–25% nodular; up to 19% diffuse. Left lateral/medial lobes and caudate lobe involved in >2/3 of massive HCC. Metastatic rate: 0–37% (massive) vs 93–100% (nodular or diffuse). (Ch 275)

**Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP):** Increased in ~75% of dogs with HCC and ~55% with bile duct carcinomas. (Ch 275)

**Treatment:**
- Massive HCC: surgical lobectomy — gold standard. In 42 dogs with massive HCC: intraoperative mortality ~5%; complication rate ~30%; MST not reached after >1460 days follow-up. Conservative management MST only 270 days; ~15× more likely to die from tumor-related causes than surgically treated dogs.
- Local tumor recurrence: 0–13% following lobectomy with complete excision
- Single-agent gemcitabine: MST 983 days (massive), 113 days (diffuse)
- Drug-eluting bead transarterial chemoembolization (16 non-resectable dogs): stable disease or partial response in 85%; MST 337 days (range 22–1061 days)
- 3D conformal RT in 6 dogs with non-resectable massive HCC: partial response in 5/6; MST 567 days
- Bile duct adenomas (cats): lobectomy — excellent prognosis, no reports of recurrence
- Bile duct carcinoma (cats) massive, after surgical resection: majority die within 6 months (Ch 275)

### Ch 276 — Pancreatitis: Etiology, Pathogenesis, Pathophysiology (Spillmann)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-GI-PANCAT (Feline Pancreatitis), DIS-SEC-PAN-DOG (Acute Pancreatitis Dog) — background pathogenesis

**Acute Pancreatitis (AP) pathogenesis:** Intracellular trypsinogen activation initiated by pancreatic ischemia/hypoxia, oxidative stress, bacterial toxemia, or retrograde bile influx (common in cats due to shared bile duct/pancreatic duct entry into duodenum in majority of cats and subset of dogs). SPINK1 overwhelmed when >10% of trypsinogen is activated. Subsequent activation of all zymogen granule proenzymes → acinar cell apoptosis → necrosis via neutrophil aggregation → neutrophil plaques → capillary stasis + hemorrhage + acinar cell necrosis. (Ch 276)

**Risk factors for AP (dogs):** Breed predispositions (terriers, non-sporting breeds, Miniature Schnauzers); neutered dogs (male and female); obesity; hypertriglyceridemia; dietary indiscretion/high-fat meals; babesiosis; EHMO (anesthesia-associated hypotension); drugs (potentiated sulfonamides, azathioprine, phenobarbital/KBr).

**Risk factors for AP (cats):** Mostly idiopathic (no breed, sex, obesity, drug, or dietary association). Abdominal trauma; hypercalcemia; organophosphate intoxication (fenthion); GI foreign bodies; comorbidities (DM, IBD, hepatic lipidosis, cholangitis, nephritis, immune-mediated anemia). (Ch 276)

**Chronic Pancreatitis (CP) etiology:** AP, immune-mediated, largely idiopathic. Breed predispositions: Cavalier KCS, English Cocker Spaniel, Boxers, Collies. Autoimmune pancreatitis (IgG4 histology) reported in English Cocker Spaniels and other breeds. CP in cats: leading cause of EPI. CP in dogs: immune-mediated PAA (German Shepherd Dog) or chronic inflammation. (Ch 276)

### Ch 277 — Pancreatitis in Dogs: Diagnosis and Management (Xenoulis & Steiner)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-SEC-PAN-DOG (Acute Pancreatitis Dog) — see diagnostic and treatment enrichments

**Clinical signs:** Lethargy 88%, anorexia 86%, vomiting 83%, abdominal pain 59% (one recent study). Vomiting + abdominal pain combination suggestive but not pathognomonic. Prayer position (Video 277.1) = severe abdominal pain response.

**Diagnosis:**

*Pancreatic Lipase Immunoreactivity (PLI) — primary diagnostic test:*
- **Spec cPL:** reference interval 0–200 mcg/L; **≥400 mcg/L** = highly suggestive of pancreatitis
- Sensitivity: 72–78% clinical study; 91% for moderate-severe in recent necropsy study; 21% for mild/potentially clinically insignificant disease (older necropsy studies)
- Specificity: 81–100%; specifically 100% in dogs with normal pancreas at necropsy (two studies)
- **SNAP cPL:** 90–100% agreement with Spec cPL; sensitivity 91–94%, specificity 71–78%. Primary use: rule out pancreatitis (normal result makes pancreatitis very unlikely)
- Spec cPL NOT significantly affected by: experimental chronic renal failure, long-term prednisone administration, pancreatic FNA or biopsy
- Spec cPL can be used as objective marker for disease progression in hospitalized acute pancreatitis dogs (Ch 277)

*DGGR lipase assay:*
- Best agreement (kappa 0.80) with Spec cPL at cutoff >216 U/L in dogs (142-dog study)
- NOT specific for pancreatic lipase — DGGR is also hydrolyzed by lipoprotein lipase and hepatic lipase. Further studies needed before routine recommendation for pancreatitis diagnosis. (Ch 277)

*Serum cTLI:*
- Sensitivity for spontaneous pancreatitis only 36–47% (short half-life of trypsinogen/trypsin)
- Can be elevated with kidney disease (false positive). Not recommended as primary pancreatitis test. (Ch 277)

*Traditional serum amylase/lipase:* Not useful for diagnosis of spontaneous canine pancreatitis (low sensitivity and specificity — many non-pancreatic tissues synthesize these). (Ch 277)

*Abdominal Ultrasound:*
- Sensitivity: if one criterion applied (pancreatic enlargement or peripancreatic echogenicity change) = 89% sensitivity, 43% specificity
- If stringent criteria (pancreatic enlargement + hypoechoic areas of necrosis + increased echogenicity of peripancreatic fat): sensitivity 43%, specificity 92%
- May have 24–48 hour delay in imaging findings vs actual disease process — repeat US if first negative
- Key US findings: hypoechoic areas within pancreas, increased echogenicity of peripancreatic mesentery (fat necrosis), pancreatic enlargement/irregularity
- Also valuable for detecting complications (fluid accumulation, EHBDO) (Ch 277)

**Management — Severe Acute Pancreatitis:**

*Fluid and Electrolyte Therapy:*
- Early and adequate fluid administration is cornerstone of management
- Crystalloid fluids preferred; in humans, lactated Ringer's preferred over normal saline
- Avoid overhydration (risk of AKI and ARDS with aggressive therapy)
- Synthetic colloids indicated in severe hypoalbuminemia or hypotension
- FFP: dogs receiving FFP had worse outcome than those not receiving it (retrospective study) — value highly questionable
- Hypokalemia (most common electrolyte abnormality): monitor throughout; supplement IV fluids as needed
- Correct other electrolyte and acid-base disturbances (Ch 277)

*Nutritional Support:*
- Early enteral nutrition is superior to parenteral nutrition and to NPO management
- Feeding within 48 hours of hospitalization → positive impact on return to voluntary intake + reduced GI signs (34-dog study)
- Dogs not vomiting: feed orally as soon as possible; if anorectic use feeding tube
- If severe vomiting: delay feeding tube placement
- No evidence that postpyloric (jejunal) feeding is superior; nasogastric/nasoesophageal routes also appropriate
- Diet: traditionally low-fat recommended (ideal fat content not determined); introduce gradually over 3–4 days until RER met
- Ultra-low-fat diet (<20 g fat/1000 kcal): generally recommended for chronic pancreatitis (Ch 277)

*Analgesic Therapy:*
| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Buprenorphine | 0.01–0.03 mg/kg IV, IM, or SC q4–8h | Injectable opioid; fast results |
| Fentanyl | 0.005–0.01 mg/kg IV, IM, or SC q2h; CRI 0.002–0.006 mg/kg/h | Injectable; or patches (q3–4 days; size based on patient weight) |
| Meperidine | 2–5 mg/kg IM or SC q2–4h | Injectable |
| Butorphanol | 0.2–0.4 mg/kg IV or SC q4–6h | Mild-moderate pain |
| NSAIDs | Generally avoid — dehydrated/hypovolemic patients at risk for GI ulceration and renal injury | |
(Ch 277)

*Antiemetic Therapy:*
| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Maropitant | 1 mg/kg PO or SC q24h | NK1 antagonist; potent antiemetic; also analgesic properties; possible anti-inflammatory in experimental AP models |
| Ondansetron | 0.1–0.2 mg/kg slow IV q6–12h | 5-HT3 antagonist |
| Metoclopramide | 0.2–0.5 mg/kg IV, IM, SQ, or PO q6–8h; OR CRI 1–2 mg/kg/day IV | CRI more effective than intermittent; enhances upper GI motility |
(Ch 277)

*Appetite Stimulants:*
- Capromorelin: 3 mg/kg PO q24h — likely most effective in hospitalized dogs
- Mirtazapine: 0.5–1.5 mg/kg PO q24h (not to exceed 30 mg/dog/day) — also possible antiemetic properties (Ch 277)

*Antibiotic Therapy:*
- Routine antibiotics NOT recommended (little scientific evidence)
- Indications: confirmed infectious complications (aspiration pneumonia, cystitis, infected pancreatic fluid accumulation)
- Broad-spectrum choices if infectious complications identified/suspected: cefotaxime, enrofloxacin, marbofloxacin (Ch 277)

*Fuzapladib (novel):* LFA-1 antagonist preventing neutrophil extravasation; received full approval in Japan + conditional approval in USA for acute pancreatitis in dogs. Dramatically decreased mortality in experimental studies. Randomized clinical trial results not yet published in primary literature. (Ch 277)

*Glucocorticoids:* Non-controlled study of 45 dogs — prednisolone 1 mg/kg SC q24h from diagnosis to discharge decreased hospitalization time and improved 1-month survival. Further studies needed before routine use recommended. (Ch 277)

*Gastric acid suppression:* ACVIM 2018 consensus: "No evidence that acid suppression treatment is beneficial or indicated in the management of dogs or cats with pancreatitis, unless animal has concurrent evidence of gastroduodenal ulceration and erosion." (Ch 277)

**Chronic/Mild Pancreatitis Management:**
- Antiemetic: maropitant (NK1 antagonist) or ondansetron (5-HT3 antagonist)
- Appetite stimulant: mirtazapine (0.5–1.5 mg/kg PO q24h, max 30 mg/dog/day) or capromorelin (3 mg/kg PO q24h)
- Analgesia: tramadol, buprenorphine, gabapentin
- Ultra-low-fat diet (<20 g fat/1000 kcal)
- Manage concurrent CIE, hepatitis, DM
- Some dogs may benefit from immunosuppression (corticosteroids or cyclosporine) for suspected autoimmune mechanism (Ch 277)

**Prognosis:**
- Mild disease: good prognosis; long survival if no recurrence
- Severe acute pancreatitis: guarded; mortality 20–42% in more severe forms
- Chronic/recurrent: difficult to predict; evaluated individually. Canine acute pancreatitis severity scoring system shows promise in predicting outcome. (Ch 277)

### Ch 278 — Pancreatitis in Cats (Forman)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-GI-PANCAT (Feline Pancreatitis) — see enrichments

**Prevalence:** Overall histologic prevalence 67% in necropsied cats (chronic 76%, acute 9%, acute + chronic 15%). 45% of necropsied cats with pancreatitis were apparently healthy. No age, sex, or breed predisposition. No correlation with body condition score, high-fat meals, or drug exposure (including steroids). >95% of acute or chronic cases are idiopathic. (Ch 278)

**Definitions:**
- Acute pancreatitis: entirely reversible after resolving inciting cause; worse short-term prognosis but more favorable long-term
- Chronic pancreatitis: irreversible histopathologic changes (atrophy, fibrosis); classified as "lymphocytic/lymphoplasmacytic"; better long-term prognosis

**Concurrent diseases:** DM, IBD, hepatic lipidosis, cholangitis, nephritis, immune-mediated hemolytic anemia. Triaditis (concurrent pancreatitis + cholangitis + IBD) recognized but causality not demonstrated.

**Clinical Signs:** Lethargy 51–100%, inappetence 62–97%; vomiting 35–52%; weight loss 30–47%; diarrhea 11–38%; dyspnea 6–20%; PU/PD 20%; dehydration 37–92%; hypothermia 39–68%; fever 7–26%. Abdominal pain only in 10–30% (challenging to identify in cats). Icterus 6–37%. (Ch 278)

**Diagnosis:**

*Spec fPL (Feline PLI):*
- Higher sensitivity for moderate-severe pancreatitis (100%) than mild (54%)
- Positive predictive value 90%; negative predictive value 76%
- SNAP fPL: correlates well with Spec fPL (normal vs abnormal)
- Agreement between DGGR-lipase (cutoff 26 U/L) and Spec fPL (>5.3–5.4 mcg/L): kappa 0.681–0.703 ("good" for subjective but suboptimal for objective modalities)
- Best agreement between increased DGGR-lipase or Spec fPL with hypoechoic or heteroechoic pancreatomegaly on US (Ch 278)

*fTLI:* Primary utility = screening for EPI (not for diagnosis of pancreatitis; sensitivity 30–86%; increased by chronic enteropathy, GI lymphoma, decreased GFR). (Ch 278)

*Abdominal US:*
- US findings consistent with pancreatitis: hypoechoic or heteroechoic pancreatomegaly (right lobe >**0.6 cm**, left lobe/body >**1 cm**); hyperechoic peripancreatic mesentery; peripancreatic effusion; pancreatic lymph node enlargement; duodenal corrugation or distention
- Chronic pancreatitis: irregular capsular margins; dilation of pancreatic duct (normal 0.7–2.5 mm); hyperechoic pancreatic echogenicity
- Normal pancreatic US: isoechoic or hypoechoic to surrounding mesentery, uniform homogenous echotexture, smooth capsular margin
- Normal pancreatic duct in cats: 0.7–2.5 mm
- Some cats with acute pancreatitis have normal pancreatic US; repeat US over time often detects abnormalities
- Overall US sensitivity: 11–67%; specificity 73%; positive predictive value 80%; negative predictive value 57% (Ch 278)

*Histology (gold standard):* Single biopsy → left lobe provides highest diagnostic yield. Acute: neutrophilic inflammation + edema. Chronic: lymphocytic/lymphoplasmacytic + mild fibrosis + acinar atrophy. Acute necrotizing pancreatitis = significant necrosis. (Ch 278)

**Treatment:**

*Fluid Therapy:*
- Subcutaneous or intravenous crystalloid fluid therapy reverses dehydration + corrects electrolytes
- No ideal crystalloid identified; lactated Ringer's favored in humans for acute aggressive hydration
- Monitor body weight, thoracic auscultation, thoracic US to prevent overhydration (Ch 278)

*Nutrition:*
- Inappetence >48 hours despite multimodal therapy → enteral nutrition via feeding tube
- Appetite stimulants first-line: **mirtazapine** (oral/transdermal — generally well-tolerated; dose not specified in Ch 278 text; higher doses → adverse effects: vocalization, agitation, vomiting, ataxia, tremors, hypersalivation, tachypnea, tachycardia); **capromorelin** (oral; approved for cats with CKD in USA; adverse reactions: vomiting, hypersalivation — more frequent in males)
- Nasogastric/esophageal/gastric feeding tubes all utilized; nasogastric tubes allow gastric suctioning in cats with ileus
- Diet: highly digestible "GI diets"; adequate arginine and methionine to prevent hepatic lipidosis; adequate protein to prevent protein-energy malnutrition; low-fat NOT mandatory in cats (unlike dogs)
- Early enteral nutrition reduces infected pancreatic necrosis and multiple organ failure (human data; extrapolated to cats) (Ch 278)

*Pain Management:*
- Signs of pain in 10–30% of cats with pancreatitis; all inappetent cats without clear pain indicators should receive pain management
- First-line: opioids — oral/transbuccal buprenorphine, gabapentin, tramadol (mild-moderate); methadone or fentanyl (severe pain) (Ch 278)

*Controlling Vomiting:*
- **Maropitant citrate:** efficacious, commonly used; additional visceral analgesic + potential anti-inflammatory benefits
- **Ondansetron:** used as first-line or combined with maropitant
- Prokinetics for functional gastroparesis/ileus: erythromycin, cisapride (gastric prokinetic in cats); metoclopramide CRI (increases gastric emptying, decreases gastric atony) (Ch 278)

*Antibiotics:*
- NOT recommended in uncomplicated cases
- Indications: cholangitis, UTI, aspiration pneumonia, pancreatic abscess, infected pancreatic necrosis (35% of cats with moderate-severe pancreatitis by FISH), sepsis signs
- Guided by bacterial culture and sensitivity (Ch 278)

*Glucocorticoids/Cyclosporine:*
- Routine corticosteroid therapy NOT recommended in uncomplicated pancreatitis
- Indicated for sterile comorbidities (chronic inflammatory enteropathy, cholangitis)
- Cyclosporine: decreased Spec fPL concentrations in preliminary evaluation in cats with chronic pancreatitis; additional work needed (Ch 278)

### Ch 279 — Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency & Rare Exocrine Conditions (Steiner)

> **ClinIQ:** ✓ DIS-GI-EPI (EPI) — see diagnostic cutoff and dose enrichments

**EPI Definition:** Insufficient synthesis and secretion of digestive enzymes by exocrine pancreas. >90% of exocrine pancreatic function must be lost before clinical signs occur. (Ch 279)

**Etiology:**
- Dogs: chronic pancreatitis (~slightly >50%); pancreatic acinar atrophy (PAA, ~remaining; almost exclusively German Shepherd Dog, Rough-Coated Collie, Eurasian; young adult onset in GSD/Rough Collie/Shar Pei vs later in other breeds). Also: Cavalier KCS, Shar Pei, West Highland White Terrier increased risk.
- Cats: chronic pancreatitis nearly 100% of EPI cases
- PAA: inherited as autosomal recessive (likely polygenic in GSDs). DLA-DQB1 duplication = risk allele. (Ch 279)

**Clinical presentation:**
- Weight loss (most common)
- Loose stools (large-volume, light-colored, clay-colored due to fat maldigestion); watery diarrhea less common
- Increased appetite, pica, coprophagia in dogs
- Borborygmus, increased flatulence in dogs
- Poor hair coat; perianal soiling in cats
- Decreased appetite: suggests concurrent chronic inflammatory enteropathy (negative prognostic factor in dogs)
- Cobalamin deficiency in 82% of dogs with EPI (36% marked); 77% of cats with EPI
- 2 cats with EPI had vitamin K-responsive coagulopathy (Ch 279)

**Diagnosis:**

*Serum TLI (species-specific immunoassay) = diagnostic test of choice:*
- **Canine TLI cutoff for EPI: <2.5 mcg/L** (current; highly sensitive and specific in dogs)
- **Feline TLI cutoff for EPI: <8.0 mcg/L** (current; highly sensitive and specific in cats)
- Fecal elastase assay (marketed in Europe for dogs only): initial studies showed acceptable sensitivity/specificity but positive predictive value <60%; 23% false-positive rate in one study; must be verified by serum TLI before diagnosis of EPI
- Fecal proteolytic activity / PABA test / turbidity test: not considered useful for EPI diagnosis (Ch 279)

**PERT (Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy):**
- Dried pork pancreatic extract: **1 teaspoon (5 mL) per 10 kg body weight per meal** — starting dose
- After full response: slowly decrease to minimally effective dose (enzyme content may vary container-to-container)
- Powder preferred over tablets/capsules (human and dog studies)
- Micropelleted enteric-coated formulations (Europe): protective against gastric acid; recent studies show equal efficacy to uncoated powder at lower doses
- Raw pancreas equivalent: **30–90 g (1–3 oz) of raw pancreas per teaspoon of dried extract**; finely chopped, frozen (maintains enzymatic activity for long periods). Species: pork, beef, sheep, game. Freeze immediately in meal-sized portions.
- Preincubation of food with extract NOT necessary
- Low-fat diets: generally NOT recommended (experimental studies show fat digestibility doesn't fully normalize with PERT; fat restriction risks fat-soluble vitamin and essential fatty acid deficiencies). Low-fat as last resort for non-responders.
- High insoluble fiber diets: AVOID (interfere with fat absorption) (Ch 279)

**Cobalamin Supplementation:**
- Supplement any EPI patient with serum cobalamin <400 ng/L (low-normal range)
- Parenteral (traditional): cyanocobalamin (also hydroxocobalamin or methylcobalamin)
  - **Cats: 250 mcg SC q7 days × 6 weeks, then one dose 1 month later; recheck cobalamin 1 month after last dose**
  - **Dogs: 250–1500 mcg SC q7 days × 6 weeks** (rough body weight scaling); then dose 1 month later; recheck 1 month after
- Oral supplementation equally effective:
  - **Cats: 250 mcg/day PO × 120 days; recheck 2–3 weeks after last dose**
  - **Dogs: 250–1500 mcg/day PO × 120 days** (body weight scaled) (Ch 279)

**Treatment for non-responders:**
1. Review enzyme type, formulation, dosage
2. Evaluate for concurrent conditions: CIE, DM, small intestinal dysbiosis
3. If no concurrent disease found: tylosin (Tylan powder) **25 mg/kg PO q12h × 6–8 weeks**
4. Trial proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole) **0.7–1 mg/kg PO q12h**: may improve therapeutic response by reducing gastric pH-mediated lipase inactivation (Ch 279)

**Prognosis EPI:**
- Most dogs/cats with EPI can be successfully managed; normal quality of life + normal life expectancy
- Marked hypocobalaminemia: negative prognostic factor in dogs → MST 1346 days vs 2709 days without marked hypocobalaminemia (p=0.012)
- Additional negative risk factors: serum cobalamin <350 ng/L, male sex, decreased appetite at diagnosis, not receiving PERT
- Do not re-evaluate serum TLI (remains subnormal throughout treatment)
- EPI traditionally lifelong (acinar cells considered non-regenerative); rare anecdotal reports of recovery (Ch 279)

**Cobalamin deficiency as leading modifiable prognostic factor:** MST in dogs with marked hypocobalaminemia 1346 days vs 2709 days (p = 0.012). (Ch 279)
