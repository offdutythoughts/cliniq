### Ch 280 — Growth Hormone Disorders in Cats

> ClinIQ: ✓ Acromegaly/Feline Hypersomatotropism (DIS-ENDO-ACRO)
> Enrichments: IGF-1 cutoff (≥1000 ng/mL), cabergoline dose, pasireotide, post-hypophysectomy desmopressin note.

#### Hyposomatotropism (HypoS) — Congenital Feline Pituitary Dwarfism

**Diagnosis**
- Congenital HypoS: proportionate ("proportionate dwarfism"), stunted growth apparent by 1–2 months.
- GH assays rarely available; feline stimulation tests not validated. Rely on low/undetectable serum IGF-1.
- IGF-1 falsely low in: liver disease, newly diagnosed DM (~9% false-negative), lymphoma, renal disease. In true HypoS, IGF-1 is undetectable or severely decreased (not merely low-normal).
- One case: 7-month-old male kitten, disproportionate growth, congenital hypothyroidism, moderate IGF-1 decrease normalized after 6 weeks thyroid hormone replacement (Ch 280).

**Treatment**
- No feline-specific GH available; porcine or human GH anecdotally used (potentially antigenic).
- Progestins do NOT raise IGF-1 or GH in cats (unlike dogs).
- Treat concurrent pituitary deficiencies (e.g., hypothyroidism).
- Dietary caloric adjustment for abdominal fat tendency.
- Acquired post-hypophysectomy HypoS: not treated (no significant clinical consequence) (Ch 280).

**Prognosis**
- Untreated congenital HypoS: poor prognosis, shortened lifespan, infectious/degenerative/neurological complications.

---

#### Hypersomatotropism (HyperS) / Acromegaly — Cats

**Prevalence**
- 18–32% of diabetic cats (UK screening: 24.8%; 95% CI 21.2–28.6%). Largest study: IGF-1 >1000 ng/mL confirmed HyperS in 95% of that subpopulation (Ch 280).
- Non-diabetic cats with HCM phenotype: ~6.7%; 1 of 6 to 1 of 50 non-diabetic cats with myocardial hypertrophy may have HyperS.
- Typical: neutered males, mean age 11.3 years (range 4–19), 87% Domestic Shorthair. Maine Coon possibly over-represented.

**Pathogenesis**
- Pituitary adenoma (somatotroph) most common; 10/37 pituitaries showed non-neoplastic hyperplasia. Carcinomas rare.
- Organohalogenated contaminants (PCBs, PBDEs) implicated.
- AIP gene mutation (AIP:c.9T>G) identified in some cats.

**Clinical Signs**
- DM-related: PU/PD, polyphagia (extreme in 20%), weight gain, insulin resistance (>1.5 IU/kg/injection BID).
- Structural: broad facial features, prognathia inferior, clubbed paws, abdominal organomegaly, respiratory stertor/snoring (38%), HCM/CHF, arthropathy.
- CNS signs (circling, blindness, seizures) rare due to slow-growing tumor.
- Fructosamine lower in hyperS cats despite higher insulin doses and higher body weight.

**Diagnosis**

*IGF-1 Screening*
- Serum IGF-1 ≥1000 ng/mL (≥131 nmol/L): positive predictive value ≥95% for HyperS in diabetic cats (Ch 280).
- Recommend screening every newly diagnosed diabetic cat with serum IGF-1.
- False negatives: ~9% of untreated diabetic cats (insufficient portal insulin to drive hepatic IGF-1 production).
- False positives: reported in non-HyperS diabetic cats; assay differences exist.

*Imaging*
- CT (preferred; less anesthesia time) or MRI: 85% CT-positive in IGF-1 >1000 ng/mL cats; of CT-negative, 50% MRI-positive; of remaining, 75% PM shows pituitary hyperplasia.
- Normal imaging does not exclude HyperS (microadenoma/hyperplasia).

*GH assay*: Commercial assays unavailable. GH suppression tests not useful (pulsatile secretion persists in HyperS) (Ch 280).

*Other markers*: PIIIP (procollagen type III propeptide) 5× higher in HyperS-DM vs non-HyperS DM. Serum ghrelin: sensitive but not specific.

**Treatment**

| Modality | Key Detail |
|---|---|
| Hypophysectomy | Best option; transsphenoidal via mouth/nasopharynx. 71% DM remission in survivors (n=68 cats); median survival 853 days. Post-op: levothyroxine + hydrocortisone oral long-term; conjunctival desmopressin needed in 72% (Ch 280). |
| Radiotherapy | Unpredictable; does NOT normalize IGF-1; myocardial hypertrophy persists even with DM improvement. Useful to reduce large tumors pre-surgery or if surgery declined. |
| Pasireotide (Signifor, Novartis) | Short-acting q12h SC or long-acting monthly SC. ~25% DM remission with monthly form. Reduces IGF-1, avg 12-hr glucose, fructosamine, insulin requirement. Side effect: transient diarrhea. Cost limiting (Ch 280). |
| Cabergoline | 10 mcg/kg PO q48h. DM remission reported in 3 separate cats; widely available, affordable, few side effects. Not effective in all cats; no known tumor size reduction (Ch 280). |
| Insulin (palliative DM control) | Use basal insulins (PZI, glargine, detemir) preferred to avoid hypoglycemia at GH nadir. Increase dose no more than 1 unit/injection/cat/week. Freestyle Libre CGM useful (Ch 280). |

**Post-hypophysectomy Monitoring**
- Serum IGF-1: best indicator of surgery success; should drop rapidly. 4-week IGF-1 nadir significantly lower in cats achieving DM remission.
- DM recurrence after surgery: 12% at median 248 days (Ch 280).

### Ch 281 — Canine Growth Hormone Disorders

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Canine GH Disorders / Pituitary Dwarfism / Canine Acromegaly)

#### Canine Acromegaly

**Pathogenesis**
- Progestin (PG)-induced mammary GH secretion: most common in middle-aged/elderly intact or PG-treated bitches.
- GH-producing mammary tumor (rare). Hypothyroidism-associated GH/IGF-1 excess (Ch 285). Pituitary somatotroph adenoma (rare in dogs; more common in cats).
- German Shepherd Dogs predisposed to acromegaly.

**Diagnosis**
- Basal GH above reference common but not specific (pulsatile; may be incidental).
- GHRH stimulation test: 1 mcg/kg IV — in acromegaly, fails to further stimulate GH.
- Somatostatin suppression: 10 mcg/kg IV — in acromegaly, fails to suppress GH.
- IGF-1: heterologous (human) assays usable. Breed-specific reference ranges needed (strong linear correlation with body size).
- GH excess in non-PG dog → exclude hypothyroidism (Ch 285) before assuming pituitary adenoma.

**Treatment**
- PG-induced: ovariectomy (reverses DM if beta cells not exhausted); discontinue PG drugs.
- PG receptor blocker: aglepristone — significantly decreases GH and IGF-1.
- Primary hypothyroidism-associated: levothyroxine → normalizes GH and IGF-1.
- Pituitary adenoma: medical (SST analogues octreotide/lanreotide — ~50% response in people, variable in dogs; GH receptor antagonist pegvisomant NOT effective in dogs — canine GH receptor differs), radiation, or hypophysectomy (limited canine experience) (Ch 281).

---

#### Canine Pituitary Dwarfism

**Genetics/Breeds**
- Simple autosomal recessive in German Shepherd Dogs: mutation in LHX3 intron 5 (deletion of one of six 7-bp repeats, reducing intron to 68 bp → defective LHX3 splicing).
- Same LHX3 mutation in Saarloos Wolfdogs and Czechoslovakian Wolfdogs (31% and 21% carrier frequency respectively).
- German Shepherd Dog dwarfs: combined GH, TSH, prolactin deficiency + impaired gonadotropins; normal ACTH. Also atlanto-axial malformation risk.
- Chihuahua case: 6-bp deletion in exon 5 of GH1 (Ch 281).

**Clinical Signs**
- Normal at birth; growth retardation apparent by 2–5 months.
- Retention of lanugo/secondary hairs, lack of primary/guard hairs (easily epilated), truncal alopecia beginning at wear points.
- Progressive hyperpigmentation, scaly skin, secondary bacterial infections.
- Fox-like muzzle; cryptorchidism (males), failure to ovulate (females).
- By 3–5 years: dull, thin, bald; progressive pituitary cyst expansion; progressive renal failure.

**Diagnosis**
- Low plasma IGF-1 (not definitive alone); low TSH; low serum T4.
- Definitive: GH stimulation test. Use GHRH (1 mcg/kg IV), clonidine (10 mcg/kg), or xylazine (100 mcg/kg). Samples before and 20–30 min post. Healthy dogs: GH increases ≥2× baseline. Dwarfs: no significant response (Ch 281).
- Ghrelin stimulation: 2 mcg/kg IV; GH >5 mcg/L at 20 min excludes pituitary dwarfism.
- CT/MRI: pituitary cysts common; small pituitary (hypoplasia). Cysts also in healthy dogs — not diagnostic alone.
- DNA test (LHX3 mutation): available for German Shepherd Dogs, Saarloos, Czechoslovakian Wolfdogs (Ch 281).

**Treatment**
- Porcine GH (canine and porcine GH identical amino acid sequence): 0.1 to 0.3 IU/kg SQ 3 times per week. Monitor plasma GH and glucose at 3-week intervals; long-term dose guided by IGF-1. Response in skin/lanugo within 6–8 weeks; guard hair variable.
- Progestins (medroxyprogesterone acetate, MPA): 2.5–5 mg/kg SQ first at 3-week intervals then 6-week intervals. Risks: pyoderma, skeletal malformations, mammary tumors, acromegaly, DM, cystic endometrial hyperplasia. Bitches must be OHE before starting. Monitor GH, IGF-1, glucose.
- Proligestone: also described with beneficial hair coat results.
- Levothyroxine: start as soon as evidence of secondary hypothyroidism.
- Prognosis without treatment: poor (usually euthanized by 3–5 years). With treatment: guarded but considerably improved (Ch 281).

#### Acquired GH Deficiency (AGHD) / "Alopecia X"
- Occurs in Pomeranian, Miniature Poodle, Chow Chow, Keeshond, others; trunk/caudal thighs/perineum/neck alopecia, dark pigmented skin.
- GH stimulation response normal in ~1/3 of affected Pomeranians → true GH deficiency poorly supported; plasma IGF-1 invariably within reference ranges.
- Also called "castration-responsive alopecia," "biopsy-responsive alopecia," "GH-responsive dermatosis."
- Treatment with heterologous GH or MPA: poor-to-moderate results (Ch 281).

### Ch 282 — Diabetes Insipidus

> ClinIQ: ✓ Central DI (DIS-PUPD-CDI); ✓ Nephrogenic DI (DIS-PUPD-NDI)
> Enrichments: Full MWDT protocol with exact USG endpoints, DDAVP doses (conjunctival/nasal/SQ/oral/IV), hydrochlorothiazide dose for NDI.

#### Physiology
- AVP (ADH): 9-amino-acid peptide; half-life in dogs 5.9 minutes; pulsatile release from hypothalamic paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei.
- Osmoreceptors detect 1–2% increase in osmolality → stimulate AVP secretion.
- 10–15% decrease in blood volume/pressure → AVP secretion.

#### Central Diabetes Insipidus (CDI)

**Causes**
- Dogs: pituitary neoplasia most common. Cats: trauma most common.
- Post-hypophysectomy CDI: >2 weeks in 53% dogs; permanent in 22%. Long-term CDI in 72% of cats post-hypophysectomy (Ch 282).
- Triphasic response (humans): Phase 1 CDI → Phase 2 SIADH-like → Phase 3 CDI. Described incompletely in dogs/cats; transient water retention has been observed.
- Post-traumatic CDI: severe hypernatremia in ~7% of dogs within 24 hours head trauma.
- Other: craniopharyngioma, meningioma, chromophobe adenoma/carcinoma, lymphoma, metastatic neoplasia, infection, inflammation, cysts, idiopathic; congenital in Afghan Hound littermates (reported).

**Clinical Features**
- Severe PD: water intake up to 5–20× normal (normal: <90–100 mL/day dogs; <50 mL/day cats).
- Complete CDI or primary NDI → hyposthenuria consistently; partial CDI → isosthenuria or minimally concentrated USG.
- Decreased BUN (medullary washout).
- Neurological signs in 7/17 CDI dogs within 2 weeks to 12 months of diagnosis (pituitary neoplasia) (Ch 282).

**Diagnosis**

*Modified Water Deprivation Test (MWDT) — Dogs*

Phase 1 (3–5 days progressive water restriction):
- Day 1: 120–150 mL/kg
- Day 2: 80–100 mL/kg
- Day 3: 60–80 mL/kg
*Dry food; aliquots divided to avoid total deprivation. Hospitalization recommended for severe PU cases (Ch 282).*

Phase 2 (Complete water deprivation):
- Remove water; empty bladder with indwelling catheter; accurate body weight.
- Monitor every 1–2 h: USG ± hematocrit, total protein, Na, BUN.
- Endpoint: 5% body weight loss OR USG ≥1.025.
- Abandon immediately if: lethargy, azotemia, hypernatremia >165 mmol/L, hyperosmolality >350 mOsm/kg.
- Most CDI/NDI dogs lose 5% BW within 3–10 hours; partial CDI/PP takes longer.
- If USG rises to >1.025 during Phase 2: CDI and NDI ruled out → consistent with PP.

Phase 3 (Desmopressin administration):
- Administer desmopressin 2–10 mcg IV (Ch 282).
- Offer small volumes water: 2.5–3 mL/kg/hr total (not all at once; prevent vomiting after dehydration).
- Monitor USG: every 30 min × 2 h, then every hour × 8 h, then at 12 and 24 h if needed.
- Maximal response to DDAVP typically 4–8 hours post-administration.
- End Phase 3 when USG ≥1.015 OR at 24 hours.

*Interpretation:*
| Pattern | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Phase 2 USG >1.025 | Primary polydipsia (PP) |
| No USG increase Phase 2 AND no response to DDAVP (USG <1.008 both) | NDI |
| No/minimal concentration in Phase 2, USG increases to >1.010–1.015 post-DDAVP | Complete CDI |
| Slight concentration Phase 2, further increase post-DDAVP (1.008–1.020 → >1.015) | Partial CDI |

*Testing Cats:* MWDT of questionable value; urinary catheterization/blood sampling difficult. Use trial DDAVP therapy for CDI-suspect cats (Ch 282).

*Trial Desmopressin Therapy (diagnostic alternative):*
- Determine average daily water intake pre-treatment.
- Give DDAVP for 5–7 days.
- CDI: dramatic decrease in water intake + increase in USG.
- NDI or PP: fail to respond.
- Advantage: avoids life-threatening hypertonic dehydration (Ch 282).

**DDAVP Treatment Doses**

| Route | Dog | Cat |
|---|---|---|
| Conjunctival/nasal solution | 1–4 drops (~1.5–4 mcg/drop) q8–24h | 1 drop q12h nasal |
| SQ injectable | 2–5 mcg | 4 mcg q24h |
| Oral tablets | 100 mcg q12–24h (variable, often poor bioavailability) | 25–50 mcg q8–12h |

*Note: Failure to respond likely due to failure to administer DDAVP or use of tablets (lower bioavailability vs liquid). Dosage/frequency based on individual response (Ch 282).*

#### Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus (NDI)

**Primary NDI**
- Extremely rare; most cases secondary (hyperadrenocorticism, pyometra, liver disease).
- Primary familial NDI: Siberian Huskies (V2 receptor low binding affinity); X-linked AVP2R mutation suspected (all affected male).
- Treatment: hydrochlorothiazide 2.5–5 mg/kg PO q12h in both dogs and cats (Ch 282).
- Low-Na, low-protein diet + NSAIDs (caution: GI adverse effects) + chlorpropamide (sulfonylurea; may upregulate AVP receptors, used for partial CDI).

**Prognosis**
- Idiopathic/traumatic CDI: several years manageable. Progressive neoplastic cause: poorer.
- In one series: 7/17 dogs alive 18–72 months after diagnosis; 10 died/euthanized 1 week–2 years (mostly from neurological progression) (Ch 282).

### Ch 283 — Primary Hyperparathyroidism

> ClinIQ: ✓ Hypercalcaemia (DIS-ENDO-HCALC); ✓ Primary Hyperparathyroidism (DIS-ENDO-PHPT)
> Enrichments: Exact PTH reference intervals, PTHrP cutoff, pamidronate/zoledronate doses, calcitriol doses, IV calcium protocol, alendronate for FIH.

#### Calcium Physiology
- Normal TCa: 2.3–2.8 mmol/L (9.2–11.2 mg/dL) dogs; 2.1–2.5 mmol/L (8.4–10.0 mg/dL) cats.
- ~50% iCa (biologically active), ~40% protein-bound, <10% complexed with anions.
- Hypoalbuminemia lowers TCa but does NOT affect iCa.
- PTH reference interval: approximately 20–65 pg/mL (2.1–6.9 pmol/L) dogs; <25 pg/mL (<2.7 pmol/L) cats. (Values vary by laboratory and assay) (Ch 283).
- PTHrP reference interval: generally <0.5 pmol/L in both dogs and cats (Ch 283).

#### PHPT Diagnostic Criteria
- Hallmark: elevated TCa AND iCa WITH PTH within or above the reference interval (inappropriately normal or high PTH given the hypercalcemia) (Ch 283).
- Mean TCa in 210 PHPT dogs: 3.6 mmol/L (14.5 mg/dL); mean iCa 1.71 mmol/L (range 1.22–2.41 mmol/L; reference 1.12–1.41 mmol/L).
- PTH in upper half of reference (>35 pg/mL for dogs; reference 20–65 pg/mL) = abnormal and consistent with PHPT in a hypercalcemic dog.
- Most non-PHPT hypercalcemia: PTH low or undetectable.
- PO4: low-normal or below reference in PHPT with normal renal function. Mean PO4 in 210 PHPT dogs: 2.8 mg/dL (reference 3.0–6.2 mg/dL).
- US neck: ~90–95% of dogs have parathyroid nodule identified; parathyroid nodules 2–20 mm, hypoechoic; require 7.5–10 MHz transducer (Ch 283).

**Signalment**
- Dogs: mean age 11.2 years (range 6–17), no sex predisposition. Keeshond at highest breed OR (50.7×). Solitary adenoma 87%, hyperplasia 8%, carcinoma 5% (Ch 283).
- Cats: middle-aged to older; palpable cervical mass possible; hyperthyroidism must be excluded.

**Severity Classification (dogs)**
- Mild: TCa >3.0 to ≤3.5 mmol/L (>12 to ≤14.0 mg/dL) — 52%
- Moderate: >3.5 to <4.0 mmol/L — 30%
- Serious: >4.0 to ≤4.5 mmol/L — 12%
- Severe: >4.5 mmol/L — 6% (Ch 283)

#### Pretreatment Management of Hypercalcemia
- IV saline (0.9% NaCl) at 5–10 mL/kg/hr to correct dehydration and enhance renal Ca excretion (Na competes with Ca in tubular reuptake).
- When optimally hydrated: furosemide 2 mg/kg PO q8–12h (further enhances renal Ca excretion). Monitor for hypokalemia.
- Glucocorticoids: NOT effective in PHPT (rarely lower Ca); only use if diagnosis confirmed but treatment delayed (prednisolone ~1 mg/kg).

**Bisphosphonates**
- Pamidronate IV: 1.3–2 mg/kg in 150 mL 0.9% saline over 2 hours. Effects last up to 3 weeks. Well tolerated; may cause GI side-effects and hypocalcemia if overdosed (Ch 283).
- Zoledronate IV: 0.1–0.22 mg/kg diluted in 45 mL normal saline over 30 minutes. >100–850× more potent than pamidronate.
- Salmon calcitonin (emergency): IV 4 IU/kg initial; then 4–8 IU/kg SQ q12–24h. Short-lived; resistance develops after a few days. Causes anorexia/vomiting (Ch 283).

#### Definitive Treatment
- Surgery: treatment of choice, especially nodule >10 mm. Success rate: 45/48 (94%) (Ch 283).
- Percutaneous ultrasound-guided heat ablation: success 44/49 (90%).
- Ethanol ablation: 13/18 (72%) in one study, but subsequent report >90% in 30 dogs.

#### Post-Parathyroidectomy Hypocalcemia Management
- Risk of hypocalcemic crisis higher if pre-treatment TCa >3.5 mmol/L (>14 mg/dL) OR iCa >1.80 mmol/L for months.
- Start vitamin D ± calcium 12–24 hours before surgery if moderately to severely hypercalcemic.

**Vitamin D**
- Calcitriol: initial 20–30 ng/kg PO q12h. Time to maximal effect <4 days; toxic effects resolve in 2–7 days. Available as 0.25 or 0.5 mcg capsules, 1 mcg/mL oral solution (Ch 283).
- Alfacalcidol: 0.01–0.03 mcg/kg PO q24h. Time to maximal effect <4 days.
- Maintenance calcitriol (chronic hypoCalcemia): 10–15 ng/kg PO q12h (Ch 283, Table 283.3).
- Dihydrotachysterol: 0.02–0.03 mg/kg daily reducing to 0.01–0.02 mg/kg q24–48h; time to effect <7 days.

**Calcium supplementation**
- Oral (elemental Ca basis): 10–20 mg/kg PO q8–12h (Ca gluconate, lactate, or carbonate).
  - 1 mg elemental Ca = 11.2 mg Ca gluconate = 7.7 mg Ca lactate = 2.5 mg Ca carbonate.

**Emergency IV Calcium**
- 10% Ca gluconate: 0.5–1.5 mL/kg IV slowly over 20–30 minutes (or until signs resolve). Monitor ECG; stop if ST elevation, QT shortening, or arrhythmias. 10% Ca gluconate = 9.3 mg Ca/mL (Ch 283).
- Then: continuous rate infusion 10–15 mg/kg/hr (= 10–15 mL/kg over 24 hr in 0.9% NaCl).
- Ca chloride: 0.16–0.5 mL/kg over 20–30 minutes (extremely caustic — avoid extravasation).
- Do NOT give calcium SQ (tissue sloughing risk) (Ch 283).

#### Feline Idiopathic Hypercalcemia (FIH)
- Diagnosis by exclusion: low/normal PTH, PTHrP, vitamin D metabolites; normal survey imaging and FNA.
- Treatment step 1: high-fiber/renal/calcium oxalate prevention diet — reassess at 1 month.
- Treatment step 2: alendronate 5–30 mg/cat PO once weekly (with water, empty stomach, 2-hour food gap after; do not split tablets). Escalate dose to effect; reassess at 3+ months (Ch 283).
- Treatment step 3 (if alendronate fails): add prednisolone 2.5–5 mg/cat/day (Ch 283).
- IV pamidronate alternative for cats intolerant of oral medication.

#### Prognosis
- Short-to-mid term (<2 years): excellent in all breeds.
- Keeshond: recurrence rate 50%; non-Keeshond breeds: rare recurrence (1 Border Terrier in a study of 15).
- Post-treatment: iCa/TCa should be rechecked yearly (Ch 283).

### Ch 284 — Hypoparathyroidism

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Hypoparathyroidism)

#### Background
- Rare in dogs and cats. Primary hypoparathyroidism first described in dogs 1966, cats 1990.
- Most cases: idiopathic (immune-mediated lymphocytic parathyroid destruction); parathyroid histopathology shows decreased parenchymal cells, lymphocyte/plasma cell/neutrophil infiltration.
- Immune-mediated etiology supported by resolution in 1 dog with immunosuppressive therapy.
- Iatrogenic: post-surgical thyroidectomy.
- Trauma-induced: bite wound injury (1 dog, transient <6 months).

#### Signalment
- Dogs: age 6 weeks to 13 years (mean ~6 years). Predisposed: females, Miniature Schnauzers, Poodles, German Shepherd Dogs, Terriers. Saint Bernards over-represented in Australia/NZ.
- Cats: primary hypoparathyroidism more common in males.

#### Clinical Signs
- Neuromuscular hyperexcitability: seizures, muscle tremors, fasciculations, stiff gait, jaw champing (~50%), panting, facial rubbing, biting paws, behavior changes (restlessness, anxiety, aggression).
- Ataxia, circling, synchronous diaphragmatic flutter (uncommon).
- Nonspecific: inappetence, vomiting, lethargy, diarrhea.
- Signs typically become more severe with time, episodic, exacerbated by exercise/excitement.
- Cats: more lethargy and poor appetite; lenticular cataracts, third eyelid prolapse.

#### Diagnosis
- Hypocalcemia: TCa <7 mg/dL (<1.75 mmol/L) OR iCa <0.8 mmol/L typically needed for signs.
- Profound decreases in TCa common in hypoparathyroidism (as low as 2.7 mg/dL [0.68 mmol/L]).
- Concurrent hyperphosphatemia (PTH absent → decreased renal PO4 excretion).
- PTH: low or at the low end of reference interval (should be elevated in hypocalcemia — paradox confirms diagnosis) (Ch 284).
- ECG: prolonged ST segment and QT interval.
- Magnesium: usually normal or mildly decreased; significance unclear.

#### Acute Treatment (in-hospital)
- IV 10% Ca gluconate bolus: 0.5–1.5 mL/kg (5–15 mg/kg) IV slowly over 10–15 minutes. (10% Ca gluconate = 9.3 mg Ca/mL). Monitor ECG; stop if bradycardia, PVCs, or QT shortening (Ch 284).
- Ca gluconate CRI (maintenance): 2.5–3.5 mg/kg/h (= 0.3–0.4 mL/kg/h of 10% Ca gluconate) in maintenance IV fluids.
- Repeat boluses only for recurring tetany signs.
- Do NOT use Ca chloride (extremely caustic).
- SQ Ca gluconate: NOT recommended (calcinosis cutis, tissue sloughing risk; death reported in 1 dog).
- Begin calcitriol and oral calcium as soon as possible.

#### Long-term Treatment

**Calcitriol** (preferred):
- Initial loading dose: 20–40 ng/kg/day PO (= 0.02–0.04 mcg/kg/day) for 2–4 days.
- Maintenance: 10–20 ng/kg/day PO (= 0.01–0.02 mcg/kg/day).
- Time to maximal effect: 1–4 days; toxicosis resolves in 1–7 days.
- Available: 0.25 and 0.5 mcg gel capsules (Rocaltrol); compounding recommended for precise dosing (Ch 284).

**Ergocalciferol** (alternative if cost-limiting):
- Initial: 4000–6000 U/kg/day. Once TCa stabilizes at 8–9.5 mg/dL (2–2.38 mmol/L), decrease to q48h dosing.
- Time to effect: 5–21 days; toxicosis resolves in 1–18 weeks (Ch 284).

**Oral Calcium**:
- Cats: 0.5–1 g/day elemental Ca, divided.
- Dogs: 1–4 g/day elemental Ca, divided.
- Ca carbonate 40% elemental, Ca gluconate 9%, Ca lactate 13% elemental Ca.
- Taper oral Ca within 1 week of starting calcitriol if eating well (dietary Ca usually adequate).

**Treatment Goal**:
- Maintain serum TCa just below low end of reference range: 8–9.5 mg/dL (2–2.38 mmol/L).
- Too low → hypocalcemic signs; too high → vitamin D toxicosis, hyperPO4, AKI.

**Monitoring**:
- Initially recheck q2–3 days; as Ca stabilizes, extend to q2–3 months.
- Owners: return if any signs of hypoCa or hyperCa.
- Discontinue vitamin D if hyperCa develops (Ch 284).

**Recombinant PTH**:
- rhPTH (1-84) SQ approved for humans with hypoparathyroidism. No reports in dogs or cats yet (Ch 284).

#### Prognosis
- Excellent with dedicated owners — most die from unrelated causes. Lifelong therapy and monitoring mandatory (Ch 284).

### Ch 285 — Hypothyroidism in Dogs

> ClinIQ: ✓ Hypothyroidism (DIS-ENDO-HYPOTHY)
> Enrichments: Full T4/TSH diagnostic algorithm with sensitivity/specificity table; NTI effect table; drug effect table; levothyroxine dosage and monitoring parameters; myxedema coma IV T4 dose.

#### Pathogenesis
- Primary (>95%): lymphocytic thyroiditis (autoimmune; ~50% have TgAA) OR idiopathic thyroid atrophy.
- Secondary (<5%): pituitary disease (TSH deficiency).
- Tertiary (extremely rare): hypothalamic disease.
- Drug-induced: potentiated sulphonamides, possibly toceranib (Ch 285).

**Breed Predispositions (lymphocytic thyroiditis)**
- English Setter, Gordon Setter, Golden Retriever, Doberman Pinscher, Old English Sheepdog, Rhodesian Ridgeback, Cocker Spaniel, Boxer, Giant Schnauzer, Hovawart.
- Mean age at diagnosis: ~7 years (range 0.5–15).
- Neutered animals, particularly neutered females, at greatest risk.

**Lymphocytic Thyroiditis Stages**
| Stage | Histology | TgAA | TSH | T4/T3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silent | Mild infiltration | Positive | Normal | Normal |
| Subclinical | Marked infiltration | Positive | Increased | Normal |
| Clinical | >75% replaced | Positive | Increased | Decreased |
| End-stage | Minimal tissue, minimal inflammation | Negative | Increased or decreased | Decreased |

*Only ~1/3 of TgAA-positive euthyroid dogs eventually develop functional hypothyroidism (Ch 285).*

#### Clinical Signs
- Metabolic (>80%): lethargy, weight gain, exercise intolerance, mental dullness, cold intolerance, generalized weakness, shivering.
- Dermatological (up to 80%): bilaterally symmetric, non-pruritic endocrine alopecia (flanks, thighs, areas of friction); dry coarse coat; seborrhea; hyperpigmentation; "rat-tail"; "tragic facial expression" (myxedema over head); thickened lips, drooping eyelids.
- Cardiovascular: bradycardia (~15%); reduced fractional shortening; atherosclerosis (rare, usually with very high cholesterol).
- Neurological (uncommon but possible): peripheral polyneuropathy, central vestibular dysfunction (reversible).
- Other: gallbladder mucocele association (~25% of mucocele dogs are hypothyroid); increased fructosamine (36–82%); hypercholesterolemia (~75%); mild anemia (normochromic/normocytic, up to 50%); increased CK (18–35%).

#### Thyroid Function Test Diagnostic Performance

| Test | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| Total T4 (TT4) | 89–100% | 73–82% |
| Free T4 (fT4 by ED) | 80–98% | 78–94% |
| cTSH | 58–87% | 82–100% |
| TgAA | 91–100% | 94–100% |

*(Ch 285, Table 285.5)*

**Key diagnostic points:**
- TT4: excellent sensitivity; poor specificity (affected by NTI, breed, age, drugs). Do NOT use alone.
- fT4 by equilibrium dialysis (ED): best single test; gold standard; less affected by NTI/drugs/autoantibodies.
- cTSH: poor sensitivity (significant proportion of hypothyroid dogs have normal cTSH — TRH receptor desensitization); good specificity. Unaffected by most drugs and NTI. Increased in hypoadrenocorticism (recovery from NTI) — exceptions.
- TgAA: confirms lymphocytic thyroiditis/thyroid disease; does NOT confirm functional hypothyroidism; not affected by NTI. Screens for T4AAs (discordant total T4/fT4 results).

**Recommended combination**: TT4 + cTSH (cost-effective, widely available).
- Low TT4 + elevated cTSH → Hypothyroid (most reliable combination).
- Low TT4 + normal cTSH → Check fT4 (NTI, drugs, or 20–30% of hypothyroid dogs).
- Normal TT4 + elevated cTSH → Subclinical/early hypothyroidism (Ch 285, Fig 285.3).

**Sighthound Breed-Specific Low T4**: TT4 below standard reference in ~90% Greyhounds, 75% Basenjis, 65% Sloughis, 55% Salukis, 25% Whippets. Breed-specific reference intervals needed (Ch 285).

**Stimulation Tests (reserved for equivocal cases)**:
- rhTSH (human recombinant TSH): 75 mcg/dog IV (100 mcg for dogs >20 kg). Samples before and 6 h post. Post-rhTSH TT4 <1.7 mcg/dL (22 nmol/L) = high diagnostic sensitivity (100%) for hypothyroidism, specificity >90% (Ch 285).
- TRH stimulation: 10 mcg/kg IV; peak cTSH at ~20 min. Limited response in hypothyroid dogs. Useful if GH assay available (GH further increases after TRH in hypothyroid dogs). Sample before and 45 min after TRH.

#### Levothyroxine Treatment
- Synthetic T4 (levothyroxine) is treatment of choice: physiological prohormone, longer half-life than T3, avoids thyrotoxicosis risk.
- Bioavailability oral T4: estimated 10–50%; almost halved when given with food. Feed consistently (with or without food) on monitoring days.

**Starting Dose**:
- 20 mcg/kg PO q24h (widely accepted starting dose) (Ch 285).
- Successful in ~65% at q24h; ~35% benefit from higher dose or q12h.
- q12h dosing: 20 mcg/kg q12h successful but not necessarily superior to q24h for most dogs.
- Lower doses may be needed for sighthound breeds.

**Monitoring TT4**:
- Steady state: within 2 weeks of initiating therapy.
- Sample 4–6 hours after dose (peak TT4).
- Target: peak TT4 of about 4 mcg/dL (50 nmol/L) on q24h dosing.
- Values <2.7 mcg/dL (35 nmol/L): inadequate response → increase dose.
- Values 7.0–7.8 mcg/dL (90–100 nmol/L): markedly high → consider dose reduction (though dogs very resistant to thyrotoxicosis).
- For q12h dosing: peak TT4 (4–6 hours post-dose) at high end or just above reference; pre-dose TT4 within reference.

**Clinical Response**:
- Metabolic (lethargy, dullness): improvement within days; increased activity within 6 weeks.
- Expected weight loss: ~10% in first few months.
- Dermatological: begin within 1 month; normalize 2–3 months (phase of increased hair loss often precedes regrowth).
- Neurological: up to 6 months.
- Cholesterol/triglycerides: decrease dramatically; fructosamine decreases (protein turnover ↑); anemia improves.
- Creatinine, SDMA, GFR, adiponectin: normalize rapidly.

**Myxedema Coma**:
- IV levothyroxine: 5 mcg/kg q12h. Switch to oral once stabilized.
- Expected resolution of abnormal mentation, ambulation, systolic hypotension within 30 hours (Ch 285).

**Adverse Effects**:
- Dogs very resistant to thyrotoxicosis. Up to 10× standard dose tolerated in healthy dogs.
- Signs of excess: PU/PD, polyphagia, panting, weight loss, hyperactivity, tachycardia, hyperthermia — resolve within days of withdrawal.

### Ch 286 — Feline Hypothyroidism

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Feline Hypothyroidism)

#### Etiology
- Congenital (more frequently recognized than adult-onset): dyshormonogenesis (most common), thyroid agenesis/hypoplasia, TSH resistance, thyroiditis.
- Adult-onset spontaneous (rare): lymphocytic thyroiditis, diffuse follicular hyperplasia, idiopathic. 7-cat series: all had increased TSH (2.1–20 ng/mL).
- Iatrogenic (most common cause in cats): post-radioactive iodine, bilateral thyroidectomy, anti-thyroid drugs (Ch 286).
  - Radioiodine (2–6 mCi): overt hypothyroidism 1–40%; subclinical 13–46% at 6 months.
  - Methimazole (80 non-azotemic cats, 6 months): 35% became hypothyroid (TT4 below reference + TSH above reference).
  - Bilateral thyroidectomy: ~50% become hypothyroid.

#### Clinical Signs
**Congenital**: Normal at birth; slow growth apparent by 6–8 weeks. Disproportionate dwarfism (large heads, short wide necks, short body/thick limbs), broad head/flat face, palpable bilateral goiter, obesity, lethargy, mental dullness, constipation, hypothermia, bradycardia, retained/delayed deciduous teeth, thick undercoat with sparse guard hairs. Radiographic: delayed epiphyseal ossification, epiphyseal dysgenesis, shortened vertebral bodies, constipation.

**Adult-onset**: Lethargy, weight gain, dermatologic abnormalities (dull/dry/unkempt coat, matting, increased shedding, dandruff, seborrhea, alopecia), inappetence, mental dullness, obesity, PU/PD, myxedema (1 cat). Bradycardia, mild hypothermia.

**Iatrogenic**: Usually mild — decreased activity, weight gain.

#### Diagnosis
- Low serum TT4 with increased TSH = primary hypothyroidism.
- Canine TSH assay (Immulite canine TSH, Siemens) validated for cats: increased TSH in cats with congenital (2.7–12 ng/mL in 6 goitrous cats), adult-onset (all 7 cats increased; 2.1–20 ng/mL), and iatrogenic (85% of 26 iatrogenic hypothyroid cats increased; only 14% euthyroid cats increased).
- fT4: low in 88% of 22 hypothyroid radioiodine-treated cats (vs TT4 low in only 19%) — fT4 more sensitive for radioiodine-treated cats but less specific (5 of 71 euthyroid cats also had low fT4) (Ch 286).
- TT4 range in 6 congenital goitrous hypothyroid cats: 0.4–1.0 mcg/dL (5.2–12.9 nmol/L).
- NTI can lower TT4 into hypothyroid range → low TT4 alone does not confirm hypothyroidism.
- Gold standard: scintigraphy and TSH stimulation test (not routinely performed).
- rhTSH stimulation test for cats: 25 mcg IV (lower dose than dogs). Euthyroid cats: 2–3 fold TT4 increase at 6–8 hours. Iatrogenic hypothyroid cats (post-radioiodine): TT4 increase ≤11% (Ch 286).

#### Treatment
- Iatrogenic (transient, post-RI or surgery): euthyroidism returns 2–3 months; treat if clinical signs present, azotemia, or if doesn't resolve within 3–6 months.
- Anti-thyroid drug-induced: reduce dose.

**Levothyroxine sodium (LT4Na)**:
- Initial dose: 10–20 mcg/kg PO q12–24h (Ch 286).
- Minimum 4–6 weeks before assessing clinical response.
- Monitor: TT4 and TSH within reference ranges as goal.
- Congenital goitrous cats (n=6): improved on 10–20 mcg/kg q12h; final dose 14–20 mcg/kg (median 16 mcg/kg) PO q12h. Haircoat and activity improved at 8 weeks; normal appearance and dentition at 4 months. Goiters shrank but remained palpable.
- Adult spontaneous hypothyroid cats (n=7): median dose 200 mcg/cat PO q24h (32.7 mcg/kg q24h), range 100–200 mcg/cat/day. Signs, azotemia, anemia resolved in all cats (Ch 286).

**Iatrogenic hypothyroidism and CKD**:
- Hypothyroid cats more likely to become azotemic (GFR decreases).
- Hypothyroid azotemic cats: median survival 456 days (range 231–1869) vs euthyroid azotemic cats: 905 days (range 316–1869). Prolonged iatrogenic hypothyroidism should be avoided (Ch 286).

### Ch 287 — Hyperthyroidism in Cats

> ClinIQ: ✓ Feline Hyperthyroidism (DIS-ENDO-HYPERTHY + DIS-SEC-HYPERT)
> Enrichments: Methimazole/carbimazole exact doses; 131I dosing principles; T:S scintigraphy cutoff; TT4/fT4/TSH diagnostic sensitivity data; survival tables; renal considerations.

#### Epidemiology & Pathogenesis
- Most common feline endocrine disease; ~10% of senior cats.
- Bilateral adenoma ~70%; unilateral ~25%; ectopic ~4%; carcinoma ~1–2%.
- Adenomas may transform to carcinomas if untreated or after chronic anti-thyroid drug use.
- Activating mutations in TSH receptor gene and GNAS subunit identified in some cats.
- Diet (iodine/selenium imbalance) and PBDEs/bisphenol A implicated in pathogenesis.

#### Clinical Features
- Most ≥9 years (mean ~13 years). Weight loss (92%), polyphagia (55%), vomiting (47%), hyperactivity (41%), PU/PD (33%), diarrhea (21%).
- Palpable thyroid nodule (98% on PE), muscle wasting (77%), tachycardia (31%), heart murmur (29%).
- Liver enzymes elevated (>90% have at least one enzyme elevated). Fructosamine and IGF-1 LOWER in hyperthyroid cats (complicates DM monitoring, insulinoma screening, acromegaly diagnosis).
- Mild erythrocytosis in non-dehydrated cat should prompt investigation for hyperthyroidism.
- USG <1.035 possibly associated with increased risk of post-treatment azotemia (controversial).

#### Diagnosis

*Total TT4:*
- Abnormal in 90–95% of hyperthyroid cats on a single random sample.
- In mild disease: only 60–80% have elevated TT4.
- NTI may decrease TT4 (masking disease). Repeat testing if strongly suspected but TT4 normal.

*Free T4 (fT4 by equilibrium dialysis):*
- Abnormal in ~98% of hyperthyroid cats; 85–95% in mild disease.
- False positives in 10–30% of euthyroid cats without concurrent disease; also in cats with systemic illness.
- Use when: clinical suspicion high + TT4 in upper-normal range (to increase sensitivity). Not appropriate as sole first-line test (low specificity).

*Canine TSH assay (used for cats):*
- Undetectable in ~98% of hyperthyroid cats (high sensitivity). Detectable TSH makes hyperthyroidism unlikely.
- Many healthy cats and cats with NTI also have undetectable TSH → poor specificity (~70%).
- Useful to confirm iatrogenic hypothyroidism during treatment (Ch 287).

*Thyroid Scintigraphy (99mTcO4−):*
- Thyroid:salivary gland (T:S) ratio ≤1.5 = normal; ratio >1.5 = diagnostic for hyperthyroidism (Ch 287).
- Uses: equivocal TT4 results, unilateral vs bilateral disease pre-surgery, ectopic/intrathoracic thyroid, suspected carcinoma (SHIM-RAD), individualizing 131I dose, assess suspected carcinoma.

#### Treatment

**Anti-thyroid Drugs**

*Methimazole:*
- Starting dose: 1.25–2.5 mg PO q12h (Ch 287). Euthyroidism attained faster with q12h vs q24h.
- Severely hyperthyroid: 5 mg PO q12h if pre-treatment renal function normal.
- Alternative when difficult to pill: 5 mg PO q24h.
- Dose adjustment: increase by 1.25–2.5 mg/day every 2 weeks until TT4 within reference range; reduce dose if TT4 too low.
- Transdermal methimazole: 2.5–10 mg q12h to inner pinna; 66% of 27 cats given 2.5 mg q12h became euthyroid at 4 weeks. Lower GI side-effects but similar incidence of other reactions; first recheck ~4 weeks. Alternate ears; remove crusts gently.

*Carbimazole (slow-release):*
- Starting dose: 15 mg PO q24h (10 mg q24h if TT4 only mildly elevated) (Ch 287).
- 5 mg carbimazole ≡ 3 mg methimazole.

*Monitoring:*
- Timing of blood sampling relative to anti-thyroid drug administration: NOT important (thyroid-blocking effect lasts >12 hours).
- Administer drug as usual on monitoring day.
- Monitor TT4 after 2–4 weeks; CBC q2 weeks for first month (optional if budget limited).
- Goal: TT4 well within reference interval.

*Adverse Reactions:*
- Non-life-threatening (up to 25%): anorexia, vomiting, lethargy (usually transient). First try 50% dose reduction for a few days; if persists, stop temporarily; if recurrent → switch to another modality.
- Facial/cervical excoriations (4–11%): permanently stop anti-thyroid drug.
- Mild hematological abnormalities (leukopenia, eosinophilia, lymphocytosis): common, monitor; rarely require discontinuation.
- Life-threatening: hepatopathy 2.6% (oral)/4% (transdermal); severe thrombocytopenia 2.8%/8%; agranulocytosis/neutropenia 2.7%/6.1%; bleeding diathesis (PT, APTT, PIVKA prolongation) 2.5%; myasthenia gravis (rare) (Ch 287, Tables 287.4 & 287.5).
- Adverse reactions typically within first 4–8 weeks. DO NOT switch from methimazole to carbimazole if adverse reaction occurs (both metabolize to methimazole).

*Iatrogenic Hypothyroidism:*
- Low TT4 + increased serum TSH = iatrogenic hypothyroidism.
- Reduce anti-thyroid drug by 25–50% (decrements of 1.25–2.5 mg/day methimazole).
- Recheck TT4 and kidney values after 1 week (Ch 287).
- Hypothyroid azotemic cats: median survival 456 days vs 905 days for euthyroid azotemic cats.

**Low-Iodine Diet**
- TT4 normalizes in only 55% by 30–60 days, 72% by 60–90 days. Does not normalize body weight or tachycardia as effectively.
- Strict adherence required (no other food). First recheck 4 weeks after starting diet.
- Do NOT combine with anti-thyroid drugs (can cause severe/fatal hypothyroidism) unless in extreme circumstances (Ch 287).

**Radioactive Iodine (131I)**
- Single injection; treatment of choice especially in younger cats and those without significant concurrent disease.
- Mandatory hospitalization: usually 3–14 days (country/jurisdiction dependent).
- Dosing: individualized based on TT4, thyroid size by scintigraphy, % pertechnetate uptake, % 24-hour 131I uptake. Low-dose protocols (<2 mCi) used by some centers.
- SHIM-RAD tumors: high doses for total thyroid ablation (usually leads to iatrogenic hypothyroidism).
- Stop anti-thyroid drugs at least 1 week before 131I (allow TT4 to rise, suppress TSH to protect normal thyroid tissue).
- Post-treatment monitoring: TT4 and TSH at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months; then yearly.
- Adverse reactions extremely rare (<0.1%): transient radiation thyroiditis (dysphagia/fever, self-limiting), rare permanent voice change.
- Cats with suspected thyroid carcinoma or SHIM-RAD: much higher 131I doses (Ch 287).

**Survival Data**
| Treatment | Median Survival (Milner et al., 2006) |
|---|---|
| Methimazole alone | 2.0 years (IQR 1–3.9) |
| 131I alone | 4.0 years (IQR 3.0–4.8) |
| Methimazole + 131I | 5.3 years (IQR 2.2–6.5) |

*(Survival longer with 131I; cats with pre-existing renal azotemia had significantly shorter survival) (Ch 287, Table 287.7)*

**Thyroidectomy**
- Bilateral: >90% permanent euthyroidism. Hypocalcemia most severe life-threatening complication post-bilateral thyroidectomy.
- Pre-treat with anti-thyroid drugs 2–4 weeks (until morning of surgery) to resolve hyperthyroid state.
- Post-bilateral thyroidectomy: monitor ionized Ca q12h × 2–3 days in hospital. If hypocalcemia → Ca + vitamin D (usually transient).
- Stage surgery (second lobe 4 weeks after first) to allow parathyroid revascularization (Ch 287).

**Renal Considerations**
- Hyperthyroidism: increased GFR, increased cardiac output → masks underlying CKD. Serum creatinine may be falsely low.
- After treatment: "normalization" of GFR (previously elevated by hyperthyroidism). True renal function apparent ≥3 months after euthyroidism.
- No parameter (including SDMA) reliably predicts post-treatment azotemia.
- Pre-existing azotemic cats: more guarded prognosis; Stage per IRIS (Ch 287).

**Beta-blockers for Cardiac Management**
- Propranolol: 2.5–5 mg/cat PO q8–12h (contraindicated in lower respiratory disease).
- Atenolol: 0.25–1 mg/kg PO q12–24h (alternative: 6.25 mg/cat q12h). Target HR <160 bpm in hospital. Never discontinue abruptly. Contraindicated in heart failure (Ch 287, Table 287.6).

### Ch 288 — Canine Hyperthyroidism (Thyroid Carcinoma)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: Canine Thyroid Carcinoma / Canine Hyperthyroidism)

#### Epidemiology & Pathogenesis
- Thyroid cancer: 1–4% of canine neoplasms; most common canine neuroendocrine tumor.
- Canine thyroid tumors: 70–90% malignant; only 10–30% benign adenomas.
- 70% follicular cell carcinomas (thyroglobulin+); 30% medullary carcinomas (calcitonin+; parafollicular/C-cell origin).
- Only ~10% of thyroid carcinomas are functional (causing clinical hyperthyroidism) (Ch 288).
- Iatrogenic: L-thyroxine overdose OR raw beef-based diets containing thyroid tissue.
- Predisposed breeds: Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever, Beagle, Boxer, Shetland Sheepdog, Siberian Husky.
- Mean age 9–11 years (some report 10–15 years); no sex predilection.

#### Clinical Signs
- Most common: palpable cervical mass (firm, non-painful; right and left lobes equally).
- Nonfunctional tumor signs: dysphagia, dysphonia, coughing, upper-airway dyspnea, head/forelimb edema.
- ~10% hyperthyroid (functional tumor): vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, hyperactivity, panting, PU/PD, systemic hypertension.

**Staging (WHO/TNM)**
| Stage | Primary Tumor | Regional Nodes | Distant Metastasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | <2 cm; movable (a) or fixed (b) | No | No |
| II | <2 cm ± ipsilateral nodes; OR 2–5 cm | None or ipsilateral (a) | No |
| III | >5 cm; OR any size bilateral or fixed nodes | Any | No |
| IV | Any | Any | Yes |

*(Ch 288, Table 288.1)*

#### Diagnosis
- Serum TT4 + fT4 (equilibrium dialysis) + cTSH: assess tumor function. T3 if T3-secreting tumor suspected. Anti-T4 antibodies.
- Functional tumor: TT4 and fT4 both elevated; cTSH decreased (negative feedback).
- US: rapid, noninvasive; identifies masses, vascularity, invasion. 14/91 hypercalcemic dogs without palpable mass had thyroid mass on US.
- CT (highest specificity 100%) and MRI (highest sensitivity 93%) for carcinoma diagnosis. CT also allows lung metastasis assessment. Recommended prior to surgery (Ch 288).
- Scintigraphy (99mTcO4): identifies functional tumors, ectopic/metastatic tissue; does NOT correlate with histology.
- FNA cytology: limited reliability for canine thyroid carcinoma (highly vascular; mostly blood). Histology = gold standard.

#### Treatment

**Surgery**
- Freely movable, solitary, well-encapsulated carcinoma: surgical excision first choice. Unilateral thyroidectomy complication rate 19.9%, perioperative mortality 1.9%.
- Non-movable/fixed (invasive) tumors: surgery often not feasible; radiation preferred.
- Bilateral thyroidectomy: bilateral discrete movable carcinomas only. Parathyroid glands must be preserved or minced and reimplanted.
- Most bilateral thyroidectomy dogs need lifelong levothyroxine (Table 288.2):
  - Levothyroxine: 0.022 mg/kg q12–24h (Ch 288, Table 288.2).
- Post-bilateral thyroidectomy hypoparathyroidism: long-term calcitriol 0.03–0.06 mcg/kg/day (Ch 288, Table 288.2).
- Median survival after unilateral or bilateral thyroidectomy: approximately 3 years.

**External Beam Radiation**
- Non-resectable tumors without metastases: mean progression-free interval 45 months; 1-year and 3-year PFS 80% and 72% (n=25 dogs).
- 8 dogs: median survival 24.5 months; 4 died from metastatic disease.
- Hypothyroidism common complication → monitor thyroid function post-radiation.

**Radioiodine (131I)**
- Adjunct to surgery in Stage II–IV disease.
- In 100 dogs: median survival 12–34 months; longest in dogs receiving 131I as adjunct to surgery.
- Serious side-effect: irreversible bone marrow suppression at doses ≥0.2 GBq/kg (≥5.5 mCi/kg) (Ch 288).

**Medical Management (Supportive — Functional/Hyperthyroid Dogs)**
- Methimazole: 2.5–5 mg/dog PO q24h or q12h. Monitor serum T4 1–2 weeks after initiation (Ch 288, Table 288.2).
- Beta-blockers peri-operatively to prevent tachyarrhythmias/thyroid storm:
  - Atenolol: 0.25–1 mg/kg PO q12h (Ch 288).
  - Propranolol: 0.1–0.2 mg/kg PO q8h (Ch 288).
- Antihypertensives:
  - Enalapril or Benazepril: 0.25–0.5 mg/kg PO q12–24h.
  - Amlodipine: 0.1–0.5 mg/kg PO q24h.
  - Telmisartan: 1 mg/kg PO q24h (Ch 288, Table 288.2).
- Paraneoplastic hypercalcemia:
  - Pamidronate: 1–2 mg/kg IV in 250 mL 0.9% NaCl over 2 hours q3–4 weeks.
  - Alendronate: 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q24h (Ch 288, Table 288.2).

**Chemotherapy**
- Toceranib phosphate (Palladia) alone or + carboplatin showing promise.
- Historical: cisplatin had greatest effect (median survival 11 months); doxorubicin, mitoxantrone disappointing.

### Ch 289 — Insulin-Secreting Tumors (Insulinoma)

> ClinIQ: ✓ Insulinoma (DIS-NEO-INSULINOMA); ✓ Hypoglycaemia (DIS-MET-HYPOGLY)
> Enrichments: Whipple's triad cutoffs, amended insulin:glucose ratio clarification, imaging sensitivity, dextrose/glucagon CRI protocols, diazoxide/prednisone/octreotide doses, surgical prognosis.

#### Pathology
- Most (~80%) solitary; either limb of pancreas (not body). 17/18 immunocytochemically confirmed carcinomas in one series.
- Metastasis detected in 45–64% of dogs (higher at necropsy). Most common sites: regional lymph nodes, liver.
- Clinical staging: Stage I (T1N0M0), Stage II (T1N1M0), Stage III (T1N1M1 or T1N0M1).

#### Pathophysiology
- Autonomous insulin secretion despite low blood glucose.
- Normal beta cells: insulin secretion completely inhibited when blood glucose <80 mg/dL (<4.4 mmol/L).
- Insulinoma: insulin secretion persists despite low glucose. Often unpredictable excess response to feeding/glucose.
- Counter-regulatory hormones: glucagon and catecholamines (short-term); GH and glucocorticoids.

#### Clinical Features
- Mean age: 9 years (range 3–15); medium-to-large breed dogs.
- Whipple's Triad: (1) clinical signs of hypoglycemia; (2) fasting blood glucose <60 mg/dL (<3.3 mmol/L); (3) resolution of signs with glucose administration.
- Most common signs (Table 289.1): seizures (48%), collapse (40%), generalized weakness (37%), shaking/trembling (20%), ataxia (20%), exercise intolerance (15%), hind limb weakness (14%).
- Post-ictal changes may be present. Paraneoplastic peripheral polyneuropathy (hind limb paresis/tetraparesis, decreased reflexes) in 13 dogs — likely immune-mediated, unrelated to hypoglycemia.

#### Diagnosis

**Blood Glucose and Insulin**
- Fasting hypoglycemia: blood glucose <60 mg/dL (<3.3 mmol/L) with normo- or hyperinsulinemia (serum insulin within or above reference interval despite concurrent hypoglycemia) = biochemical hallmark (Ch 289).
- In most dogs, hypoglycemia develops within 12 hours of previous meal during supervised fast.
- Monitor blood glucose q30–60 min if euglycemic at presentation.
- Rarely: 48–72 hour fast required for diagnosis.
- Low fructosamine strengthens clinical suspicion; 76% of insulinoma dogs have elevated insulin on single measurement; 91% on two measurements.
- Insulin:glucose ratio, glucose:insulin ratio, and "Amended Insulin:Glucose Ratio": NOT sensitive; NOT recommended (Ch 289).
- Tolerance/stimulation tests: NOT advocated (questionable usefulness, potentially fatal hypoglycemia risk).

**Imaging**
- Abdominal US: pancreatic mass identified in 49/87 (56%) dogs; abdominal metastases in 17 (19%).
- CT (dual-phase): most sensitive — 10/14 (71%) detected. CT superior to US and single-photon emission CT (Ch 289).
- Dual-phase CTA: strong insulinoma enhancement in arterial phase only → dual-phase required; identified masses in 3 dogs including 2 US-negative dogs.
- Scintigraphy (somatostatin receptor): limited value in dogs (variable receptor expression).

#### Treatment

**Acute Hypoglycemia (at home)**
- Feed small meal NOT containing simple sugars.
- If dog cannot eat: rub sugar solution (Karo syrup) onto outer gums/inner lips. Partially fill 6-cc syringe for controlled drip. NEVER put hand in mouth; NEVER pour down throat (Ch 289).
- After oral sugar: feed small meal to prevent rebound.

**Acute Hypoglycemia (in-hospital)**
- 50% dextrose: 0.5 g/kg IV slowly as bolus, diluted 1:3 in 0.9% NaCl (to avoid phlebitis). Lowest effective glucose CRI thereafter (Ch 289).
- CRI: 2.5–5% dextrose at lowest amount needed (avoid stimulating further insulin secretion → rebound cycle).
- Discontinue dextrose when clinical signs resolve even if mild hypoglycemia persists (neuroglycopenia resolves quickly).
- If unresponsive to dextrose alone:
  - Dexamethasone: 0.1 mg/kg IV q12h (anti-insulin gluconeogenic effect for 6+ hours).
  - Somatostatin analogue (octreotide): 10–50 mcg SQ q8–12h.
  - Glucagon CRI: 5–13 ng/kg/min (with or without 10% dextrose). In 1 dog: signs resolved within 20 minutes, hypoglycemia within 1 hour. Monitor for rebound hypoglycemia (Ch 289).
- Severe seizures: sedate with diazepam or pentobarbital.
- Cerebral edema (if suspected): mannitol 1 g/kg IV as 20% solution at 2 mL/kg/h + furosemide 1–2 mg/kg IV q4h (Ch 289).

**Long-term Medical Treatment**

*Diet:* Small frequent meals (every 4–6 hours) high in proteins, fats, complex carbohydrates. AVOID simple sugars (soft moist dog foods) (Ch 289).

*Prednisone:*
- Initial: approximately 0.5 mg/kg/day PO. Start as low as possible; increase as needed.
- In one study: median survival 785 days in 19 post-pancreatectomy dogs; improved to 1316 days in subgroup also receiving prednisone (Ch 289).

*Diazoxide:*
- 10–40 mg/kg/day PO divided q8–12h (about 70% of dogs respond).
- Mechanism: inhibits closure of ATP-dependent K+ channels → decreases insulin exocytosis; also increases glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, inhibits glucose uptake (Ch 289).
- Side effects (uncommon): ptyalism, vomiting, anorexia.
- Start at lowest effective dose; increase gradually.

*Octreotide (synthetic SST analogue):*
- 10–50 mcg SQ q8–12h (dosing variable by report).
- 50 mcg/dog SQ single dose (n=12, median 23 kg): decreased plasma insulin BUT may worsen hypoglycemia by also suppressing glucagon and GH (dose-dependent unpredictability).
- Variable response: some insulinomas lack somatostatin receptors (Ch 289).
- Side effects: pain at injection site, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, constipation, steatorrhea.

*Streptozocin (cytotoxic):*
- Protocol: 500 mg/m2 IV every 3 weeks × 5 treatments, with 0.9% NaCl 18 mL/kg/h for 3 hours pre-, 2 hours during, 2 hours post-infusion (nephroprotective diuresis). Butorphanol 0.4 mg/kg IM as antiemetic (Ch 289).
- Median normoglycemia: 163 days (not significantly different from surgical or medical treatment).
- Intensive 2-week protocol: 8/19 dogs developed DM, 2/19 nephrotoxicosis. Further studies needed before recommended.

#### Surgery
- Treatment of choice long-term; partial pancreatectomy of tumor + obvious metastases.
- Median survival (142 dogs, multiple studies): 12–14 months (range 0 days to 5 years).
- Stage I disease: ~50% normoglycemic at 14 months post-op; Stage II/III: only ~20%.
- Post-operative hyperglycemia (transient, 10% → DM): beta cells suppressed by autonomous insulinoma secretion recover gradually.
- Post-operative DM: requires exogenous insulin for 1–37 months.
- Other complications: pancreatitis, DKA, arrhythmias, hemorrhage, sepsis, leukopenia.

**Prognostic Markers**
- Tumor size and Ki67 index (proliferation marker): independent predictors for survival and disease-free interval (Ch 289).
- Age, sex, body weight, clinical signs, blood glucose/insulin concentration, US detection: NOT significantly associated with prognosis.

#### Feline Insulinoma
- Only 10 well-described cases; mean age 9–17 years, males predominant (8:2).
- Breeds: Siamese (3), DSH (4), DLH (2), Maine Coon (1). One cat had MEN type 1 (insulinoma + adrenocortical adenoma + parathyroid adenoma; full recovery after resection of all 3).
- Diagnosis: hypoglycemia + inappropriate normal/elevated insulin. Mass in left pancreatic lobe (6), right lobe (3), angle (1); size 0.4–3 cm.
- Treatment: surgery (monitor for post-operative transient DM).
- If surgery declined: small frequent meals + prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg q12h.
- Earlier cats: survival 1 day to ~18 months; 2 later cats alive at 32 months post-surgery (Ch 289).
