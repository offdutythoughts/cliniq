### Ch 290 — Diabetic Ketoacidosis and the Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State

> ClinIQ: ✓ DKA (DIS-ENDO-DKA) — ENRICHMENTS: full insulin CRI dose + IM/SQ protocol verbatim, K/PO₄/Mg supplementation table, HHS as distinct syndrome (BG >600 mg/dL + osmolality cutoffs) — flag HHS as candidate for separate entry.

**Author:** Eleonora Malerba

#### Definitions & Diagnostic Criteria

**DKA (ESVE/ALIVE criteria) — all three required:**
1. Diagnosis of DM (per ALIVE criteria)
2. Ketonemia: increased BHB concentrations, ketonuria, and/or detectable AcAc (nitroprusside test strips)
3. Metabolic acidosis: venous/arterial blood pH <7.35 and decreased bicarbonate

> If blood gas unavailable, suspect DKA if criteria 1 + 2 met.

**HHS diagnostic criteria (not uniformly defined; chapter values):**
- Severe hyperglycemia: BG >600 mg/dL (>34 mmol/L)
- Serum osmolality >325–330 mOsm/kg in dogs; >330–350 mOsm/kg in cats
- Dehydration; absence of significant ketoacidosis

**Ketone body detection:**
- Urine dipstick (nitroprusside): detects AcAc and acetone, NOT BHB (the predominant KB in DKA — may underestimate severity)
- Direct BHB: portable blood ketometer preferred for accurate ketonemia

#### Lab Tests Required for DKA Management

- Blood gas (venous/arterial), CBC, serum biochemistry with electrolytes, UA
- BG every 1 hour first 24 h of therapy
- BHB + electrolytes every 2–4 h until DKA resolution
- Other: urine culture, thoracic radiographs, abdominal US, pancreatic lipase, viral testing (cats)

#### Fluid Therapy — DKA

- **Balanced crystalloids with buffer recommended** (Ringer's solution, Plasma-Lyte 148, Normosol-R)
  - 0.9% saline: unbuffered, may prolong acidosis recovery (hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis)
  - Hypotonic fluids (0.45% saline): rarely indicated, risk of cerebral edema
- **Fluid deficit formula:** Fluid deficit (mL) = % dehydration × body weight (kg) × 1000 mL/kg
- Deficit replaced gradually over 24 h (60–80% in first 12 h, remaining 20–40% in next 12 h)
- Maintenance rates 1.5–2× maintenance (60–100 mL/kg/24 h) used initially
- Shock: boluses of 5–10 mL/kg over 20 min until heart rate and blood pressure adequate

#### Potassium Supplementation in DKA (Table 290.1)

| Serum K⁺ (mEq/L) | Dog: add KCl (mEq/L fluids) | Cat: add KCl (mEq/L fluids) |
|---|---|---|
| >5.0 | Wait | Wait |
| 4.0–5.0 | 20–30 | Wait |
| 3.5–4.0 | 30–40 | 20 |
| 3.0–3.5 | 40–50 | 30 |
| 2.5–3.0 | 50–60 | 40 |
| 2.0–2.5 | 60–80 | 60 |
| <2.0 | 80 | 80 |

**K⁺ supplementation must not exceed 0.5 mEq/kg/h**
Frequency of monitoring: every 4–8 h

**Phosphate (PO₄³⁻) supplementation — DKA:**

| Serum PO₄³⁻ (mg/dL) | Dog dose (CRI in calcium-free IV fluids) | Cat dose |
|---|---|---|
| Mild hypophosphatemia (<3 mg/dL) | 0.01–0.03 mmol/kg/h | If 2–2.9 mg/dL → 0.06 mmol/kg/h |
| Severe hypophosphatemia | 0.03–0.12 mmol/kg/h | If <2 mg/dL → 0.12 mmol/kg/h |

- Indications: hemolytic anemia, neuromuscular signs, anorexia/vomiting from intestinal ileus, serum PO₄³⁻ <1.5 mg/dL
- Contraindications: hyperphosphatemia, hypercalcemia, oliguria
- Monitoring: every 8–12 h

**Magnesium supplementation — DKA:**

| Serum Mg²⁺ | Dog dose | Cat dose |
|---|---|---|
| Total Mg <1.5 mg/dL or ionized Mg <1.0 mg/dL | Slow: 0.3–0.5 mEq/kg/d; Rapid: 0.5–1 mEq/kg/d | 0.5–1 mEq/kg/d (if Mg 1.6–1.9) or 1 mEq/kg/d (if Mg <1.6) |
- Given in dextrose 5% in water or 0.9% NaCl; monitoring every 8–12 h

#### Blood Glucose Targets and Dextrose

- Delay insulin 2–4 h (at minimum 2 h if K+ normal, up to 4 h if hypokalemia persists)
- BG target during DKA: keep 150–300 mg/dL (8.3–16.7 mmol/L)
- When BG ≤250 mg/dL (≤13.9 mmol/L) or drops >50–75 mg/dL/h: add dextrose to IV fluids
  - Start with 2.5% glucose solution; 5% may be needed
- Goal: BG decrease of 50–75 mg/dL/h (2.8–4.1 mmol/L/h)

#### Acidosis Management

- Fluid + insulin is usually sufficient
- Sodium bicarbonate: **strictly reserved for plasma bicarbonate ≤11 mEq/L** despite several hours of fluid therapy
- Bicarbonate deficit (mEq) = body weight (kg) × 0.4 × (12 − patient's bicarbonate)
  - Give only HALF this deficit over 6 hours; re-evaluate; repeat until bicarbonate ≥12 mEq/L

#### Insulin Therapy — DKA

##### Low-Dose IV Constant Rate Infusion (CRI) — Regular Insulin

- **Dog: 2.2 U/kg** Regular insulin added to 48 mL 0.9% saline
- **Cat: 1.1 U/kg** Regular insulin added to 48 mL 0.9% saline
- Initial infusion rate: 2 mL/hour (delivers entire 48 mL over 24 h)
- Adjust infusion rate per BG concentrations (see monitoring flowchart)
- Insulin binds to plastic: prime infusion line by flushing entire 48 mL solution (leave in syringe/line 30 min, then discard and use fresh solution)

**CRI efficacy:** 1.1 U/kg/24h, 2.2 U/kg/24h, and escalating dose equally effective in cats. Lispro, aspart CRIs equal to Regular in dogs/cats.

##### Intramuscular (IM) / Subcutaneous (SQ) Protocol — Regular Insulin

- **Loading dose: 0.1–0.2 U/kg IM**
- Then: **0.1 U/kg IM every 1–2 hours**
- Reduce dose 25–50% in first hours if hypokalemia is a concern
- When BG approaches 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L):
  - If dehydrated: 0.1–0.3 U/kg IM every 4–6 h
  - If adequately hydrated: 0.1–0.3 U/kg SQ every 6–8 h
- Maintain BG 150–300 mg/dL with 2.5–5% dextrose solution until DKA resolves

**Lispro IM protocol (dogs):** Initial 0.25 U/kg IM; if BG does not drop ≥10% in 1 h, repeat hourly; otherwise repeat q3h. When BG reaches 250 mg/dL → reduce to 0.125 U/kg IM or SQ q3h.

##### Insulin Glargine — Cats with DKA

- IM glargine 1–2 U/cat, q2–22h, with or without concurrent SQ glargine 1–3 U/cat q12h
  - Associated with successful DKA resolution in all 14 cats studied

#### HHS Therapy

- Slower fluid correction (24–48 h); isotonic 0.9% saline initially
- Initial KCl supplementation: 20 mEq/L recommended
- Delay insulin until plasma volume restored
- BG must not drop >50 mg/dL/h (2.8 mmol/L/h) to avoid cerebral edema
- Initial insulin dose: **reduce by 50% compared to DKA protocols**
- Add 2.5–5% dextrose IV when BG <250 mg/dL (14 mmol/L)
- **Prognosis: guarded to poor**

#### Prognosis — DKA

- Mortality reduced from 25–35% (historical) to <10% with modern standardized protocols
- DM remission possible in cats post-DKA, especially with concurrent pancreatic disease or cats being treated with glucocorticoids

### Ch 291 — Diabetes Mellitus in Dogs

> ClinIQ: ✓ DM (DIS-ENDO-DM) — ENRICHMENTS: all insulin types/doses table, canine-specific starting doses, monitoring protocol box, breed risk table, honeymoon period, diestrus management.

**Author:** Federico Fracassi

#### Diagnosis

- Classic DM: random (fasted or unfasted) BG >200 mg/dL (>11.1 mmol/L) + classic clinical signs with no other plausible cause
- Renal threshold for glucosuria: >180–220 mg/dL (>10–12.2 mmol/L)
- Fasting BG >130 mg/dL but ≤200 mg/dL: document persistent hyperglycemia on separate days AND/OR increased glycated proteins
- Ketonuria + illness + metabolic acidosis + increased anion gap = DKA

#### Breed Risk

High risk (OR >2): Australian Terrier (9.39), Standard Schnauzer (5.85), Miniature Schnauzer (5.10), Bichon Frisé (3.03), Spitz (2.90), Fox Terrier (2.68), Miniature Poodle (2.49), Samoyed (2.42), Cairn Terrier (2.26), Keeshond (2.23)

Low risk (OR <0.5): German Shepherd Dog (0.18), Collie (0.21), Shetland Sheepdog (0.21), Golden Retriever (0.28)

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lente | Vetinsulin/Caninsulin | Porcine | U40 | 8–14 | q12h | 0.25 | 0.6 (0.3–0.9); 0.8 (0.3–1.4) |
| NPH | Humulin N/Novolin N | Recombinant human | U100 | 4–10 | q12h | 0.25 | 0.5 (0.2–0.7); 0.8 (0.4–1.9) |
| PZI | ProZinc | Recombinant human | U40 | 10–16 | q12h (q24h) | 0.25–0.5 | 0.9 (0.4–1.5) |

| Glargine U300 | Toujeo | Recombinant human analog | U300 | 12–24 | q24h (q12h) | 0.5 | 1.9 (0.2–5.2) q24h |
| Detemir | Levemir | Recombinant human analog | U100 | 8–16 | q12h (q24h) | **0.1** | 0.12 (0.05–0.34) |

**Key dosing notes:**
- **Initial recommendation for all newly diagnosed uncomplicated dogs:** 0.25 U/kg SQ q12h (Lente or NPH); conservative — avoids DKA or hypoglycemia
- **Glargine U300 (Toujeo):** now a first-choice for author; initial dose 0.5 U/kg once daily. 54% remain on q24h (median final dose 1.9 U/kg); 46% require q12h. When switching to q12h: decrease each injection dose ~30% per injection (total daily dose increases ~40%). May add NPH 0.25 U/kg SC after meals for post-meal hyperglycemia.
- **Detemir:** ~4× more potent than other insulins in dogs — START at 0.1 U/kg q12h (lower doses required); use with caution in small dogs; do not dilute
- Glargine: **do NOT dilute** (pH dependent)
- Vetsulin/Caninsulin: shake thoroughly until homogeneous milky suspension before use

#### Initial Monitoring Protocol

- BG goals: 80–250 mg/dL (4.4–13.9 mmol/L)
- Pre-insulin BG target: ≤250 mg/dL; nadir: ≥80 mg/dL
- Fructosamine: ideally **slightly above reference range** (normal/low fructosamine in DM dog = concern for hypoglycemia)
- HbA1c: reflects average BG over 2–3 months
- BGC (blood glucose curve): every 1–2 weeks initially, then q3–4 weeks
- Dose adjustments: 10–25% of current dose

#### Re-evaluation Schedule

- 1 week post-diagnosis: BGC or FGMS review
- 2–3 weeks: repeat all
- 3–4 weeks: repeat all
- 8 and 12 weeks: hospital evaluation
- Thereafter: q4 months or as needed

#### Females in Diestrus

- Diestrus (60–90 days every 6–12 months): progesterone (Pg) stimulates mammary GH → both antagonize insulin
- OHE as soon as feasible (ideally 1–3 days after starting insulin): eliminates Pg source → may allow DM remission in some dogs
- Even if no clear link to diestrus: spay all intact bitches with DM
- Post-spay: monitor closely and adjust insulin dose
- If spay impossible: Pg receptor antagonist aglepristone may reduce insulin resistance

#### "Honeymoon Period"

- Some newly diagnosed dogs respond excellently to small insulin doses (<0.2 U/kg/injection) due to residual beta-cell function
- Ends within months as remaining beta cells die; insulin requirements increase

### Ch 292 — Diabetes Mellitus in Cats

> ClinIQ: ✓ DM (DIS-ENDO-DM, cat) — ENRICHMENTS: ALIVE DM diagnosis criteria, SGLT2 inhibitor doses (bexagliflozin 15 mg/cat q24h, velagliflozin 1 mg/kg q24h), GLP-1RA (exenatide-ER doses), glargine/detemir/degludec pharmacodynamics table, DM remission rates and protocols, CGM usage.

**Authors:** Chen Gilor, Linda Fleeman

#### Diagnosis of DM in Cats (ALIVE Criteria, Box 292.1)

**Diagnosed when:**
1. Random (fasted or unfasted) BG ≥270 mg/dL (≥15 mmol/L) WITH classic clinical signs (PU/PD/PP/weight loss) or hyperglycemic crisis AND at least ONE of:
   - Increased glycated proteins
   - Glucosuria on >1 occasion from home samples ≥2 days after any stressful event
2. OR BG >125 mg/dL to ≤270 mg/dL AND at least TWO of the above criteria

> The potential for stress hyperglycemia warrants caution — stress hyperglycemia cannot exceed ≈270 mg/dL in healthy cats (rare exceptions)

**Renal threshold in cats: ~270 mg/dL (15 mmol/L)**

#### DM Remission in Cats

- **Definition:** Euglycemia with NO insulin therapy for ≥4 weeks when insulin was previously required
- NOT a cure — underlying disease not cured; impaired glucose tolerance persists
- Remission does NOT imply cure
- Factors associated with higher remission rates:
  - Milder/earlier DM (lower BGs, shorter disease duration)
  - Absence of hyperlipidemia
  - Older cats
  - History of glucocorticoid therapy (if discontinued at DM diagnosis)
  - Low carbohydrate diet (canned, 3.5 g/100 kcal carbohydrate): 68% remission vs 41% with higher-carb diet
  - Near-euglycemic management target (BG 60–160 mg/dL): 78% remission vs 14% with traditional paradigm in one retrospective study
- Typical remission rates: 30–50% in academic settings; lower (~13%) in general practice (possible unrecognized HS)
- No difference in remission rates between glargine U100 and PZI in the only RCT (n=46)

- Most insulins: use similar doses (glargine U100 and detemir: median ~2 U/cat q12h in clinical studies)
- Low initial doses recommended to minimize hypoglycemia risk
- **Do not dilute detemir**

#### Non-Insulin Hypoglycemic Agents for Cats (Table 292.3)

| Drug | Target | Effect in Cats | Suggested Dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exenatide-ER | GLP-1RA | Potentiation of insulin secretion (requires residual beta cells), weight loss, slows gastric emptying | 0.13 mg/kg SC q30d OR 0.2 mg/kg SC q7d |
| Glipizide | KATP channel inhibitor | Stimulation of insulin secretion (requires residual beta cells) | 5 mg/cat PO q12h |
| Acarbose | Alpha-glucosidase inhibitor | Decreases intestinal glucose absorption | 12.5 mg/cat PO q12h |
| **Velagliflozin** | SGLT-2 antagonist | Increases renal excretion of glucose | **1 mg/kg PO q24h** |
| **Bexagliflozin** | SGLT-2 antagonist | Increases renal excretion of glucose | **15 mg/cat PO q24h** |
| Metformin | Under investigation | Decreases hepatic gluconeogenesis | 2 mg/kg PO q12h |

> SGLT-2 antagonist SAFETY: Monitor ketones in blood or urine closely for ≥first 7 days and whenever cat becomes unwell — risk of euglycemic or hyperglycemic ketoacidosis. Prompt insulin treatment required if ketosis develops. Only use in clinically well cats.

#### Monitoring

- CGM (FreeStyle Libre) measures interstitial glucose (IGc), not BG — delay between BG and IGc changes exists; absolute values may not match
- **With insulin detemir or glargine U300 (low day-to-day variability):** aggressive dose increases by up to 20–25% every day if IGc consistently >350 mg/dL (19 mmol/L)
- **Once IGc <350 mg/dL:** change doses after 2–3 days minimum (or longer for less consistent insulins)
- Fructosamine: reflects BG over prior 2–3 weeks; lower half of reference range in a diabetic cat = concern for hypoglycemia episodes
- HbA1c (A1Care, Baycom Diagnostics): reflects average BG over prior 70 days; reference interval 1.9–3.1% in healthy cats
- Suspected hypoglycemia: reduce insulin by 20–25% without measuring BG first

#### Diabetic Neuropathy in Cats

- Plantigrade/palmigrade stance, hindlimb weakness
- Usually reversible with 2–3 months of DM treatment

### Ch 293 — Hyperadrenocorticism (Cushing's Syndrome) in Dogs

> ClinIQ: ✓ HAC-dog (DIS-PUPD-HAC) — ENRICHMENTS: complete LDDST protocol + all interpretation patterns verbatim, ACTH stim protocol (5 mcg/kg IV or 250 mcg fixed dose), HDDST, eACTH for PDH/ADH discrimination, UCCR, trilostane doses + monitoring algorithm, mitotane protocol.

**Author:** Sara Galac

#### Prevalence

- HC in ~1–2/1000 dogs per year; PDH ~0.2% of dog population
- PDH: 80–85% of naturally occurring HC cases
- Adrenal-dependent HC (AHC): 15–20%
- Predisposed breeds: Miniature Poodle, Dachshund, Terriers, Boxer, Standard Schnauzer

#### Endocrine Testing Overview

**Two-step process:**
1. Screening test: distinguish HC from non-HC dogs
2. Discrimination test: PDH vs adrenal HC

**Tests:** LDDST (screens + discriminates), UCCR + oral HDDST (screens + discriminates), ACTHst (screens only), eACTH (discriminates only)

> No test is 100% accurate. Cortisol assay methods are NOT interchangeable — use laboratory-specific reference ranges.

#### Low-Dose Dexamethasone Suppression Test (LDDST)

**Protocol:**
- Blood at 0, 4, and 8 hours after IV dexamethasone **0.01 mg/kg**
- Can begin at any time of day; avoid food (lipemic serum interferes with cortisol)
- Sensitivity 85–100%; specificity 44–73%

**Interpretation:**
- **Lab cut-off: 1 or 1.4 mcg/dL (28–39 nmol/L)**
- 8-h cortisol **below** lab cut-off → HC ruled out (unless 4-h is elevated → "inverse pattern" → equivocal, pursue imaging)
- 8-h cortisol **above** lab cut-off → consistent with HC

**Discrimination patterns (in confirmed HC):**
- **Partial suppression pattern:** 4-h or 8-h cortisol suppression >50% from basal → compatible with PDH (68% PPV)
- **Escape pattern:** 4-h cortisol below cut-off AND 8-h above cut-off → equivocal (37% PPV for HC)
- **No suppression:** 4-h AND 8-h above cut-off AND >50% of basal → consistent with HC; does NOT differentiate PDH from AT; positive predictive value for HC = 94%
- Lack of suppression → pursue eACTH and diagnostic imaging

#### UCCR (Urine Cortisol to Creatinine Ratio)

- Urine collected at home (at least 2 days after clinic visit) on 2 consecutive mornings; second sample taken after giving dexamethasone PO 0.1 mg/kg at 12:00, 18:00, and 24:00 hours; third sample collected next morning (~8h after last dex dose)
- Sensitivity 75–100% for HC diagnosis; specificity only 20–25% — high false-positive rate
- When combined with oral HDDST: both screens for HC and may help discriminate PDH from adrenal HC

#### ACTH Stimulation Test (ACTHst) — Dogs

**Protocol:**
- Collect cortisol at baseline and 1 hour after synthetic ACTH
- **Synthetic ACTH dose:** 5 mcg/kg IV or IM; OR **250 mcg/dog (>10 kg) or 125 mcg/dog (<10 kg)**
- No difference between 60- and 90-min samples after 5 mcg/kg IV
- **Do NOT use 1 mcg/kg** — leads to different interpretation in ~25% of dogs
- Aqueous porcine ACTH gel: 2.2 IU/kg IM
- **Do NOT use compounded ACTH preparations** (variable concentrations, may give lower post-ACTH cortisol)
- Sensitivity: 80–83% for PDH; 57–63% for AHC; specificity 59–93%
- Does NOT help differentiate PDH from adrenal HC

**Interpretation for HC screening:**
- Post-ACTH cortisol above reference interval → consistent with HC (interpret in light of other findings)
- Post-ACTH cortisol within reference interval → HC unlikely, but cortisol-secreting AT still possible
- Post-ACTH cortisol below reference interval → HC unlikely; consider exogenous steroid or non-cortisol-secreting AT

#### High-Dose Dexamethasone Suppression Test (HDDST)

- Same protocol as LDDST but at **0.1 mg/kg IV**
- Suppression >50% → PDH
- When LDDST shows no suppression, HDDST provides differentiation in only 12% additional dogs → **HDDST is no longer recommended**; use eACTH and diagnostic imaging instead

#### Endogenous ACTH (eACTH)

- **Most accurate standalone biochemical test for PDH vs AHC discrimination in dogs with proven HC**
- Low or undetectable eACTH → AHC (AT suppressing pituitary via cortisol feedback)
- Low eACTH also characteristic of food-induced HC
- Sample handling CRITICAL: chilled silicon-coated glass/plastic EDTA tubes, centrifuge within 15 min (chilled centrifuge), transfer plasma to plastic, freeze immediately

#### Diagnostic Imaging

**Pituitary:** CT or MRI
- P/B value (pituitary height/brain area) >0.31 mm/mm² = pituitary enlargement
- Microadenoma <10 mm / macroadenoma >10 mm classification (from human medicine) not useful in dogs — avoid

**Adrenals US:** Contralateral atrophy <4–5 mm consistent with AT; bilateral symmetric enlargement with PDH

#### Treatment — Medical

##### Trilostane

**Mechanism:** Competitive inhibitor of 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase → inhibits cortisol + mineralocorticoid synthesis; also possibly affects 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase

**Dose:**
- **PDH: initial 0.5–1 mg/kg PO q12h**
- **Adrenal-dependent HC: initial 0.2–0.5 mg/kg PO q12h**
- Give WITH food; individual dose requirements vary markedly
- Larger dogs generally need lower mg/kg dose
- q12h dosing preferred: duration of cortisol suppression often <12 h; q12h improves response, keeps total daily dose lower, reduces adverse effects

**Time to response:** Weeks for PU/PD/PP; months for skin/coat; coat may initially worsen

**Adverse effects:**
- Transient hypocortisolism (most common; ~15% chance within first 2 years)
- Permanent hypoadrenocorticism (adrenal necrosis — rare but fatal)
- If adverse effects: STOP trilostane; usually resolve quickly; restart at lower dose when HC signs recur

**Monitoring Protocol — Pre-Pill Cortisol Method (Figure 293.6):**
- Day 10: Clinical evaluation only; if well → continue; if unwell → ACTHst + bloodwork
- Day 28: History + PE:
  - HC signs still present:
    - Pre-pill cortisol <1.5 mcg/dL (41 nmol/L): re-evaluate; consider lower dose
    - Pre-pill cortisol 1.5–5 ± 15% mcg/dL: increase dosing frequency or dose
    - Pre-pill cortisol >5 ± 15% mcg/dL: increase dosing frequency or dose
  - HC resolved:
    - Pre-pill cortisol <1.5 mcg/dL: re-evaluate; consider small decrease
    - Pre-pill cortisol 1.5–5 ± 15% mcg/dL: continue
- Clinically unwell: measure pre- and post-ACTHst cortisol 2–4 hours after trilostane

**Monitoring Protocol — ACTHst Method (Figure 293.7):**
- Perform ACTHst **2–4 hours after** trilostane administration
- Post-ACTH cortisol targets and actions:
  - Clinical signs of hypocortisolism (poor appetite, vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, weakness):
    - Post-ACTH <1.5 mcg/dL → discontinue trilostane; start supportive/symptomatic care; consider glucocorticoids
    - Post-ACTH >1.5 mcg/dL → unlikely hypocortisolism; investigate other diseases; restart when well
  - Well controlled (normal appetite, no PU/PD):
    - Post-ACTH 1.5–5.5 mcg/dL → dosage unchanged
    - Post-ACTH <1.5 mcg/dL → discontinue trilostane 5–7 days; restart at reduced dose
    - Post-ACTH >5.5 mcg/dL → increase dosage; dosage unchanged + monitor if short duration suspected
  - HC signs still present (polyphagia, PU/PD, panting):
    - Post-ACTH >5.5 mcg/dL → increase dose
    - Post-ACTH 1.5–5.5 mcg/dL → short duration suspected; consider more frequent administration; dosage unchanged + monitor for recurrence
- Unit conversion: 1.5 mcg/dL = 41 nmol/L; 5.5 mcg/dL = 152 nmol/L

**Prognosis with trilostane:**
- PDH: median survival 549–662 days (once daily); ~852 days (once or twice daily)
- AT: trilostane is palliative only (does not affect tumor growth); median survival 353–596 days

##### Mitotane (o,p'-DDD)

**Preferred for adrenal-dependent HC** (can destroy AT cells); largely replaced by trilostane for PDH

**PDH protocol (non-selective):**
- Daily doses of **50–75 mg/kg PO** for 5 days, then q48h for 40 days
- Small breeds may require up to 100 mg/kg PO
- Divide daily doses into 3–4 portions; give WITH food
- Replacement therapy begins day 3: cortisone acetate 2 mg/kg q12h, fludrocortisone acetate 0.0125 mg/kg q12h, NaCl 0.1 g/kg q12h
- After initial course: reduce glucocorticoid to 0.5–1 mg/kg cortisone acetate; double for 1–2 days in injury/stress/anesthesia
- Maintenance: mitotane at initial daily dose once weekly for ≥6 months (or lifelong)

**Monitoring:** UCCR after day 45 of initial course and q6 months; Na and K assessment for mineralocorticoid dosing

##### Treatment at Pituitary Level

- **Hypophysectomy:** Transsphenoidal approach; 91% alive at 4 weeks; 92% remission; disease recurrence in 27%. Median survival 781 days (range 0–3808). Life-long replacement: glucocorticoids, thyroxine; desmopressin needed temporarily in all, permanently in ~10%.
- **Radiation:** Conventional 36–48 Gy in 3–4 Gy fractions (12 anesthesias); median survival 539–1405 days; does not eliminate ACTH hypersecretion
- **Adrenalectomy:** Treatment of choice for solitary AT; perioperative mortality 6–8% (non-invasive) to 24% (invasive); median survival 778–953 days

### Ch 294 — Feline Hyperadrenocorticism

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: DIS-ENDO-FHAC) — Feline HAC is distinct from canine, warrants separate entry: skin fragility, 79% concurrent DM, unique testing cutoffs, limited treatment evidence.

**Authors:** Ian K. Ramsey, Michael E. Herrtage

#### Overview

- Less common than in dogs; worse prognosis; greater challenges to diagnose and treat
- PDH: ~85% of fHAC; ADH: ~15%
- **79% have concurrent DM (often insulin-resistant, requiring ~2 U/kg/day)**
- Skin fragility syndrome (spontaneous tearing) — handle cats with great care
- No calcinosis cutis reported in naturally occurring fHAC

#### Clinical Findings (Approximate Frequency)

| Finding | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Concurrent DM | 79% |
| PU/PD | 79% |
| Polyphagia | 60% |
| Abdominal distension | 54% |
| Alopecia/failure to regrow hair | 43% |
| Skin hyperfragility/spontaneous tearing | 40% |
| Weight loss/muscle atrophy | 31% |

#### Lab Findings

- Cats lack steroid-induced ALP isoenzyme → only ~30% have mild ALP/ALT increase (vs dogs)
- Hyperglycemia >91%, glucosuria >87%, proteinuria 79%
- "Stress leukogram" (neutrophilia, lymphopenia, eosinopenia) NOT common in cats
- Systemic hypertension: ~20% (lower than in dogs)

#### Endocrine Testing — Cats (Table 294.3)

| Test | Criterion for fHAC | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|
| ACTHst | 60 or 90 min post-ACTH cortisol **>400 nmol/L (>14.5 mcg/dL)** | 71% (28/39) |
| LDDST 0.01 mg/kg (canine dose) | 8-h post-dex cortisol >27 nmol/L (1 mcg/dL) | 96% (but low specificity — 20% healthy cats don't suppress!) |
| DST 0.1 mg/kg (cat dose) | 8-h post-dex cortisol >27 nmol/L (1 mcg/dL) | 84% (38/45) |
| eACTH | >20 pg/mL (4.4 pmol/L) = PDH | 73% (14/19) |
| Adrenal US | Bilateral adrenomegaly (PDH) or adrenal mass (ADH) | 92% (49/53) |

**ACTHst Protocol — Cats:**
- 5 mcg/kg or 0.125 mg/cat tetracosactrin IV; samples at 0, 60, and 90 minutes
- Post-ACTH cortisol **>400 nmol/L (14.5 mcg/dL)** = very likely fHAC
- Values between 400–600 nmol/L = supportive of fHAC
- Values >600 nmol/L = very suggestive
- Cats with fHAC rarely have post-ACTH cortisol >800 nmol/L (>29 mcg/dL)
- Post-ACTH cortisol <40 nmol/L (<1.4 mcg/dL) = consider iatrogenic fHAC
- Normal result does NOT rule out fHAC

**DST Protocol — Cats (screening):**
- **Dose: 0.1 mg/kg IV** (higher than canine dose); samples at 0, 3, and 8 hours
- 8-h cortisol **>40 nmol/L (1.4 mcg/dL)** = fHAC very likely
- 8-h cortisol **<40 nmol/L** = fHAC unlikely or iatrogenic
- **Canine LDDST dose (0.01 mg/kg) should NOT be used to screen for fHAC** — 20% of healthy cats don't suppress → too many false positives

**eACTH for PDH vs ADH differentiation:** Normal or elevated in PDH; low or undetectable in ADH

#### Treatment

**Surgery:**
- Unilateral adrenalectomy: treatment of choice for ADH; DM may remit in months post-surgery; remaining diabetic cats often need much lower insulin doses
- Bilateral adrenalectomy for PDH: requires lifelong prednisolone + fludrocortisone
- Hypophysectomy: definitive treatment for PDH; limited availability

**Trilostane (medical):**
- **1–2 mg/kg q24h OR 0.5–1 mg/kg q12h** (can give when insulin is given)
- Reformulation often necessary
- Monitoring: ACTHst performed 4h after trilostane (extrapolated from dogs)
  - Target post-ACTHst cortisol: 40 nmol/L (1.4 mcg/dL) to 140 nmol/L (5 mcg/dL) — 4h after trilostane
  - Note: ACTHst unreliable predictor of clinical status in dogs; same limitation applies to cats
- In 9 cats with fHAC+DM treated with trilostane: insulin dose decreased average 36% in 6 cats; 1 needed more insulin; none went into DM remission
- Important complications: anorexia, lethargy, hypernatremia, hypokalemia

**Mitotane:** NOT effective in fHAC in cats
**Ketoconazole:** Ineffective in cats
**Metyrapone:** Possibly useful for presurgical stabilization (65 mg/kg PO q12h in one case)

#### Prognosis

- Progressive disorder; poor prognosis overall
- Appropriate therapy: cats with adrenal adenomas or PDH may have good to excellent prognosis
- Complete DM remission may occur after successful definitive HAC treatment

### Ch 295 — Non–Cortisol-Secreting Adrenocortical Tumors

> ClinIQ: ✓ Primary Hyperaldosteronism/Conn (DIS-ENDO-CONN) — ENRICHMENTS: PAC measurement, ARR, fludrocortisone suppression test, perioperative management, spironolactone dose, amlodipine dose, hypoK K supplementation dose; also covers sex hormone-secreting ATs (MISSING candidate) and incidentaloma algorithm.

**Authors:** Ellen N. Behrend, Brett Wasik

#### Incidentaloma (IAGM — "Incidental" Adrenal Gland Mass)

- Incidence: ~4% of dogs on US; ~9% on CT; cats: unknown
- 27% of canine and 60% of feline ATs are metastatic lesions (most common primaries: pulmonary, mammary, prostatic, gastric, pancreatic carcinomas, melanoma)
- Management algorithm: re-verify on repeated US; then assess for hormonal function and malignancy
- Malignancy indicators: >2 cm diameter, vascular invasion, heterogeneous appearance, metastases
- If non-malignant, non-functional, <2 cm: US monitoring monthly × 3 months, then q3 months

#### Primary Hyperaldosteronism (PHA) — Cats

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common feline adrenocortical disorder
- Most common in cats >10 years (median ~13 years); no sex or breed predisposition
- Causes: idiopathic bilateral micronodular hyperplasia OR adenoma OR adenocarcinoma
  - Adrenocortical carcinomas more frequent than adenomas in cats (differs from humans)

**Clinical Features:**
- HypoK most common abnormality — moderate to severe; muscle weakness at K <2.5–3 mmol/L
- Clinical signs of hypoK: cervical ventroflexion, hindlimb weakness (sometimes plantigrade stance), difficulty jumping, listlessness, ataxia, forelimb stiffness, collapse, dysphagia
- ~85% persistently hypertensive
- About 50% have increased BUN, creatinine, and CK (CK can be >100,000 U/L)
- Mild metabolic alkalosis from increased acid excretion
- HypoP or hypoMg may develop
- Usually NOT hypernatremic (water retention dilutes Na)

**Diagnosis:**
- HypoK + hypertension → strongly consider PHA
- Low normal serum phosphorus in azotemic cat → suspicious (hyperphosphatemia expected with CKD)
- **Plasma Aldosterone Concentration (PAC): elevated in ALL reported cats with PHA due to AT**
  - PAC >1000 pmol/L also seen in some secondary HA (especially CKD) — overlap exists
  - Serial testing may be needed if PAC is in upper normal range
- **Plasma Renin Activity (PRA): should be within or below reference range in PHA**
  - Elevated PRA in secondary HA (CKD, heart disease, hepatic failure)
- **Aldosterone:Renin Ratio (ARR): sensitive initial screening test** (same as humans)
  - PRA assays not widely commercially available for cats; require large (4 mL) samples, rapid separation, frozen plasma
  - A single ARR within reference interval does NOT exclude PHA in cats
- Imaging: US, CT, MRI for adrenal mass; CT more sensitive for metastasis

**Diagnosis confirmation (all required):**
1. Elevated PAC
2. Identification of AT on imaging
3. (Optional confirmation) Histological confirmation, resolution of clinical signs, normalization of PAC post-adrenalectomy

**Medical Treatment (when surgery not possible):**
- Spironolactone **2 mg/kg PO q12h** (mineralocorticoid receptor blocker)
- K supplementation **2–6 mEq q12h** for hypoK
- Amlodipine besylate **0.625–1.25 mg/cat PO q24h** for hypertension
- Medical management: survival 7 months to 984 days in 4 cats (one surviving only 50 days due to poor owner compliance)

**Surgical Treatment:**
- Unilateral adrenalectomy: treatment of choice (if no obvious metastases)
- Perioperative: correct hypoK pre-operatively (oral and/or parenteral K)
- Monitor electrolytes frequently post-operatively; IV fluids to maintain K and Na in reference range
- Exogenous mineralocorticoids usually NOT needed post-operatively
- If cortisol was co-secreted: anticipate hypocortisolemia → ACTHst 6–8 h post-op to assess remaining gland
- Prognosis: median survival 1297 days (2–1582 days) in one study; no significant difference adenoma vs adenocarcinoma

#### PHA — Dogs

- Rare in dogs; usually solitary adrenal adenoma or carcinoma
- Most dogs with aldosteronoma: aldosterone concentrations **>3000 pmol/L (>108 ng/dL)**
- Treatment: unilateral adrenalectomy + perioperative management same as cats
- If hypoK + hypoNa persist >72 h post-op: DOCP (one dose) then taper oral fludrocortisone within a week

#### Sex Hormone-Secreting Adrenocortical Tumors

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: DIS-ENDO-SEXHORMONE-AT)

- Cats: progesterone-secreting tumors → signs mimicking cortisol excess (skin fragility, DM)
- Diagnosis: cortisol and sex hormones before and 1h after ACTH stimulation (125 mcg cosyntropin/cat IV)
  - If progestin secreted but not cortisol → cortisols at or below low-end of reference range
  - Estradiol does NOT increase with ACTH injection
- Dogs: sex hormone-secreting AT suspected when cortisol below reference range in dog with Cushing's signs (iatrogenic first excluded)
- Treatment: adrenalectomy is treatment of choice

### Ch 296 — Hypoadrenocorticism (Addison's Disease)

> ClinIQ: ✓ Hypoadrenocorticism/Addison (DIS-SEC-HYPO) — ENRICHMENTS: complete ACTHst protocol (1 mcg/kg cat vs 5 mcg/kg dog, or 250 mcg fixed dog), post-ACTH cutoffs, Na:K ratio cutoffs, baseline cortisol screening cutoff, DOCP dosing algorithm, prednisone doses, fludrocortisone initial dose.

**Author:** Rebecka S. Hess

#### Pathophysiology

- Most common: immune-mediated destruction of adrenal cortex (auto-antibodies against 21-hydroxylase and P450 enzymes)
- Atypical primary HA: glucocorticoid absent but Na/K normal; some progress to classic HA, others maintain normal electrolytes for years
- Secondary HA: pituitary/hypothalamic failure → impaired ACTH/CRH secretion; only glucocorticoid deficiency (RAAS intact); ACTH concentrations low

#### Predisposed Breeds

Standard Poodle, Leonberger, Bearded Collie, Great Dane, Portuguese Water Dog, Cocker Spaniel, Springer Spaniel, Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever

#### Diagnosis

**Na:K Ratio:**
- **<27 (or 28): ~95% of dogs correctly identified as having or not having HA**
- Na:K **≤24**: high likelihood of HA confirmed by ACTHst
- Na:K should be about 30 in treated HA; no lower than 28 and no higher than 32

**CBC clues:**
- Lymphocytes ≥2.0 × 10³/mcL in ill dogs → 85% specific for HA
- Lack of "stress leukogram" (instead: lymphocytosis, eosinophilia) in sick dog → suspicious for HA

**Baseline Cortisol:**
- **Basal cortisol >2 mcg/dL (>55 nmol/L): unlikely to have HA** — useful SCREENING tool (especially in dogs with normal Na:K and only suspected glucocorticoid dysfunction)
- Basal cortisol <1 mcg/dL: most have HA, but NOT diagnostic alone

**ACTHst — GOLD STANDARD:**

*Dog protocol:*
- Cortisol at baseline and 1 hour after **1 mcg/kg synthetic short-acting ACTH IV** (preferred)
- Alternate IV or IM; if severely dehydrated → IV preferred (SQ may not be absorbed)
- Synthetic ACTH: refrigerate up to 60 days in humans (manufacturer recommends ≤14 days); can freeze at −20°C in plastic syringes for up to 6 months

*Interpretation (dogs):*
- Normal: post-ACTH cortisol >2 mcg/dL (>55 nmol/L); pre-ACTH 0.5–6 mcg/dL
- **HA diagnosis:** pre- and post-ACTH cortisol **<1 mcg/dL (<28 nmol/L)** in most dogs
- Post-ACTH 1–2 mcg/dL (28–55 nmol/L): diagnose HA only with caution
- **HA diagnosis excluded if serum cortisol after ACTH >5 mcg/dL (>139 nmol/L)**

> Note: Table 296.1 (n=53 HA dogs vs 110 non-HA ill dogs): HA diagnosed at cortisol ≤1.0 mcg/dL 1h post-ACTH; excluded at basal cortisol >2.0 mcg/dL OR post-ACTH >5.0 mcg/dL

*Cat protocol:* Different — see Ch 294 (fHAC chapter), ACTHst dose 5 mcg/kg IV

**Secondary HA vs Primary HA:**
- Endogenous ACTH: low in secondary HA; extremely elevated in primary HA
- Na and K: normal in secondary HA (RAAS intact)

**Adrenal US:** Thickness usually <3 mm in HA

#### Treatment — Acute Crisis

**Fluid therapy — SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT TREATMENT:**
- 0.9% NaCl (contains more Na and Cl, less K than balanced crystalloids) — preferred
- Hyperkalemia typically resolves with IV 0.9% saline
- Monitor Na and K every 2–6 hours; hypoNa correction: no faster than 1–2 mEq/L per 2–3 hours, no more than 8–12 mEq/L per 24 hours

**Glucocorticoids during acute crisis:**
- Usually NOT necessary initially — IV saline alone is often lifesaving and allows ACTHst to be completed
- **If glucocorticoids needed BEFORE ACTHst:** use **dexamethasone** (not detected by cortisol assay); can be given IV with rapid onset
- Avoid oral prednisone ≥48 h before ACTHst

**If severe hyperK persists after 6–8 h of fluid therapy or profound bradycardia:**
- IV glucose: stimulates endogenous insulin secretion → shifts K intracellularly
- If glucose alone insufficient AND BG >200 mg/dL (>11.1 mmol/L): add short-acting IV insulin
  - Monitor BG every 2 hours to prevent hypoglycemia
  - Severe hypoglycemia (BG <60 mg/dL): 5% dextrose or 0.25–0.5 g/kg dextrose (diluted 1:3) IV

**DOCP (acute):**
- If rehydrated dog remains hyperK and hypoNa and other major diagnoses excluded: **SQ DOCP 1.5 mg/kg**
- DOCP appears benign if administered to dog not in need

**Hydrocortisone sodium succinate:** 0.5–0.625 mg/kg/hr (reported treatment option)

**Bicarbonate:** Generally NOT indicated; if severe acidosis (pH <7.1) not correcting with fluids:
- Give in increments of ¼ × (0.3 × base deficit × body weight in kg) every 20 min while monitoring venous pH

#### Long-Term Treatment

**Mineralocorticoids:**

*DOCP (desoxycorticosterone pivalate):*
- **Labeled dose: 2.2 mg/kg SQ every 25 days**
- **Initial recommended dose: ~1.5 mg/kg SQ** (lower than labeled)
- Young growing dogs: ~1.7 mg/kg initially
- Most dogs ultimately require dose reductions
- Dosing interval can often be extended to ~once monthly; some dogs every 2–3 months

*DOCP Dose Adjustment Algorithm (Figure 296.5):*
- Na:K on day 14: if >32 → reduce next dose by ~10% (e.g., 1.5 → 1.35 mg/kg)
- Na:K on day 25: if >32 → postpone next injection by ≥5 days
- Na:K on day 30: if >32 → postpone next injection by ≥5 days
- Na:K on day 35: if still >32 → postpone further; if at or below 32 → proceed with injection
- Target Na:K: ~30 (range 28–32)
- Check Na:K at mid-interval when extending interval

*Fludrocortisone:*
- **Initial dose: 0.01 mg/kg q12h** (oral)
- Adjust based on Na:K at days 5, 14, and 30 after first dose; titrate until Na:K ~30
- Some dogs develop iatrogenic hyperadrenocorticism at doses required for electrolyte control

**Glucocorticoids:**

*Prednisone/prednisolone (most commonly used):*
- Immediately post-crisis: **0.5 mg/kg q12h × 2–3 days**
- Then taper to physiologic dose: **0.1–0.2 mg/kg q12–24h**
- Many dogs require even lower doses over time
- Owners should have extra supply for stress (travel, kennel, guests): give 0.5 mg/kg as needed

*Fludrocortisone (combined mineral + glucocorticoid):*
- Contains both; may be difficult to balance both hormones with single drug
- Some dogs develop signs of iatrogenic hyperadrenocorticism at doses required for electrolytes

#### Monitoring — Long Term

- After starting treatment: ACTHst no longer useful
- Glucocorticoid dose: based on owner opinion (resolve vomiting/diarrhea/poor appetite/weight loss; avoid PU/PD/polyphagia)
- Mineralocorticoid: objective, based on Na:K ratio
- Na:K target: ~30, not lower than 28, not higher than 32
- Monitor Na:K every few months even in well-regulated dogs
- Atypical HA: monitor Na:K over time — some progress to mineralocorticoid deficiency

#### Prognosis

- **Excellent** with successful acute crisis management and established diagnosis
- Most HA dogs requiring IV fluid therapy discharged within ~48 hours

#### Feline Hypoadrenocorticism

- Extremely rare; similar clinical signs/PE/lab findings to dogs
- Treat with glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid after acute crisis managed
- **Prognosis appears worse than in dogs**

### Ch 297 — Gastrointestinal Endocrinology (Gastrinoma, Glucagonoma)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: DIS-ENDO-GASTRINOMA, DIS-ENDO-GLUCAGONOMA) — rare neuroendocrine tumors with specific clinical syndromes, diagnostic tests (serum gastrin + gastric pH), provocative testing, and pathology.

**Author:** Thomas Schermerhorn

#### Gastrinoma

**Overview:**
- Uncommon tumor; <50 cases in veterinary literature (mostly dogs, few cats)
- Mean age at diagnosis: ~8 years dogs; slightly older cats
- Zollinger-Ellison syndrome: gastric antral hypertrophy, hyperacidity, ulceration
- About 85% have metastases at diagnosis (lymph nodes, mesentery, peritoneal surfaces, spleen, liver)

**Clinical Signs:**
- Vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss (most common)
- Apparent abdominal pain, GI bleeding, polydipsia, constipation (less frequent)
- Reflux esophagitis/regurgitation, esophageal colic
- Gastric mucosal hypertrophy, ulceration; in severe cases, outflow obstruction

**Laboratory:**
- No specific CBC or biochemistry findings
- Nonspecific: leukocytosis, neutrophilia with left shift, anemia, panhypoproteinemia secondary to ulceration
- Also: hypoalbuminemia, hypokalemia, hypochloremia, hyponatremia, metabolic alkalosis, hyperbilirubinemia, increased liver enzymes

**Diagnosis:**

*Serum Gastrin:*
- In people: **serum gastrin >1000 ng/L together with gastric pH <2.5 = diagnostic for gastrinoma**
- In dogs/cats: serum gastrin in confirmed gastrinomas well above control values but no specific cutoffs established
- Increased serum gastrin is NOT specific: also elevated in kidney disease, gastropathies, hepatopathies, proton pump inhibitor use

*Provocative testing — Secretin stimulation test:*
- Protocol: serum samples at 0, 2, 5, 10, and 20 min after IV secretin **2–4 U/kg**
  (alternative: 0, 2, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min after 2 U/kg)
- Samples at 2 and 5 min most useful (tumor response rapid and short-lived)
- In people AND dogs with gastrinoma: ~2-fold increase in gastrin following secretin injection
- Not all dogs with gastrinoma demonstrate >2-fold increase

*Provocative testing — Calcium stimulation test:*
- Protocol: serum samples at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 90 min after IV Ca infusion **2 mg/kg IV over 1 min**
- Maximum gastrin secretion at 60 min; in people and dogs: typically ~2-fold increase

*Imaging:* Abdominal US more sensitive than radiography for pancreatic mass; contrast radiography may show rugal fold hypertrophy, antral narrowing, mucosal defects

**Treatment:** Surgical removal + supportive treatment for gastric acid suppression (proton pump inhibitors)

#### Glucagonoma

**Overview:**
- Extremely rare: only a few dogs, one cat
- Arises from neuroendocrine alpha cells in pancreatic islets
- Autonomous glucagon → hepatic gluconeogenesis/glycogenolysis, insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, may cause DM
- Necrolytic migratory erythema (NME; superficial necrolytic dermatitis): pathognomonic in people; seen with glucagonoma in dogs but more commonly caused by liver disease in dogs

**Clinical Signs:**
- Middle-aged to older dogs; nonspecific signs (lethargy, decreased appetite)
- Non-healing skin erosions/ulcers; distribution suggests possible autoimmune component
- Lesions at lips, nose, ears, muzzle, paws, ventrum, inguinal region, extremities
- If DM present: hyperglycemia resistant to insulin (requires high doses)

**Lab:**
- Increased ALT, ALP; decreased albumin, BUN, cholesterol
- Similar to primary liver disease (which is more common cause of NME in dogs)
- Bile acids + blood ammonia: usually normal in glucagonoma; important to differentiate from liver disease

**Diagnosis:**
- Serum glucagon: 1.5–15× upper reference limit; **>1000 ng/L considered diagnostic in humans**; no canine cutoff established
- No commercially available canine/feline glucagon assay widely available
- Plasma amino acids: arginine, histidine, lysine decreased in glucagonoma
- CT: identified mass in one dog; US may show liver abnormalities (not specific)

**Treatment:** Surgical removal (definitive); octreotide used in one dog for NME management

### Ch 298 — Pheochromocytoma

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: DIS-ENDO-PHEO) — distinct entity with specific biochemical diagnosis (urinary normetanephrine), imaging workup, preoperative phenoxybenzamine protocol, and surgical considerations.

**Author:** Eric Zini

#### Overview

- Neuroendocrine tumor arising from chromaffin cells of adrenal medulla
- Accounts for ~0.01–0.1% of all canine tumors; many undiagnosed (episodic/nonspecific signs)
- >90% unilateral; average diameter 5 cm (up to 15 cm) at diagnosis
- Local invasion and metastases reported in 66% and 20% of dogs, respectively
- Vena cava invasion: up to 56% (usually right-sided)
- About 50% of dogs with PCC have a second endocrine or nonendocrine tumor
- No breed, sex, or age predisposition identified

#### Biochemical Diagnosis (Key)

**Urinary normetanephrine is the diagnostic test of choice:**
- **Only parameter significantly higher in dogs with PCC than in healthy dogs AND dogs with adrenocortical or pituitary tumors**
- Only result unaffected by stress of veterinary manipulation during sample collection
- Expressed as ratio to creatinine
- Cut-off: 4× upper limit of normal → excellent specificity but poor sensitivity (many cases missed)
- **Cut-off of 2–3× upper limit of normal balances sensitivity and specificity**
- Metanephrine preferred over catecholamines (secreted continuously vs episodically)
- Normetanephrine preferred over metanephrine (higher sensitivity in plasma and urine)

**Sample handling (critical):**
- Urine: acidify to pH ≤2 after sampling; store frozen at −20°C (−4°F)
- Plasma: deliver to laboratory on dry ice
- Some medications affect results: phenoxybenzamine and dexamethasone → increased levels (may be given after adrenal mass detected); trilostane does NOT significantly decrease urinary catecholamines/metanephrines

**Urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA):creatinine ratio:** moderate to high sensitivity and specificity for PCC in dogs

**Serum inhibin:** usually undetectable in PCC, detectable in adrenocortical tumors — useful differentiator (neutered dogs only)

#### Imaging

- **US:** 80% sensitivity, 90% specificity for vena cava thrombus; phrenicoabdominal vein invasion often missed; heterogeneous appearance with foci of necrosis/hemorrhage in larger tumors
- **CT (preferred):** 92% sensitivity, 100% specificity for vascular invasion; superior to US for size, architecture, invasion; nonionic contrast media preferred (ionic media may trigger hypertensive crisis)
- **MRI:** useful for vascular invasion; isointense/slightly hypointense T1, slightly/markedly hyperintense T2
- **Caution with FNA/biopsy:** 10% complication rate in dogs; in humans, up to 70% serious complications; only if PCC cannot be excluded beforehand

#### Treatment

##### Surgical (Definitive)

**Preoperative Phenoxybenzamine:**
- Recommended before adrenalectomy (phenoxybenzamine-treated dogs 6× more likely to survive vs untreated in one study)
- Non-competitive, non-selective, long-acting alpha-adrenergic blocker
- **Starting dose: 0.25 mg/kg PO q12h**
- Increase gradually every 2–3 days, reaching **1–1.5 mg/kg PO q12h**
- Treat for **10–14 days before surgery** (both hypertensive and normotensive dogs)
- Side-effects: hypotension (lethargy, inappetence, vague signs), vomiting, tachycardia
- Owners: stop medication and call veterinarian if side-effects

**Additional Preoperative Medications:**
- Beta-adrenergic blocker (selective beta-1): atenolol (NOT non-selective propranolol unless after alpha-blockade established)
- Calcium-channel blocker (amlodipine) may be added — reduces required phenoxybenzamine dose

**Adrenalectomy:**
- Laparoscopic: preferred for small-medium PCC without vena cava invasion
- Laparotomy/celiotomy: for large tumors or with vascular invasion; cavotomy possible by experienced surgeons

**Perioperative monitoring:**
- Continuous ECG + blood pressure throughout surgery and for 48 hours post-op
- Life-threatening catecholamine-induced complications possible: hypertension, tachycardia, arrhythmias
- Significant hypotension after tumor removal (sudden catecholamine drop)

##### Medical Management (Long-Term)

- When surgery not possible: long-term alpha-adrenergic blockade with phenoxybenzamine
- Add calcium-channel blocker or selective beta-adrenergic blocker if needed for residual hypertension/tachycardia

#### Prognosis

- Favorable if: tumor removable, no metastases, no other concurrent diseases, perioperative complications avoided → long-term survival months to several years
- Dogs on chronic phenoxybenzamine without adrenalectomy: can live >1 year after diagnosis
- Key survival predictors: absence of intraoperative arrhythmias + short duration of surgery
- Tumor location, duration of anesthesia, and perioperative hypertension do NOT independently affect survival in studies
- Right-sided tumors: no significant difference in postoperative survival vs left-sided

#### Feline Pheochromocytoma

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate: DIS-ENDO-PHEO-CAT) — rare, only 6 confirmed + 2 suspected cases; circulating free normetanephrine elevated; adrenalectomy treatment of choice.

- Extremely rare (<10% of all feline adrenal neoplasms; only 6 confirmed + 2 suspected cases)
- Clinical signs comparable to dogs (nonspecific)
- Circulating free normetanephrine (not metanephrine) markedly higher in suspected feline PCC vs healthy cats and cats with non-adrenal disease
- Adrenalectomy: treatment of choice; no detailed perioperative management information available for cats
