### Ch 299 — Clinical Approach and Laboratory Evaluation of Renal Disease
*(Authors: Harriet M. Syme, Rosanne Jepson)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Chronic Kidney Disease (DIS-SEC-CKD) — enrichments: full IRIS staging, USG thresholds, biopsy indications, UPCR interpretation, SDMA nuances.
> ClinIQ: ✓ Acute Kidney Injury (DIS-SEC-AKI) — enrichments: prerenal/renal/postrenal distinction by USG.

#### Prerenal vs Renal Azotemia — USG Thresholds
- USG **>1.030 dog** or **>1.035 cat** → usually prerenal. Some cats with CKD retain concentrating ability (>1.035–1.040) despite azotemia.
- Maximally concentrated urine in dehydrated/prerenal normal animal: **>1.030 dog, >1.040 cat**.
- Isosthenuria (USG 1.008–1.015, ~300 mOsm/kg): seen in health, AKI, and CKD.
- Hyposthenuria (USG <1.008): indicates active tubular dilution → investigate non-renal PU/PD causes.
- Prerenal confirmed by IV fluid administration and documenting resolution of azotemia. (Ch 299)

#### Surrogate GFR Markers

**Creatinine**
- Exponential relationship with GFR; less sensitive near-normal GFR (Figure 299.3).
- Produced from dehydration of creatine/dephosphorylation of phosphocreatine in muscle.
- Fasting 8–12 h recommended; affected by lean body mass; very weakly secreted in canine renal tubules.
- Serial trending with same laboratory analyser enhances utility in early renal disease. (Ch 299)

**SDMA (Symmetric Dimethylarginine)**
- Synthesized by all nucleated cells; primarily excreted by renal filtration.
- Exponential relationship with GFR almost identical to creatinine; linear relationship between 1/SDMA and GFR.
- Not influenced by body muscle mass or recent food ingestion (fasting still recommended).
- Incorporated into IRIS staging (Table 301.1); especially important for IRIS Stage 1 and sarcopenic patients.
- Confounders: thyroid status, neoplasia, diabetes mellitus; some cats with normal kidneys have markedly elevated SDMA. (Ch 299)

**Normal GFR Reference Values**
- Dogs: **3.5–4.5 mL/min/kg**
- Cats: **2.5–3.5 mL/min/kg** (Ch 299)

#### Proteinuria Assessment

**UPCR Thresholds**
- Persistent UPCR **>0.4 cats**, **>0.5 dogs** = abnormal (urine sediment must be inactive).
- UPCR **>2.0** = strongly suggestive of underlying glomerular disease.
- Day-to-day variability: UPCR must change **≥35%** (at UPCR ~12) or **≥80%** (at UPCR ~0.5) to reflect true change.
- For UPCR <4.0: 1 sample sufficient in dogs. For UPCR >4.0: average of >2 samples; pooled 3-sample equivolume urine is cost-effective. (Ch 299)

**IRIS Substaging by Proteinuria** *(see Table 301.1 below for complete table)*
| Substage | Dog UPC | Cat UPC |
|---|---|---|
| Proteinuric (P) | >0.5 | >0.4 |
| Borderline Proteinuric (BP) | 0.2–0.5 | 0.2–0.4 |
| Nonproteinuric (NP) | <0.2 | <0.2 |

#### Renal Biopsy

**Indications (current IRIS guidelines):**
- Persistent substantial proteinuria (UPCR **>3.5**)
- Proteinuria unresponsive to anti-proteinuric therapy
- Progressive worsening proteinuria or declining renal function despite therapy
- To determine if immunosuppressive therapy is indicated (Ch 302)

**Contraindications:**
- IRIS Stage 4 CKD
- Primary tubulointerstitial disease
- Hydronephrosis, pyelonephritis, hemostatic disorders, renal abscessation
- Little benefit in amyloidosis or hereditary nephropathy (not immunosuppressant-responsive). (Ch 299)

**Pre-biopsy:** Discontinue anti-thrombotic therapy ≥72 h before; assess hemostasis (platelet count, coagulation profile, TEG, buccal mucosal bleeding time). (Ch 299)

#### Glucose Renal Thresholds
- **Glucosuria threshold:** >180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L) dogs; >280 mg/dL (15.5 mmol/L) cats.
- Euglycemic glucosuria = renal tubular dysfunction (primary renal glucosuria or Fanconi syndrome). (Ch 299)

#### Urine Casts (clinical significance)
- WBC casts: pyelonephritis, glomerulonephritis.
- RBC casts: vasculitis or glomerulonephritis.
- Epithelial casts: acute tubular necrosis.
- Large numbers granular/epithelial casts: indicative of acute tubular necrosis.
- Hyaline or rare granular casts: may be normal. (Ch 299)

#### Biomarkers of Renal Disease (Table 299.1)
Key novel markers (research only unless noted):
- **NGAL** (25 kDa, urine/blood, dog/cat): upregulated with direct renal injury; confounded by UTI/lower urinary tract inflammation.
- **KIM-1** (104 kDa, urine/blood, dog/cat): transmembrane glycoprotein upregulated with ischemic/toxic PTC injury.
- **Clusterin** (60–80 kDa, urine, dog/cat): renal-specific glycoprotein upregulated with direct injury.
- **Cystatin B** (11 kDa, urine/blood, dog/cat): intracellular; indicates direct renal injury. [Commercial/Research]
- **FGF23** (32 kDa, blood, dog/cat): phosphaturic hormone; increased early in CKD mineral and bone disorder. [Research/becoming commercial]
- GGT/creatinine ratio: commercially available; used for gentamicin nephrotoxicosis monitoring.

### Ch 300 — Acute Kidney Injury
*(Author: JD Foster)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Acute Kidney Injury (DIS-SEC-AKI) — enrichments: full IRIS AKI grading table (I–V), AKD staging, drug dose table (Table 300.7), oliguria algorithm, leptospirosis detail.

#### IRIS AKI Grading (Table 300.1)

| AKI Grade | Serum Creatinine | Clinical Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Grade I** | <1.6 mg/dL (<140 mcmol/L) | Non-azotemic AKI. Historical/clinical/lab/imaging evidence of AKI; progressive non-azotemic ↑ Cr ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.4 mcmol/L) within 48 h; or ≥6-h duration oligoanuria |
| **Grade II** | 1.7–2.5 mg/dL (141–220 mcmol/L) | Mild AKI. Documented AKI with static or progressive azotemia; progressive ↑ Cr ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 h; or ≥6-h oligoanuria |
| **Grade III** | 2.6–5.0 mg/dL (221–439 mcmol/L) | Moderate to severe AKI |
| **Grade IV** | 5.1–10 mg/dL (440–880 mcmol/L) | Severe AKI |
| **Grade V** | >10.0 mg/dL (>880 mcmol/L) | Severe AKI |

*Each grade sub-graded by UOP (non-oligoanuric vs. oligoanuric) and need for renal replacement therapy.*
*Unit conversion: mg/dL × 88 = mcmol/L.* (Ch 300)

#### Acute Kidney Disease (AKD) — Temporal Definitions
- **AKI**: injury over ≤7 days
- **AKD**: persistent decrease in kidney function >7 days (analogous to people)
- **CKD**: decrease in kidney function persisting ≥3 months
- AKD Stage 1 subtypes:
  - **1C**: creatinine remains above baseline 7 days after AKI diagnosis (no demonstrable recovery)
  - **1B**: creatinine normalizes but biomarkers (proteinuria, hypertension) of renal injury persist
  - **1A**: no evidence of renal injury ("recovery") (Ch 300)

#### Oliguria / Anuria Definitions
- Normal UOP: **1–2 mL/kg/hr** in healthy dogs and cats.
- Relative oliguria: UOP **<2 mL/kg/hr** in a pet receiving IV fluids.
- Absolute oliguria: UOP **<1 mL/kg/hr**.
- Anuria: **0–0.5 mL/kg/hr**.
- Oligoanuric AKI mortality: up to 20-fold increased risk of death vs. polyuric AKI. (Ch 300)

#### Uroabdomen Diagnosis (Table 300.6)
| Criterion | Significance |
|---|---|
| Effusion creatinine ≥2× serum creatinine | Diagnostic for uroabdomen |
| Effusion creatinine >1 but <2× serum creatinine | Suggestive; imaging required for confirmation |
| Effusion K > serum K | Suggestive; higher ratio = stronger suspicion |

#### Drug Doses in AKI (Table 300.7)

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Amlodipine** | 0.3–1 mg/kg PO q24h | Loading dose: 0.25 mg/kg PO/rectally hourly until response (max cumulative 1 mg/kg) |
| **Calcium gluconate** (10% solution) | 0.5–1 mL/kg IV to effect | Emergency cardiac arrhythmia from hyperkalemia only; does NOT change serum K |
| **Capromorelin** | 3 mg/kg PO q24h | — |
| **Famotidine** | 1 mg/kg IV bolus, then 8 mg/kg/day IV CRI | Use if GI ulcers identified/suspected (NOT for prevention) |
| **Maropitant** | 1 mg/kg IV, SQ; 2 mg/kg PO q24h | — |
| **Metoclopramide** | 1–2 mg/kg/day IV CRI | — |
| **Mirtazapine** | Dog: 1–2 mg/kg PO q12h; Cat: 1.8 mg PO q24–48h or 2 mg transdermal q24–48h | Decrease frequency to q48h in azotemic cats; transdermal may not require dose frequency reduction |
| **Omeprazole/Esomeprazole** | 1 mg/kg IV q12h | Use if GI ulcers identified/suspected (NOT for prevention) |
| **Ondansetron** | 0.5–1 mg/kg IV, SQ, PO q8h | — |
| **Regular insulin** | 0.5 U/kg IV + 1–2 g dextrose/U of insulin | For hyperkalemia; deliver dextrose over 4–6 h; monitor for hypoglycemia |

(Ch 300)

#### Hyperkalemia Management in AKI
- Calcium gluconate (10% solution) 0.5–1 mL/kg IV: transiently restores cardiac action potentials (effects ~20–30 min only; serum K still needs correction).
- Regular insulin 0.5 U/kg IV + dextrose: shift K intracellularly, ~1 mmol/L/hr decrease.
- Albuterol (nebulization, high doses): shift K intracellularly, ~0.5–0.75 mmol/L decrease within 60 min.
- **Sodium bicarbonate**: NOT recommended as sole K-lowering agent; may dilute K via hypertonicity rather than translocation.
- Furosemide + NaCl 0.9% + fludrocortisone: may enhance K excretion in urine-producing pets.
- Na polystyrene sulfonate, newer agents (Na zirconium cyclosilicate, patiromer): not evaluated in animals. (Ch 300)

#### Converting Oligoanuria to Polyuria
- **Mannitol**: IV 0.25–1 g/kg over 15–20 min; if response, repeat q4–6h or as CRI 60–120 mg/kg/hr. Contraindicated if overhydrated. No evidence supports benefit in established AKI.
- **Furosemide**: IV bolus/IM 1–4 mg/kg; if response, repeat q6–12h or CRI. Dogs/cats failing to respond to bolus unlikely to respond to CRI.
- **Dopamine** (renal-dose): NOT currently recommended; no benefit demonstrated.
- **Fenoldopam**: retrospective study showed no improvement in survival or hospitalization. (Ch 300)

#### Leptospirosis-Associated AKI (Table 300.4 detail)
- AKI often with thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, and ↑ liver enzyme activities.
- Associated with more kaluresis and glucosuria than other AKI causes.
- Pulmonary hemorrhage may be present.
- May cause PU/PD without azotemia (nephrogenic DI).
- Fanconi syndrome and proximal RTA reported in dogs.
- Urine PCR: 100% sensitivity, 88% specificity; blood/urine PCR falsely negative days after starting antibiotics.
- Serologic titers only 50% sensitive in dogs with AKI.
- Treatment: antibiotics and supportive care for AKI. (Ch 300)

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — Leptospirosis-associated AKI as a separate entity (candidate for DIS-INF-LEPTO or merged into DIS-SEC-AKI with sub-cause).

#### Nephrotoxin Highlights
- **Ethylene glycol**: lethal dose 1.4 mL/kg cats, 4.4 mL/kg dogs. Treatment: **4-methylpyrazole (fomepizole)** for dogs (prevents metabolism; treatment of choice). IV ethanol: alternative (competitive inhibition). Both ineffective once renal damage has occurred. Hemodialysis removes ethylene glycol.
- **Lily**: cats only. Phase 1 (1 h post): ataxic/"drunk"; Phase 2: asymptomatic; Phase 3 (1–3 days): oliguric AKI. Only 9% of cats with presumed lily exposure became azotemic after gastric decontamination + 48 h IV fluids.
- **Grapes/raisins**: cause unknown (tartaric acid suspected). Amount inducing AKI: 2.8–19.6 g/kg; ~35% of dogs ingesting this amount did not develop azotemia. Survival 53%.
- **Aminoglycosides**: toxicosis ~5–10 days post-start; BUN/creatinine rise; monitor trough levels and daily USG/sediment.
- **Amphotericin B**: newer lipid-based formulations less toxic; largely supplanted by other antifungals except severe fungal infections. (Ch 300)

#### Systemic Hypertension in AKI
- Found in 37–55% at admission, up to 75–81% during hospitalization.
- **Amlodipine**: effective; can be given rectally if needed.
- **ACE-I or ARBs**: avoid in AKI due to effect on GFR. (Ch 300)

#### Metabolic Acidosis in AKI
- Consider IV Na bicarbonate if venous pH **<7.2** and serum bicarbonate **<12 mEq/L** after correcting hypoperfusion and hyperlactatemia. Give slowly to prevent paradoxical CNS acidosis. (Ch 300)

### Ch 301 — Chronic Kidney Disease (IRIS Staging)
*(Author: Jessica M. Quimby)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Chronic Kidney Disease/Uraemic Vomiting (DIS-SEC-CKD) — enrichments: COMPLETE IRIS staging table (creatinine + SDMA, dog + cat, all substages), phosphate targets per stage, drug doses (mirtazapine, capromorelin, darbepoetin, calcitriol, amlodipine, telmisartan), survival/MST per stage.

#### Prevalence
- Dogs: 0.5–3.74% in primary care practice.
- Cats: 1.2–3.6% primary care; one study found 80% prevalence in cats, rising with age; 37.5% even in 0–4.9-year-old cats.
- CKD most common cause of death in cats >5 years; dogs ≥12 years have 5.5× greater risk than 7–12-year-olds. (Ch 301)

#### IRIS Staging Notes
- Based on minimum **2 fasting, well-hydrated** serum creatinine values, weeks apart.
- Reduced muscle mass (sarcopenia) may substantially reduce creatinine relative to true GFR → SDMA can modify staging in these cases. (Ch 301)

#### CKD-Mineral Bone Disorders (CKD-MBD)

**Serum Phosphorus Therapeutic Goals (Table 301.2)**

|---|---|---|
| I | No recommendation | — |
| II | 2.5–4.5 | 0.81–1.45 |
| III | 2.5–5.0 | 0.81–1.61 |
| IV | 2.5–6.0 | 0.81–1.94 |

*Maintaining PO4 <4.5 mg/dL (<1.45 mmol/L) for Stage 2 and <5.0 mg/dL (<1.6 mmol/L) for Stage 3 improves survival.* (Ch 301)

**Phosphate Binders (Table 301.3)**
| Binder | Dosage |
|---|---|
| **Aluminum hydroxide** | 30–90 mg/kg/day† PO |
| **Lanthanum carbonate** | 12.5–25 mg/kg/day† PO |
| **Chitosan + calcium carbonate (Epakitin)** | 4.4 g/10 kg† PO |
| **Sevelamer carbonate** | 30–135 mg/kg† PO |

*Al hydroxide toxicosis reported at 126–200 mg/kg/day via feeding tube. Ca-based binders contraindicated in hypercalcemia. Lanthanum: effective, minimal systemic absorption, well tolerated by cats. Sevelamer carbonate preferred over sevelamer HCl (less acidosis).* (Ch 301)

**Calcitriol**
- Dose: **2.0–2.5 ng/kg daily**; do not exceed **5 ng/kg/day**.
- Give on empty stomach (food increases Ca absorption and hyperCa risk).
- Monitor iCa, PO4, PTH; contraindicated if PO4 or iCa elevated.
- Contraindication: hyperCa; caution with nephroliths.
- Half-life <1 day; rapid offset when stopped.

**Cinacalcet (calcimimetic):** 0.5 mg/kg once daily; increase every 2–3 weeks; max 3 mg/kg. (Ch 301)

#### Arterial Hypertension in CKD

**Treatment Targets:** Reduce systolic BP **<150 mm Hg**. Therapy when BP consistently **>160 mm Hg**. Urgent when **>200 mm Hg** or TOD present.

**Antihypertensive Drugs:**
- **Amlodipine** (Ca channel blocker): effective monotherapy in cats; indicated with TOD or BP >200 mm Hg.
- **Telmisartan** (ARB): effective antihypertensive in cats; monitor BP at 3–6 months and decrease dose if hypotension develops. Has not been assessed with TOD or BP >200 mm Hg.
- ACE inhibitors: NOT demonstrated to adequately reduce BP in cats as monotherapy.
- Dogs: monotherapy usually insufficient; combination of ACE-I, ARB, and Ca channel blocker typically used. (Ch 301)

#### Proteinuria Management in CKD
- Standard of care: treat dogs and cats with CKD + UPCR **>0.5** (dogs) or **>0.4** (cats) with ACE-I or ARB.
- Telmisartan dose for proteinuria: **1 mg/kg PO q24h** starting dose; up-titrate up to **3 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- Enalapril/benazepril dog: start **0.5 mg/kg PO q24h or q12h**; up to **1.5 mg/kg PO q12h**.
- Benazepril cat: **0.5–1 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- In azotemic animals: start at 1/4–1/2 of typical starting doses.
- Dual RAAS therapy (ACE-I + ARB): not advisable unless life-threatening hypoalbuminemia unresponsive to monotherapy.
- **Kidney biopsy** for proteinuric CKD: consider if UPCR >3.5 or if unresponsive to standard therapy. (Ch 301)

#### Nausea, Vomiting, Appetite Stimulation

**Antiemetics:**
- **Maropitant**: 1–2 mg/kg/day PO or 1 mg/kg/day SQ/IV in CKD dogs/cats. Oral 4 mg/cat/day effective in IRIS CKD Stage II–III cats.
- **Ondansetron**: 0.5–1 mg/kg SQ (parenteral has better bioavailability and longer half-life in cats than oral).
- **Metoclopramide**: prokinetic effects; weak antiemetic.

**Appetite Stimulants:**
- **Mirtazapine (oral)**: cats **1.87 mg PO q48h** in CKD (renally excreted → reduced dose); dogs **0.6–1 mg/kg PO q12h** (shorter half-life in dogs).
- **Mirtazapine transdermal (Mirataz)**: **2 mg applied to inner ear pinna q24h** (FDA approved); efficacy documented; cats with CKD do not appear to require less frequent dosing.
- **Capromorelin (ghrelin agonist)**:
  - Cats (Elura): **2 mg/kg PO q24h** → significant weight increase; contraindicated in acromegaly; caution in diabetes.
  - Dogs (Entyce): **3 mg/kg PO q24h** → increases appetite and weight. (Ch 301)

**Gastric Acid Suppressants:** Prophylactic use NOT recommended in IRIS Stage I–III CKD. Only give if clinical suspicion of GI ulceration. If needed: omeprazole 1 mg/kg q12h is more effective than famotidine; tolerance to famotidine develops quickly at q12h. (Ch 301)

#### Hypokalemia in CKD
- **Prevalence:** ~20–30% of cats with CKD; more common in IRIS Stages 2 and 3.
- **Oral supplementation:**
  - K gluconate: **1–4 mEq/cat/day PO** (divided as needed).
  - K citrate: **40–75 mg/kg PO q12h** (~2–6 mEq/cat/day); also alkalinizing.
  - **KCl NOT recommended** orally (acidifying, unpalatable).
  - KCl in SQ fluids: up to 30 mEq/L; in IV maintenance up to 30 mEq/L (concentrations of 13–20 mEq/L for maintenance IV).
  - **IV K maximum rate: 0.5 mEq/kg/hr** (Ch 301)
- K goal: maintain serum **>4 mEq/L**.
- Severe hypoK (<2.5 mEq/L): cervical ventroflexion, plantigrade stance in cats.
- Moderate hypoK (2.5–3.0 mEq/L): weakness, lethargy, inappetence, constipation. (Ch 301)

#### Anemia of CKD (ESAs)

**Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents:**
- Preferred: **Darbepoetin alfa** (Aranesp) over epoetin alfa (lower immunogenicity, weekly dosing).
- Indication: IRIS Stage III–IV CKD, PCV <25%, clinical signs attributable to anemia.
- **Darbepoetin dose:** Initial **1 mcg/kg SQ weekly** until target PCV reached.
  - Target PCV: **cats 28–35%; dogs 37–42%**.
  - Once target reached: decrease to **q2 weeks**, then adjust monthly.
- Epoetin alfa: 25–30% of cats, up to 50% of dogs develop neutralizing anti-EPO antibodies (cross-react with all ESAs → PRCA).
- Darbepoetin: much less immunogenic; anti-EPO antibody risk appears lower.
- **Iron supplementation:** start with ESA initiation; repeat q few months.
  - Iron dextran: **50 mg/cat IM; 10–20 mg/kg dog IM** (well tolerated; oral iron poorly absorbed and bitter).
- Response: 56% of cats achieved target PCV (25%); responders: mean 238 days survival; non-responders: 83 days. (Ch 301)

#### Metabolic Acidosis in CKD
- ~15% of cats in IRIS Stage III, ~53% in IRIS Stage IV.
- Treatment threshold: blood bicarbonate **<16 mmol/L** (goal 16–24 mmol/L). Parenteral therapy: pH <7.10 → target >7.20.
- **K citrate:** starting **40–60 mg/kg PO q8–12h** (preferred; provides K and alkalinization).
- **Na bicarbonate:** initial **8–12 mg/kg PO q8–12h** (less palatable; Na load potentially activates RAAS).
- Reassess after 10–14 days. (Ch 301)

#### Prognosis — Median Survival Times (MST)

**Cats:**
- IRIS CKD Stage IIb (creatinine 2.3–2.8 mg/dL): MST **1151 days** (95% CI: 1014–1565 days).
- IRIS CKD Stage III: MST **679 days** (95% CI: 445–910 days).
- IRIS CKD Stage IV: MST **35 days** (95% CI: 21–99 days).

**Dogs (first-opinion practice, n=228):**
- Overall MST from diagnosis: **226 days** (95% CI: 112–326 days).
- Stage III vs I/II: 2.6× higher mortality.
- Stage IV vs I/II: 4.7× higher mortality.
- Smaller study (n=27): Stage II MST 14.8 months (9.1–37.4), Stage III 11.14 months (0.46–38.4), Stage IV 1.98 months (0.32–12.8).

**Prognostic factors:**
- Cats: phosphorus, FGF23, proteinuria, anemia, body weight, uremic toxins.
- Dogs: phosphorus, FGF23, proteinuria, hypertension, low BCS, low muscle mass, Ca×PO4 product, uremic toxins. (Ch 301)

#### SQ Fluid Therapy Guidelines
- Indication: cats prone to dehydration/constipation who subjectively benefit.
- Volume: **75–150 mL SQ every 1–3 days** depending on IRIS stage and clinical need.
- Less often needed in dogs. (Ch 301)

#### Kidney Transplantation (Cats)
- Ideal candidates: stable late Stage 3 or early Stage 4 CKD.
- Immunosuppression: cyclosporine microemulsified (Neoral 100 mg/mL) lifelong + prednisolone tapered.
- Survival: hospital discharge 77.5–93%; 84% at 30 days; 65–76% at 6 months; 40% at 3 years; MST 613–653 days. (Ch 301)

### Ch 302 — Glomerular Diseases (Protein-Losing Nephropathy)
*(Authors: Shelly Vaden, Bianca Lourenço)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — Glomerular Disease/Protein-Losing Nephropathy as a distinct disease entity. Candidate: DIS-SEC-PLN or DIS-URO-GLOM.
> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — Renal Amyloidosis. Candidate: DIS-URO-AMYLOID.

#### Epidemiology
- Glomerular lesions prevalence in randomly selected dogs: up to 43–90%.
- Most common glomerular diseases (dogs, 663 biopsied): ICGN (48.1%), amyloidosis (15.2%), FSGS (20.6%).
- Cats: ICGN most common (58% of biopsied, 74% of those with glomerular disease).
- Mean age of dogs with glomerular disease: 8.3 years; NS at ~6.2 years. (Ch 302)

#### Diagnostic Thresholds
- UPC **>0.5 dog** or **>0.4 cat** in inactive sediment = abnormal.
- UPC **>2.0** in dogs: suggestive of glomerular disease; UPC >3.5: consider biopsy.
- Nephrotic syndrome: hypoalbuminemia + proteinuria + hypercholesterolemia + edema/ascites; found in only 15% of dogs with GN; incomplete NS (without edema) in 49%.
- Hypoalbuminemia: 60% of dogs with GN, 70% of dogs with amyloidosis.
- Hypercholesterolemia: 79% of dogs with GN, 86% with amyloidosis, 56% of cats with various glomerular diseases. (Ch 302)

#### Biopsy Processing (Minimum Requirements)
- Minimum **5 glomeruli** by light microscopy; 1 may suffice for diffuse disease (e.g., amyloidosis).
- Must include **TEM** (transmission electron microscopy) and **IFM** (immunofluorescent microscopy) to determine immune-complex presence.
- Sample for IFM: freeze or immerse in Michel's solution.
- Minimum 2 quality cortical samples each **>10 mm long**; 16- or 18-gauge needle. (Ch 302)

#### Glomerular Disease Types

**MPGN (Membranoproliferative GN):**
- 26% of 89 canine biopsies; familial in Bernese Mountain Dogs.
- Rapidly progressive fatal form associated with Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme) in dogs (Labs/Goldens predisposed; mean age 5.6 years).
- NS in 36% of dogs with MPGN. (Ch 302)

**Membranous Nephropathy (MN):**
- 26% of thoroughly evaluated biopsies (most common in cats: followed by MPGN).
- Male:female ~1.75:1 dogs, ~6:1 cats; mean age dogs 6.8 years, cats 3.6 years.
- NS in 38% of dogs with MN. Spontaneous remission in 20–30% of humans.
- Cats: 17% survived 4–10 months, 33% long-term (2.5–6 years), 29% clinical remission. (Ch 302)

**Amyloidosis:**
- 7–15% of glomerular lesions in dogs; 58% died or euthanized at diagnosis.
- Survival (remaining): 2–20 months; only 8.5% survived ≥1 year.
- **Colchicine:** 0.01–0.03 mg/kg PO q24h. Effective in pre-deposition phase (Shar-Pei with fever/swollen hocks). No benefit once persistent azotemia or hypoalbuminemia present. GI upset = primary side-effect.
- **DMSO:** 90 mg/kg PO or SQ 3× weekly; dilute 1:4 with sterile water for injection; controversial benefit; unpleasant smell causing noncompliance. (Ch 302)

**Hereditary Nephritis (HN):**
- English Cocker Spaniel (autosomal recessive, COL4A4): azotemia progresses in first/second years of life.
- Samoyed (X-linked dominant, COL4A5): proteinuria detectable ≤3 months; death ~1 year in males; females progress slowly.
- Bull Terrier, Dalmatian (autosomal dominant): presentation highly variable; some survive 10 years.
- No specific therapy; standard PLN therapy beneficial; early microalbuminuria screening recommended. (Ch 302)

**FSGS (Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis):**
- 20–33% of thoroughly evaluated biopsies; females overrepresented (1.5:1).
- Most not azotemic or severely hypoalbuminemic at biopsy; median systolic BP 153.5 mm Hg.
- Median survival post-biopsy: **258 days** (range 26–1003 days). (Ch 302)

**Minimal Change Disease (MCD):**
- Uncommon in dogs/cats; diagnosed in 11/162 dogs in one study.
- **TEM required** for diagnosis (underdiagnosed).
- Expected response to corticosteroids 70–90% in humans. (Ch 302)

#### Standard Therapy of Glomerular Disease

**Anti-Proteinuric Drugs (RAAS Antagonists):**
- **Telmisartan** (ARB, preferred by authors): starting **1 mg/kg PO q24h** dogs or cats; up-titrate to max **3 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- **Enalapril/benazepril** (ACE-I): starting **0.5 mg/kg PO q24h or q12h** dogs; benazepril cats **0.5–1 mg/kg PO q24h**.
- Azotemic animals: start at **1/4–1/2 of standard dose**.
- Therapeutic target: UPC **<0.5** (ideal) or **>50% reduction from baseline** (alternate target).
- Dual ACE-I + ARB therapy: avoid unless life-threatening hypoalbuminemia; associated with worsening azotemia and AKI.
- Severe hyperkalemia (>6.5 mmol/L): substitute ACE-I with ARB. (Ch 302)

**Antithrombotic Therapy** (thromboembolism risk):
- Low-dose **aspirin**: 0.5–5 mg/kg PO daily; inconsistent platelet inhibition; inexpensive.
- **Clopidogrel**: 1.1–3 mg/kg PO q24h; more reliable platelet inhibition; widely available.
- **Warfarin**: NOT recommended in hypoalbuminemic dogs.
- Low molecular weight heparin (dalteparin), direct Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban): used in confirmed thromboembolism. (Ch 302)

**Omega-3 Fatty Acids:** Renoprotective in dogs; reduces serum triglycerides and cholesterol in NS. (Ch 302)

**Immunosuppressive Therapy for ICGN (dogs):**
- Consider when: source clearly glomerular, not familial nephropathy or amyloidosis, serum creatinine **>3.0 mg/dL** (>265 mcmol/L) or progressively increasing, OR serum albumin **<2.0 g/dL** (<20 g/L).
- Rapid-onset protocol (high magnitude proteinuria, NS, rapidly progressive azotemia): **mycophenolate OR cyclophosphamide** ± short-term glucocorticoids.
- Stable/slowly progressive: **mycophenolate, chlorambucil, or cyclophosphamide** (cyclosporine studied prospectively — NO benefit in dogs).
- Treat 8–12 weeks before switching; taper if response achieved. Discontinue if no response at 3–4 months.
- Glucocorticoids: limit to short-term only (corticosteroid excess promotes proteinuria). (Ch 302)

**Response Definitions:**
- **Complete response**: UPC <0.5 OR creatinine <1.4 mg/dL (<124 mcmol/L) OR sustained albumin >2.5 g/dL (>25 g/L).
- **Partial response**: >50% UPC reduction OR >25% creatinine reduction OR >50% albumin reduction from baseline, OR albumin increase to >2.0–2.5 g/dL (20–25 g/L). (Ch 302)

#### Prognosis (Glomerular Disease)
- Median survival (53 dogs with GN or amyloidosis, surviving >immediate): **28 days** (some >3 years).
- With NS: median 12.5 days vs. without NS: 104.5 days.
- Non-azotemic dogs with NS: 51 days; without NS: 605 days.
- Hypertension in glomerular disease: up to 80% dogs, ~60% cats. (Ch 302)

### Ch 303 — Renal Tubular Diseases
*(Author: Marie E. Kerl)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Primary Renal Glucosuria/Fanconi (DIS-ENDO-RENGLUC) — enrichments: Fanconi treatment protocol (Gonto), 2-MPG dose, cystinuria/RTA detail, NDI treatment.

#### Cystinuria
- Inherited proximal tubular defect; failure of resorption of nonessential amino acids (cystine, lysine, glycine, ornithine, arginine).
- Cystine calculi form in acidic urine.
- Types: Type 1 (autosomal recessive); Type III (sex-limited, intact males, genetic defect unknown).
- Common breeds: English Bulldog, Staffordshire Bull Terrier, Chihuahua, Newfoundland, Dachshund.
- Mean age at diagnosis: 4.9 years; Newfoundland/Labs: as early as 4–6 months.
- **Treatment:**
  - Protein-restricted diet + urinary alkalization (target urine pH 7.8 reduces cystine solubility 2×).
  - **2-Mercaptopropionylglycine (tiopronin [Thiola]): 30 mg/kg PO q12h** — treatment of choice.
  - D-penicillamine: effective but metal chelation and GI side-effects make it less desirable.
  - K citrate for urinary alkalization if diet alone insufficient.
  - Induce diuresis: canned food or added water to dry food. (Ch 303)

#### Hyperuricosuria (Dalmatian + others)
- Dalmatians: active distal tubular secretion of urates (autosomal recessive Slc2a9 mutation); also in English Bulldogs, Black Russian Terriers.
- 25% of male Dalmatians clinically affected.
- **Allopurinol** (XO inhibitor): reduces serum and urine uric acid. Must be given WITH purine-restricted diet to avoid xanthine stone formation. NOT for cats; NOT for dogs with hepatic urate defects.
- **Urine alkalization**: target pH 7.0–7.5 (NaHCO3 or K-citrate).
- Purine-restricted calculolytic diet (e.g., Hill's u/d).
- Average time to dissolution of urate calculi: ~3.5 months (range 1–18 months). (Ch 303)

#### Renal Glucosuria
- Primary (inherited): Scottish Terriers, Basenjis, Norwegian Elkhounds. Persistent glucosuria → osmotic diuresis/PU/PD.
- Can be initial sign of Fanconi syndrome.
- No cure; long-term prognosis good with adequate fluid intake and UTI control. (Ch 303)

#### Fanconi Syndrome

**Inherited:** Basenjis (10–30% prevalence; genetic testing available since 2011). Rare in other breeds and Irish Wolfhounds.

**Acquired:** Gentamicin, acute tubular necrosis toxins, primary hypoparathyroidism, Chinese chicken jerky treats, chlorambucil in cats.

**Pathophysiology:** Abnormal fractional excretion of glucose, amino acids, HCO3−, Na, K, phosphate, urate. Isosthenuria may precede glucosuria (osmotic diuresis from nephrogenic DI).

**Clinical Features (Basenjis):** Onset 4–8 years; PU/PD, weight loss, poor hair coat, weakness, dehydration. Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and renal failure in advanced disease.

**Treatment (Gonto Protocol):**
- Alkalization goals: serum HCO3− **>12 mEq/L**, K **4–6 mEq/L**.
- **K-citrate** (preferred over NaHCO3): avoids bicarbonaturia while providing K.
- Monitor for metabolic acidosis, UTIs, azotemia, systemic hypertension.
- Treat CKD and hypertension per standard guidelines.
- **Prognosis:** Median survival time 5.25 years (range 7 days to 9.8 years) from diagnosis; 86% owners reported good/excellent quality of life. (Ch 303)

#### Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)

**Table 303.1 — Proximal vs. Distal RTA Features**

| Feature | Proximal RTA (Type II) | Distal RTA (Type I/Classic) |
|---|---|---|
| Hypercalciuria | Yes | Yes |
| Hyperphosphaturia | Yes | Yes |
| Urinary citrate | Normal | Decreased |
| Bone disease | Less severe | More severe |
| Nephrocalcinosis | No | Possible |
| Nephrolithiasis | Not usually | Yes |
| Hypokalemia | Mild | Mild to severe |
| Potassium wasting | Worsened by alkali therapy | Improved by alkali therapy |
| Alkali required | >11 mEq/kg/day | <4 mEq/kg/day |
| FE of HCO3− at normal serum HCO3− | >15% | <15% |
| Urine pH during acidemia | <6.0 | >6.0 |
| Urine pH after NH4Cl challenge | <6.0 | >6.0 |

**Proximal RTA treatment:** K-citrate preferred. One 540 mg tablet of K-citrate provides 5 mEq K and 1.7 mEq citrate (metabolizes to 5 mEq HCO3−). (Ch 303)

**Distal RTA treatment:** K- and Na-citrate **70–75 mg/kg PO q12h** (preferred over HCO3−).
- Ammonium chloride challenge: 100 mg/kg PO (max 3000 mg) once; measure urine pH before and hourly × 6 h. Normal: dogs acidify to pH 5.0; cats to pH 5.5. (Ch 303)

**Distal RTA reported in:** Cats with pyelonephritis; cats with hepatic lipidosis; dogs with IMHA (3), leptospirosis (1), zonisamide (1). (Ch 303)

#### Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus (NDI)
**Normal urine:** >1 mOsm/kg concentrated.
**Congenital NDI:** USG 1.001–1.005; osmolarity <200 mOsm/kg (profoundly hyposthenuric).
**Acquired NDI causes:** E. coli endotoxin, glucocorticoids, chemotherapeutics, hypokalemia, hypercalcemia, bacterial pyelonephritis, medullary washout.

**Treatment (congenital):** Free-choice water + dietary Na and protein restriction + thiazide diuretics:
- **Chlorothiazide:** 20–40 mg/kg PO q12h
- **Hydrochlorothiazide:** 2 mg/kg PO q12h
→ Achieve 20–50% reduction of urine output. (Ch 303)

### Ch 304 — Pyelonephritis
*(Author: Ewan Wolff)*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Pyelonephritis (DIS-URO-PYELO) — enrichments: ISCAID 2019 guidelines, antibiotic choice/duration, renal pelvic dilation thresholds, prognosis by IRIS stage.

#### Etiology
- Always classified as a **complex UTI**.
- Most common pathogens: E. coli (58% of complex UTI pyelonephritis in dogs), Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Proteus.
- Proteus mirabilis: associated with only 2/39 cats.
- Organisms tend to be more resistant to TMP-sulfa and amoxicillin-clavulanate.
- Fungal (rare): Cryptococcus, Cladophialophora bantiana reported. (Ch 304)

#### Clinical Signs
- Anorexia (57%), lethargy (51%), diarrhea (36%), dehydration (28%), vomiting (17%), fever (21%) in one report.
- 75% of dogs had likely underlying causes: immunosuppression (38%), epithelial inflammation (30%), urinary obstruction (19%). (Ch 304)

#### Diagnosis
- AKI defined by ↑Cr **≥0.3 mg/dL** (≥27 mcmol/L) above baseline.
- Minimally concentrated urine: USG **1.012–1.018** with active sediment.
- Pyelectasia on US: 66% of dogs with pyelonephritis; ureteral dilation: 41%.
- Renal pelvic dilation thresholds:
  - Pyelonephritis dogs: median **3.6 mm**; cats: **4.0 mm**.
  - Physiologic dilation dogs: **2 mm**; cats: **1.6 mm**.
  - Obstructive dilation dogs: median **15.1 mm**; cats: **6.8 mm**.
  - Dilation **>1.3 mm**: non-specific for pyelonephritis vs. obstruction.
  - Dilation **>13 mm**: suggestive of obstruction.
- Urine culture: >90% positive in people; obtain by cystocentesis or US-guided pyelocentesis. (Ch 304)

#### Treatment
**ISCAID 2019 Guidelines:**
- Immediate antibiotic therapy: **fluoroquinolone** OR **2nd/3rd generation IV cephalosporin**.
- Ascending infection: based on **urine culture**.
- Hematogenous origin: based on **blood culture**.
- Oral treatment if pet able; extrapolation from human data.
- Recommended treatment duration: **10–14 days** (ISCAID).
- Note: ASVNU consensus suggests 2–3+ weeks based on clinical circumstances and attending clinician discretion.
- Adjust to culture and sensitivity. (Ch 304)

**Acute Management:**
- IV antibiotics empirically for intrinsic renal AKI until culture results available.
- IV fluids (judicious use).
- Manage AKI complications (BP, K, Ca, anemia, vomiting, dehydration).
- Hold ACE-I/ARB during acute AKI phase; may restart when values improve. (Ch 304)

#### Prognosis
- Dogs with acute-on-CKD pyelonephritis (creatinine 1.4–2.8 mg/dL): two-thirds survived; median all-cause survival **130 days**; only 13% survived to 1 year.
- Cats with acute-on-CKD pyelonephritis: one-half survived; median all-cause survival **233 days** for IRIS Stage II CKD cats.
- No association between creatinine at presentation and long-term survival. (Ch 304)

### Ch 305 — Familial and Congenital Renal Diseases of Cats and Dogs
*(Author: Gilad Segev)*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING — Polycystic Kidney Disease / Familial/Congenital Nephropathies as a disease group. Candidate: DIS-URO-PKD and/or DIS-URO-FAMRENAL.

#### Key Definitions
- **Congenital:** present at birth OR develops later due to a defect present at birth; not necessarily inherited.
- **Familial:** high prevalence in specific breeds, early onset, suspected inherited basis.
- **Hereditary nephropathy:** inheritance pattern determined.
- **Juvenile onset CKD:** clinical term for CKD at young age (<5 years); not proof of congenital disorder. (Ch 305)

#### Staging and Management
- Same IRIS CKD staging/substaging as acquired CKD (serum creatinine or SDMA; UPC substage; BP substage).
- Treatment guidelines parallel acquired CKD (Ch 301).
- Dogs/cats with congenital CKD more resilient to azotemia and may show no clinical signs until very late (e.g., Stage 4 with no signs). Once diagnosed, do not delay management.
- Survival often longer vs. acquired CKD at same stage when diagnosed early. (Ch 305)

#### Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD) — Cats

**Genetics:** Autosomal dominant; PKD1 gene exon 29 C→A transversion → premature stop codon. No homozygous cats identified (likely embryonic lethal). Worldwide prevalence in Persian cats: ~40%.

**Diagnosis:**
- US: cortical/medullary hypoechoic to anechoic cysts as early as 7 weeks of age; absence at 6 months correlates with absence of PKD.
- Genetic testing available and recommended.
- Hepatic and pancreatic cysts in some cats.

**Clinical:** Renomegaly on PE (vs. small kidneys in acquired CKD). Gradual function decline. Clinical signs appear when function declines.

**Management:** Same as acquired CKD (Ch 301). Address hyperphosphatemia, proteinuria, hypertension.

**Infected cysts:** Suspected if fever/leukocytosis + hyperechoic cyst on US. Aspirate, culture, inject local antibiotic; risk of septic peritonitis.

**Elimination from breeding:** Genetic testing; remove affected animals. (Ch 305)

#### PKD — Dogs
- Bull Terrier (AD, PKD1 mutation), Cairn Terrier (AR), West Highland White Terrier (AR).
- Bull Terriers: decreased kidney function by a few years; cysts limited to kidneys.
- Cairn/WHWT: clinical signs by few months of age; cysts in kidneys and liver. (Ch 305)

#### Hereditary Nephritis — Breed Details

**Samoyeds (X-linked dominant, COL4A5):**
- Proteinuria detectable in males as early as 3 months.
- Azotemia progresses over months → death at ~1 year of age in males.
- Carrier females: proteinuria early; azotemia apparent only at a few years of age.

**English Cocker Spaniels (AR, COL4A4):**
- Proteinuria detectable few months of age.
- Azotemia progresses over 1st and 2nd years.

**Bull Terriers + Dalmatians (AD):**
- Presentation highly variable; azotemia can be apparent from months to 7–8 years.
- IFM shows normal type IV collagen pattern → TEM required for diagnosis.

**Diagnosis:** Kidney biopsy (LM + IFM + TEM). Immunostaining of GBM demonstrates abnormal type IV collagen (except Bull Terriers/Dalmatians). Genetic testing available for some types. (Ch 305)

#### Fanconi Syndrome in Basenjis — Clinical
- Onset 4–8 years; no sex predilection.
- Incidence ≥10%; genetic testing since 2011.
- Isosthenuria may precede glucosuria.
- Advanced disease: hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, renal failure.
- MST post-diagnosis: 5.25 years (range 7 days to 9.8 years); 86% good/excellent QoL. (Ch 305)
