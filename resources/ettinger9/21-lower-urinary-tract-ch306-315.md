### Ch 306 — Ureteral Disorders
*Berent AC, Adams LG. PDF pp. 2530–2536*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Ureteral Obstruction / SUB / Ureterolith)

#### Ureteral Anatomy (Key Facts)
- Normal ureteral luminal diameter: dog <2.7 mm; cat only 0.3–0.4 mm
- Even ureteroliths <1–2 mm fail to pass in most cats
- Intramural ureter has j-hook configuration at trigone; overdistension of bladder impairs retrograde guidewire access

#### Ureteral Obstruction — Clinical Signs
- Unilateral obstruction often subclinical unless contralateral kidney impaired
- Complete bilateral obstruction may be fatal within 48–72 hours
- Signs: ureteral colic (inconsistent vs humans), dysuria (especially distal in cats), anorexia, vomiting, oliguria, azotemia, hyperkalemia

#### Imaging Diagnosis
- Abdominal ultrasound + radiographs: preferred primary tools
- Renal pelvic dilation >13 mm reliably associated with obstruction; however obstruction confirmed with pelvic dilation <4 mm in many cats
- Ureteral strictures present in ~17–25% of cats with concurrent ureteroliths (often missed on US)
- US sensitivity for ureterolithiasis: 98% (recent study); earlier studies lower — sonographer skill critical
- Ureteral stricture: more common than reported; often missed sonographically

#### Ureterolith Composition
- Dogs and cats: calcium oxalate most common in upper tract (majority of cases); not amenable to dissolution without stent
- Struvite ureteroliths in dogs: second most common in upper tract; associated with urease-producing UTI; may be dissolved after stent placed

#### Medical Expulsive Therapy (MET) for Ureteroliths
- Alpha-adrenergic antagonists:
  - **Prazosin** (cat): **0.25–0.5 mg/cat PO q8–12h**
  - **Prazosin** (dog): **1 mg/15 kg PO q8h**
  - **Tamsulosin** (dog): **0.004–0.006 mg/kg PO q12–24h** (may take 3 days to be effective)
- IV fluids + mannitol diuresis adjunct
- MET only if: no UTI, no severe obstruction, minimal azotemia, no oliguria, no hyperkalemia, no severe hydronephrosis, no sepsis
- In cats: MET success only ~13%; pass rate ~8%. Authors limit MET to 24–48 h in cats
- If ureterolith not moving distally after 2 weeks → intervention

#### Interventional Options
| Intervention | Dog | Cat |
|---|---|---|
| Ureteral stent | First-line; retrograde via cystoscopy; ~95% success | Difficult; antegrade via nephropyelocentesis; long-term dysuria ~38% |
| SUB (subcutaneous ureteral bypass) | Available as backup | **Preferred** over stent in cats; 90–94% survival to discharge |
| SWL (shock wave lithotripsy) | ~80% success; 2+ treatments needed in 50% | **Contraindicated** — feline CaOx resistant; even <1 mm fragments fail to pass |
| Ureterotomy/ureteroneocystostomy | Only if single stone, no stricture, no concurrent nephroliths | Same; higher mortality than minimally invasive |

#### Struvite Ureterolith Protocol (Dogs)
1. Correct obstruction first (stent + renal pelvis lavage via cystoscopy)
2. Then dissolution: antimicrobials guided by culture + struvite dissolution diet (ch. 159, 308)
3. Continue diet + antibiotics 2–4 weeks beyond radiographic/US confirmation of complete dissolution
4. Remove stent by cystoscopy once dissolved; monitor for recurrent UTI

#### SUB Device
- Surgical placement; nephrostomy tube + SC port + cystostomy catheter bypasses obstructed ureter
- Cat outcomes correlate to residual CKD stage post-obstruction: stage 1–2 CKD → significantly longer survival vs stage 3–4
- SUB exchange required in ~15–20% due to encrustation/occlusion (lower rate than stent exchange for cats)
- >30% of cats have ureteral stricture as sole cause — not amenable to ureterotomy → SUB preferred

#### Prognosis
- Long-term prognosis correlates with CKD stage at discharge
- Retroperitoneal fibrosis: reported in 21% of feline renal transplant recipients; treated surgically in most

### Ch 307 — Lower Urinary Tract Infections
*Wood M. PDF pp. 2537–2540*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Bacterial Cystitis / UTI)

#### Bacteriology
- Dogs: E. coli 45–55%; Proteus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella 2–14% each; Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Enterococcus 2–14% each; Gram-negative >60%
- Cats: E. coli 37.3%; Enterococcus faecalis 27%; Gram-positive and -negative ~equal; overall bacteriuria incidence much lower than dogs
- Spayed female dogs: 2× risk vs intact males

#### ISCAID Definitions (Weese et al., Vet J. 2019;247:8–25)
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sporadic bacterial cystitis** | Clinical bladder infection <3 times in a 12-month period, regardless of sex/neuter/comorbidities |
| **Recurrent bacterial cystitis** | ≥3 documented clinical infections within 12 months |
| **Subclinical bacteriuria (SCB)** | Bacteriuria without clinical signs (preferred over "asymptomatic bacteriuria" in vet medicine) |
| **Persistence** | Appropriate antimicrobial fails to sterilize urine (resistance, immune compromise, subtherapeutic MUC) |
| **Relapse** | Urine sterilized then recolonized by same organism from tissue reservoir (kidney, prostate, uroliths, vagina) within days–weeks |
| **Reinfection** | New bacterial strain colonizes weeks–months after prior UTI; host defense alteration |

#### Culture Thresholds
- Cystocentesis: gold standard
- Voided: **>100,000 CFU/mL** → 94% sensitivity and specificity for true bacteriuria
- Catheter-collected: quantitative colony counts required
- Urine stored at room temperature: bacteria proliferate or die within 1 hour; refrigerate at 4°C for 2–24 h

#### Treatment — Sporadic Bacterial Cystitis
- **First-line antibiotics**: amoxicillin, cephalexin, or trimethoprim-sulfonamide
  - Local resistance rate should be <~10% for sporadic cases
- **Duration**: **3–5 days** (current ISCAID recommendation)
  - Evidence: TMP-SMX or high-dose enrofloxacin for 3 days = equivalent cure to 10 days cephalexin or 14 days amoxicillin-clavulanic acid
- Fluoroquinolones and cefovecin: **NOT recommended** for sporadic cystitis (reserve for complicated/culture-guided use)
- NSAIDs as sole therapy: **not recommended** (pyelonephritis risk; no vet evidence)
- Post-treatment monitoring not needed for sporadic uncomplicated cases

#### Treatment — Recurrent Bacterial Cystitis
- Full systemic evaluation: external genitalia conformation, voiding assessment, post-void residual volume (normal ~0.1–0.4 mL/kg)
- Antibiotic selection guided by culture and sensitivity (MUC must be ≥4× MIC)
- Persistent infections: adjust dose or change antibiotic to maximize urinary concentration; evaluate GI absorption, renal function, hepatic metabolism
- Relapsing infections: tissue-penetrating antibiotic concentrations (plasma levels, not MUC); **7–14 days to 4–6 weeks** recommended
- Reinfection: culture-guided; short courses adequate (no prolonged treatment needed)

#### Prevention
- Subclinical bacteriuria (SCB): generally do not treat; vigilant monitoring
- Adherence blockade (cranberry PAC, D-mannose, GAGs): limited/no proven in vivo benefit in dogs/cats
- Long-term modified-dose antibiotic prophylaxis (one-third to one-half daily dose, nightly after last void, for 6 months; monthly cultures): evidence limited; risk of multidrug resistance
- Bacterial interference (E. coli 83972, E. coli 2-12): promising but no clinical recommendations yet
- Methenamine salts (bacteriostatic, converts to formaldehyde at pH <5.5): no veterinary recommendations; avoid with urease-positive UTI or CKD

### Ch 308 — Lower Urinary Tract Urolithiasis in Dogs
*Defarges A, Furrow E. PDF pp. 2541–2552*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Urolithiasis Canine)

#### Urolith Prevalence (Minnesota Urolith Center 2020 submissions)
| Type | % of submissions | Major mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Struvite | 40–43% | UTI with urease-producing bacteria (Staphylococcus, Proteus) → ↑ ammonium + alkaline pH |
| Calcium oxalate (CaOx) | 35–42% | Idiopathic hypercalciuria (normocalcemia); genetic predisposition |
| Cystine | 6% | Androgen-dependent cystinuria (Type III, 70–90% intact males); SLC3A1/SLC7A9 mutations |
| Urate | 5% | Portosystemic shunt; hereditary hyperuricosuria (HHU; SLC2A9 mutation — Dalmatian, Black Russian Terrier, English Bulldog, >30 breeds) |
| Calcium phosphate | 1% | Alkaline urine + hypercalciuria; primary hyperparathyroidism |
| Silica | <1% | High dietary silica (corn gluten, rice hulls); German Shepherd predisposed |
| Xanthine | 0.1% | Usually iatrogenic (allopurinol therapy); XDH deficiency |

#### Age/Sex Composition Shifts
- Dogs ≥7 years: 60% CaOx, 35% struvite, 9% urate, 1% cystine
- Dogs <7 years: 58% struvite, 26% CaOx, 25% urate, 5% cystine
- Female dogs: 74% of uroliths = struvite (UTI predisposition)
- Male dogs: 73% = CaOx; intact males: ↑ cystine risk

#### Breed Predispositions
- CaOx: Miniature Schnauzer, Yorkshire Terrier, Shih Tzu, Bichon Frisé, Lhasa Apso
- Urate/HHU: Dalmatian (>90%), Black Russian Terrier, English Bulldog
- Cystine androgen-dependent: Mastiff, Scottish Deerhound, English/French Bulldog, Rottweiler, Basset Hound, Irish Terrier
- Cystine Type I/II: Newfoundland, Australian Cattle Dog, Labrador Retriever, Miniature Pinscher
- Silica: German Shepherd Dog

#### Diagnostic Approach
- Radiography: preferred; >90% CaOx and struvite visible; only 70–75% urate/cystine (radiolucent)
- Struvite radiographic features: maximum diameter >10 mm, ovoid, or pyramidal shape → >90% probability struvite; rosette shape → 95% CaOx
- Ultrasound: more sensitive for ≤1 mm; limited for urethral stones (pelvic superimposition); overestimates cystolith size
- Urolith analysis: **quantitative (optical crystallography or infrared spectroscopy)**; submit uroliths dry; submit all uroliths from each recurrence

#### Full Urolith Management Table
| Urolith Type | Dissolution Protocol | Prevention | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Struvite** | Antimicrobial (guided by culture) + therapeutic dissolution diet; check radiographs q4–6 weeks; 50–60% success; median ~1 month | Prevent/eradicate UTI; q3-month urine cultures if recurrent UTI history | Contraindications: urethral obstruction, comorbidity preventing therapeutic diet |
| **Calcium oxalate** | **Cannot be dissolved** | Low-oxalate, moderate-Ca diet; target USG ≤1.020; potassium citrate 75 mg/kg PO q12–24h if urine pH <6.5; hydrochlorothiazide 2 mg/kg PO q12h for recurrent formers | Rule out hypercalcemia before hydrochlorothiazide; avoid cranberry in K-citrate supplements |
| **Cystine** | Low animal protein diet + tiopronin **20 mg/kg PO q12h**; 60% success, median 2 months; potassium citrate 75 mg/kg PO q12h; castration (androgen-dependent Type III) | Castration resolves androgen-dependent form (cystine-to-creatinine ratio returns normal in 2–4 months); tiopronin 15 mg/kg PO q12h for persistent cystinuria post-castration; target urine pH 7.5 | Tiopronin side effects: aggression, myopathy, proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, skin lesions |
| **Urate (HHU)** | Low-purine alkalinizing diet + allopurinol **15 mg/kg PO q12h**; 30–40% full dissolution; median ~1 month; failures often due to xanthine formation | Low-purine diet; potassium citrate if urine pH acidic (target pH ≥7); allopurinol **5–7 mg/kg PO q12–24h** (lower prevention dose); correct PSS surgically | Allopurinol must be combined with dietary purine restriction (risk of xanthine uroliths from XDH inhibition) |
| **Silica** | No dissolution | Low-silica diet (avoid corn gluten feed, rice hulls, intact grains); increase moisture | — |

#### Dissolution Monitoring
- Struvite: radiographs q4–6 weeks; no dissolution by 4–6 weeks → non-struvite composition likely
- Continue antimicrobials until full dissolution OR discontinue and repeat urine cultures q4–6 weeks
- Post-dissolution: continue diet 4 weeks beyond radiographic resolution

#### Urolith Removal Options (when dissolution inappropriate or failed)
1. **Voiding urohydropropulsion (VUH)**: general anesthesia, bladder filled with saline, manual expression vertical; suitable for smooth stones <5 mm in dogs >8 kg; best for female dogs; 10 mL/kg max saline
2. Cystoscopic basket retrieval
3. Laser lithotripsy
4. Percutaneous cystolithotomy (PCCL)
5. Cystotomy (last resort; risk of suture-induced recurrence — 9% of recurrences)

#### Prevention Monitoring
- Urinalysis: q1 month, then q3–6 months until target pH/concentration achieved
- Bladder imaging: q3–6 months, then q6–12 months
- **Target USG ≤1.020** for all types

### Ch 309 — Lower Urinary Tract Urolithiasis — Feline
*Bourque C, Labato MA. PDF pp. 2553–2558*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Urolithiasis Feline / FLUTD)

#### Feline Urolith Composition (Minnesota Urolith Center 2020, n=18,073)
| Type | % |
|---|---|
| Struvite | 52% |
| Calcium oxalate | 36% |
| Purine (urate) | 6% |
| Compound | 4% |
| Mixed | 1% |
| Other | 1% |
| Calcium phosphate | <1% |
| Cystine | <1% |

#### Key Differences from Canine
- **Feline struvite is typically STERILE** (unlike dogs); only ~11% of submitted feline struvite stones culture-positive
- **No sex predilection for infection-induced struvite** in cats (contrast to dogs)
- Calcium oxalate: middle-aged to older, male neutered cats; Burmese, Himalayan, Persian predisposed

#### Calcium Oxalate (Cat)
- **Cannot be dissolved medically**
- Dietary management: high-moisture diet (≥73.3% moisture) significantly reduces CaOx RSS; dry food → ~30% less water intake
- **Goal: USG <1.030** (cats); acidifying diets ↑ CaOx risk — avoid; aim urine pH ↑ (reduces CaOx saturation but risks struvite)
- Cats fed diet producing urine pH <6.2: **3× greater risk** of CaOx compared to pH 6.5–6.9
- One-third of cats with CaOx stones had idiopathic hypercalcemia → measure serum ionized calcium
- **Potassium citrate** (urinary alkalinizer): **50–75 mg/kg q12h PO**, titrated until urinary pH = 7.0; monitor serum K, bicarbonate, renal values, urine pH q7–14 days
- **Hydrochlorothiazide**: **1 mg/kg q12h PO** (significant decrease in urinary CaOx RSS in healthy cats); efficacy/safety data currently limited
- Recurrence probability: up to ~7%; recheck imaging 4 weeks post-removal, then 3 and 6 months

#### Struvite (Cat)
- Sterile struvite: dissolution in **1–4 weeks** with dissolution diet; some take up to 5 months
- Dissolution diet: acidifies urine (pH <7), reduces magnesium and phosphorus; acceptable brands include Hill's S/D, Purina UR, Royal Canin Urinary SO
- Abdominal radiographs: reevaluate 2–4 weeks; should be 33–100% smaller by then
- Continue diet 2–4 weeks beyond radiographic dissolution
- VUH: female cats and male cats post-perineal urethrostomy with low stone burden and stones <5 mm
- Stones >5 mm or high burden → **PCCL** (percutaneous cystolithotomy) recommended
- Concurrent UTI (rare in cats): antibiotic throughout dissolution

#### Urate / Purine (Cat)
- Surgical removal is treatment of choice (limited dissolution data)
- Prevention: protein-restricted alkalinizing diet (liver disease or kidney disease diets); canned food preferred
- Hepatic dysfunction workup required (PSS, microvascular dysplasia — uncommon in cats vs dogs)
- Breeds: Ocicat (higher risk); Siamese, Birman, Egyptian Mau historically overrepresented
- Allopurinol: **not recommended** until more feline safety data; xanthinuria risk

#### Cystine (Cat)
- Rare; SLC7A9 missense mutation (c.881T>A); reported in Maine Coon, Sphynx, DMH
- Type III (androgen-dependent) not yet confirmed in cats; consider castration in intact males
- Treatment: ↑ water intake, reduce protein, **potassium citrate 75 mg/kg PO q12h** to achieve urine pH >7.5; cystine-binding drugs (tiopronin) used in humans; feline data limited
- Neurological signs (arginine deficiency): hypersalivation, lethargy, behavior changes, seizures possible

#### Stone Removal (Feline)
- VUH: <3 mm diameter, female cats (or male after perineal urethrostomy); not recommended for large stone burden, stones >3 mm, or intact male cats
- Cystoscopic basket retrieval or laser lithotripsy for small number/urethroliths
- PCCL: male cats or when VUH/cystoscopy inadequate
- Pre-procedure urine culture: always within 2 weeks

#### Dried Solidified Blood (DSB) Stones
- 1.8% of submissions; predominantly male cats (84.7%); Bengals, Persians, Domestic Longhair at risk
- Hematuria thought to be predisposing factor; pathogenesis not well understood

### Ch 310 — Diseases of Abnormal Micturition
*Byron JK. PDF pp. 2559–2565*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Urinary Incontinence / USMI)

#### Peripheral Nervous System Micturition Components
| Type | Location | Nerve | Function stimulated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parasympathetic (M3) | Bladder body (detrusor) | Pelvic nerve (S1–S3) | Bladder contraction/emptying |
| Sympathetic (beta3) | Bladder body | Hypogastric nerve (L1–L4) | Detrusor relaxation/bladder filling |
| Sympathetic (alpha1) | Bladder neck/urethra | Hypogastric nerve (L1–L4) | Urethral contraction/continence |
| Somatic (nicotinic) | Distal urethra/pelvic floor | Pudendal nerve (S1–S2) | Conscious reflex contraction/continence |

#### USMI (Urethral Sphincter Mechanism Incompetence)
- Most common cause of urinary incontinence in dogs
- Affects up to **20% of spayed female dogs**, particularly large breeds (>20 kg)
- Breeds: Doberman Pinscher, Giant Schnauzer, Old English Sheepdog, Rottweiler, Weimaraner, Boxer
- Spaying before 3 months of age: ↑ risk; the larger the adult dog, the higher the risk of UIC if spayed early → delay spay to sexual maturity in large breeds where possible
- Less common in castrated males; rare in intact males and cats

#### USMI Medical Treatment (Table 310.2)

| Drug | Class | Dose | Side-effects | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phenylpropanolamine (PPA)** (Proin) | Alpha-agonist | **2 mg/kg PO q12h** | Hypertension, aggression, restlessness, GI upset, anxiety | Hypertension, hyperadrenocorticism, kidney disease |
| **Diethylstilbestrol (DES)** | Estrogen | **0.1–1 mg/dog PO q24h × 3–5 days, then 1–3× weekly as needed** | Myelosuppression (rare at these doses), attraction to males, mammary/vulvar swelling | Do not use in males (prostatic metaplasia) |
| **Estriol** (Incurin) | Estrogen | **2 mg/dog PO q24h × 14 days, then reduce to 1 mg q24h** | Same as DES | Same as DES |
| **Testosterone cypionate** | Androgen | **2.2 mg/kg IM q4–8 weeks** | Behavior change, aggression, perianal adenoma, prostatic hyperplasia | Cardiac, kidney, hepatic, prostatic disease |

- Estriol response rate: **89%** vs DES **65%** in female dogs
- PPA response: **75–90%** in female dogs; ~43% in male dogs
- Combined PPA + estrogen: anecdotally superior for severe/refractory cases

#### USMI Surgical Options
- Colposuspension, trans-obturator vaginal tape, urethropexy: variable/poor long-term outcomes
- **Artificial urethral sphincter (AUS)** with SC port: most promising; significant continence improvement in males and females after medical failure
- Periurethral bulking agents (bovine cross-linked collagen, cross-linked gelatin): minimally invasive; median continence 17 months

| **Phenoxybenzamine** | Dog: **0.25 mg/kg PO q8–12h**; Cat: **1.25–7.5 mg/cat q8–12h PO** | Non-specific alpha antagonism |

#### Functional Urethral Outflow Obstruction (FOO / Detrusor Urethral Dyssynergy)
- Primarily young-to-middle-aged, large/giant breed male dogs (mean age 4.9 years in one series of 22)
- Attenuated urine stream, large residual urine volume (normal: 0.1–3.4 mL/kg; use <1 mL/kg guideline)
- Easy catheterization (unlike mechanical obstruction); contrast urethrography normal or shows urethrospasm
- Treatment: prazosin (alpha1-specific) ± tamsulosin; benzodiazepines or acepromazine if striated muscle involved; diazepam 30 min before voiding
- Prognosis: good but most require **lifelong therapy**; relapses with stressful events

#### LMN vs UMN Bladder
- LMN (S1–S3 lesion): easily expressed bladder, ↓ anal tone, poor perineal reflex; manual expression or catheterization 2–4×/day
- UMN (cranial to sacrum): bladder difficult to express; overflow incontinence; manage with alpha-adrenergic blockade + catheterization until spinal recovery

#### Cats
- 53% of feline urinary incontinence due to voiding disorders; spinal cord disease most common (trauma); unfavorable outcome more likely than other causes

### Ch 311 — Feline Idiopathic/Interstitial Cystitis (FIC)
*Buffington CA, Delgado MM. PDF pp. 2566–2573*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — FIC / FLUTD / Pandora Syndrome)

#### Overview
- FIC: most common cause of chronic lower urinary tract signs (cLUTS) in domestic cats
- Presents with dysuria, stranguria, pollakiuria, hematuria, periuria
- Pandora syndrome: term describing multiple comorbid health problems associated with FIC (not just bladder)
- Etiopathogenesis: persistent activation of central threat response system (CTRS); NOT primarily a bladder disorder
- 20–60% of male feline urethral obstruction cases due to mucous/crystalline matrix plugs (associated with FIC)

#### Epidemiology / Natural History
- Of 101 cats with cLUTS (Kaul et al. 2020): 52 (52%) diagnosed with FIC, 21 with urolithiasis, 13 with UTI, 15 undetermined
- Recurrence rate: **58%** across all LUTS diagnoses
- Mortality due to LUTS among all 101 cats: 5%
- Episodes of LUTS in individual cats can have **different etiologies at different episodes** (28% of recurring cats)

#### Diagnosis — Criteria
- **Diagnosis of exclusion**: no sensitive/specific test available
- Based on: signalment + history including environmental risk factors + exclusion of other LUTS causes + response to MEMO
- **Key rule-outs**:
  1. Urolithiasis (next most common; ~20% of LUTS cats have bladder stones on radiographs)
  2. UTI (culture only if bacteriuria + clinical signs on cystocentesis sample)
  3. Neoplasia
  4. Anatomical abnormalities
- Urinalysis + urine culture: recommended at least once; most young healthy cats do NOT have true bacterial cystitis
- Abdominal radiographs: if MEMO fails or signs recur — detect radiopaque stones
- Do NOT recommend antimicrobials unless positive cystocentesis culture with compatible clinical signs
- Do NOT culture urine after urinary catheter removal in obstructive FIC unless clinical signs return

#### Pharmacological Treatment
- **No medications proven effective** in treatment or prevention of FIC (prednisolone, chloramphenicol, pentosan polysulfate, propantheline, meloxicam, prazosin — none proven in controlled trials)
- **Analgesia** (e.g., **buprenorphine**): critical for acute clinical signs (welfare concern)
- Robenacoxib: safer for cats; no FIC efficacy data
- SSRIs/tricyclic antidepressants: may be appropriate for cats with anxiety/frequent recurrence (amitriptyline anecdotal; no controlled trials demonstrating efficacy for FIC per se)
- Synthetic pheromone analogue (Feliway): no statistically significant benefit in pilot study (n=9)
- Nutraceuticals, CBD: no studies demonstrating efficacy

#### MEMO — Multimodal Environmental Modification
**Primary treatment; most important intervention**

Goals: decrease cat's perception of threat; increase sense of control

Key MEMO elements:
- **Litter boxes**: ≥N+1 boxes (N = number of cats); separate locations; scoop ≥1×/day; large, open, accessible; unscented clumping litter preferred; not in closets/basements
- **Resources**: separate food/water bowls per cat; not visible to other cats; away from appliances
- **Play and predatory behavior**: daily interactive play; rotating toys; vertical space (climbing structures)
- **Safe space**: retreat areas where no person/animal can ambush cat; vantage points
- **Social**: assess cat–human and cat–cat interactions; avoid punishment; positive reinforcement
- **Routine**: predictable schedule; introduce changes as choices (not forced replacements)
- **Safe outdoor access** (harness, catio, stroller) where feasible

MEMO evidence: environmental changes reduce severity and frequency of FIC episodes (Buffington et al. 2006); most cats with periuria in shelter adopted successfully after litter substrate trial.

#### Prazosin for Urethral Spasm (FIC-associated obstruction)
- Prazosin used for urethral spasm in male cats with obstructive FIC: see Ch 310 dose (0.25–0.5 mg/cat PO q8–12h)
- Smooth muscle relaxation to relieve/prevent recurrent obstruction

#### Prognosis
- Good if owners receive effective support and environmental guidance
- Recurrence common but MEMO reduces severity and frequency
- Overall LUTS mortality ~5%

### Ch 312 — Urethral Disorders
*Byron JK. PDF pp. 2574–2577*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Urethral Obstruction / Urethrolith / Stricture)

#### Key Conditions

**USMI** — See Ch 310 (most common urethral disorder in dogs); affects up to 20% of spayed females

**Urethrolithiasis**
- Any urolith type can lodge in urethra; male dogs most common site: narrowed portion at proximal os penis
- Male cats: 20–30% of urethral obstructions due to uroliths
- Management: minimally invasive preferred — retrograde flushing into bladder + cystotomy/PCCL, or laser lithotripsy + basket retrieval via urethroscopy

**Feline Urethral Plugs**
- 20–60% of male feline urethral obstruction from mucous/crystalline matrix plugs (FIC-associated)
- May be flushed into bladder during unblocking or passed through catheter; gentle massage of sedated penis may expel plug

**Urethral Stricture**
- Causes: trauma, iatrogenic (catheterization, surgery), urethrolithiasis erosion, neoplasia
- Diagnosis: cystourethrogram with fluoroscopy, radiographs, or CT; cystoscopy
- Treatment: balloon dilation (urethroscopic or fluoroscopic guidance), laser (diode or holmium:YAG), or urethral stent
- Recurrence rate: ~70%; stent → median 843 days to relapse; balloon dilation alone → 452 days; medical only → 101 days

**Functional Outflow Obstruction (FOO)** — See Ch 310

**Proliferative Urethritis**
- Circumferential proliferation in urethra; lymphoplasmacytic/neutrophilic/eosinophilic/histiocytic; predominantly female dogs
- >50% positive for urethral bacteria by FISH (even culture-negative)
- Clinical signs: stranguria, pollakiuria, incontinence, abdominal pain; bladder often distended
- Diagnosis: cystoscopy + histopathology (rule out TCC)
- Treatment: antibiotic (if UTI) + immunosuppressives + anti-inflammatories ± urethral stenting; recurrence ~70%

**Urethral Neoplasia**
- Rare primary; more commonly extension from bladder TCC or prostatic adenocarcinoma
- Palliative urethral stenting: treatment of choice for obstruction

**Urethral Prolapse**
- Primarily male dogs; English Bulldogs (77%), Yorkshire Terriers (4%); intact males overrepresented
- Treatment: surgical resection + anastomosis or urethropexy; recurrence 38% (sedated with barriers) to 71% (without)

### Ch 313 — Congenital Lower Urinary Tract Disorders
*Owen L. PDF pp. 2578–2587*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Ectopic Ureter)

#### Ectopic Ureters (EU)

**Signalment / Breeds**
- Dogs: Golden Retriever, Labrador Retriever, Newfoundland, Siberian Husky, Poodle, Soft Coated Wheaten Terrier; hereditary confirmed in Entlebucher Mountain Dog
- Females more commonly clinically affected (shorter urethra); mean age dogs <6 months (but lifelong differential)
- Cats: no breed predisposition; mean age at presentation 1.4 years; majority extramural (vs intramural in dogs)

**Types**
- Intramural: 86–100% of dog cases; ureter tunnels submucosally through bladder/urethral wall before opening distal to trigone
- Extramural: majority of cats; ureter bypasses bladder entirely; inserts into urethra, vagina, or uterus

**Concurrent Abnormalities** (commonly associated)
- Vestibulovaginal septal remnants (PPMR) — present in 93% of dogs with EU in one study
- Ureterocele, urethral hypoplasia, renal dysplasia/hypoplasia/aplasia, bladder hypoplasia

**Clinical Features**
- Primary: urinary incontinence (continuous to intermittent dripping); wet fur around vulva/perineum
- Recurrent UTIs in up to 83% of bitches, 36% of cats with EU (usually E. coli)
- Hematuria, stranguria, pollakiuria from concurrent UTI or ureterocele obstruction

**Diagnosis**
- Imaging method of choice: **cystoscopy** in bitches (100% sensitivity), **CTEU** in male dogs and cats
- Ultrasound: 95% diagnostic accuracy in recent study; useful initial screening; combined with color Doppler ± furosemide (1 mg/kg IV/SQ)
- CTEU (4D-CTEU): 97% sensitivity, 94.6% specificity; ventral recumbency preferred; post-processing enhances detection
- Urine culture: treat UTIs before correction where possible

**Treatment**
- Intramural EU: **preferred — transurethral cystoscopic-guided laser ablation** (minimally invasive, low morbidity); cystoscopic scissor transection comparable efficacy where laser unavailable
- Surgical correction (neoureterostomy intravesicular or extravesicular reimplantation): effective but ↑ morbidity vs laser
- Extramural EU: **surgical only** (ureteral reimplantation)
- Ureteronephrectomy: only for severe unilateral hydronephrosis/pyelonephritis

**Outcome / Post-treatment**
- Persistent incontinence: 35–63% of female dogs after EU correction — largely attributed to concurrent USMI
- Medical management if ureteral openings confirmed correctly positioned:
  - **Phenylpropanolamine: 0.8 mg/kg PO q8h OR 1.2 mg/kg PO q12h OR 1.5 mg/kg PO q24h**
  - Duration: until first estrus, or lifelong if persistent
- Delay surgical procedures until after ≥1 estrous cycle (urethral resistance/bladder capacity increase documented over first 2 cycles in immature Beagles)
- AUS after failed laser ablation: 80% continence in bitches
- Male dogs: significantly better long-term outcome than females regardless of treatment; 100% continence in 4 males treated by laser; 82% in 16 males treated surgically

#### Ureteroceles
- Cystic dilation of submucosal distal ureter; orthotopic (in bladder) or ectopic (in urethra)
- Treatment of choice: **endoscopic-guided laser ablation** (safe, effective, low morbidity)
- Surgical resection ± neoureterocystostomy: alternative with similar results

#### Urachal Anomalies
- Patent urachus: open bladder–umbilicus communication → urine scald, omphalitis, ascending UTI
- Urachal cyst: isolated epithelium-lined section retaining fluid
- Vesicourachal diverticulum: most common (96.7% cats, 89.5% dogs in one study); persistent communication with bladder apex; outpouching of epithelial tissue
- Macro diverticulae with recurrent UTIs: surgical resection may benefit (weak evidence); micro diverticulae: usually clinically silent

#### Congenital USMI / Urethral Hypoplasia
- May self-resolve as urogenital tract matures post-estrus
- Medical management: alpha-1 adrenergic agonists (see Ch 310)
- Urethral lengthening procedures: described for severe hypoplasia; effective in 8 female cats and 1 dog

### Ch 314 — Prostatic Diseases
*Zambelli D, Cunto M. PDF pp. 2588–2596*

> ClinIQ: ✗ MISSING (candidate — Prostatic Disease: BPH / Prostatitis / Neoplasia)

#### Frequency of Prostatic Disorders in Dogs (Box 314.1)
| Condition | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) | 45% |
| Acute or chronic prostatitis | 38.6% |
| Abscesses | 7.7% |
| Cysts | 5% |
| Neoplasia | 2.6% |
| Squamous metaplasia | 0.2% |

- 75% of prostate diseases subclinical
- Cats: extremely rare; few case reports of prostatitis, abscess, squamous metaplasia, cysts, neoplasia
- Large breeds overrepresented (except prostatic neoplasia)

#### Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

**Prevalence**
- Uncommon in dogs castrated young
- Gross/microscopic BPH: ~80% of intact males >5 years; >95% >9 years
- Large breed predisposed: Doberman Pinscher, German Shepherd, Rhodesian Ridgeback, Labrador Retriever
- Subclinical BPH: ~45% of dogs with subclinical prostatic disorders

**Clinical Signs (Box 314.2 — BPH)**
- Blood dripping from prepuce (increased vascularization) +++
- Hematospermia (1st and 3rd ejaculate fraction), hematuria ++
- Subfertility +
- With large prostate/large cysts: tenesmus, thin tape-shaped feces, constipation, dyschezia ++; dysuria, urinary incontinence +

**Diagnosis**
- Rectal exam: large, symmetrical, mobile, normally firm prostate; cysts may give asymmetry/soft areas
- CPSE (canine prostatic specific esterase): >90 ng/mL when prostate >2.5× normal size; not differentiated between BPH/prostatitis/carcinoma
- Ultrasound: uniformly echogenic or nodular parenchyma; cysts with well-defined margins

**Treatment**
- **Watchful waiting**: for mild signs not affecting quality of life; q3–6 months monitoring
- **Castration**: definitive; prostatic volume decreases within **7–14 days**; always resolves BPH
- **Finasteride** (5-alpha-reductase inhibitor): **1.25 mg/day PO for 195 days** (lowest effective dose regardless of weight or severity); does not affect libido or sperm production
- **Osaterone acetate** (anti-androgen/progestogen): **0.2–0.5 mg/kg/day PO for 7 days**; significant prostate volume reduction; some semen quality changes (not likely to affect fertility)
- **Deslorelin acetate** (GnRH agonist implant, 4.7 mg): prostate volume >50% reduction at ~6 weeks; testosterone reduced up to 90%; semen quality decline day 22, inability to collect day 37; effect reversible — prostate returns to pre-treatment volume by ~48 weeks

#### Acute Prostatitis (AP)

**Clinical Signs**
- Fever, anorexia, lethargy, vomiting, caudal abdominal pain, difficulty rising, painful stiff gait, low sperm quality, poor libido, subfertility +++
- Hematospermia, pyospermia, hematuria, pyuria, urethral discharge ++
- Dysuria, tenesmus, peritonitis/septic shock +

**Causative Bacteria**
- E. coli, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Proteus, Pseudomonas, **Brucella canis**, Klebsiella
- Usually multiple organisms; single bacterium infection uncommon
- Definitive diagnosis: >10,000 bacteria/mL prostatic fluid culture; 2-log difference between prostatic and urine CFU counts consistent with bacterial prostatitis

**Treatment — Prostatitis**
- Antibiotics based on prostatic/urine culture + sensitivity + drug pharmacokinetics
- **Duration: 4–6 weeks for AP; 6–8 weeks for chronic prostatitis (CP) or prostatic abscess (PA)**
- Blood-prostate barrier intact in CP → only lipid-soluble, low-protein-bound, high-pKa drugs penetrate: **trimethoprim, clindamycin, chloramphenicol, fluoroquinolones** (zwitterion characteristic)
- In AP/PA: blood-prostate barrier often disrupted → broader antibiotic access
- Broad-spectrum empiric antibiotics while awaiting culture; culture 7–10 days after start, then 30 days after discontinuation
- **Castration**: strongly recommended; ideally **5–7 days after antibiotics started** (reduces need for prolonged antibiotic use)
- Supportive care: IV fluids, analgesics

#### Chronic Prostatitis (CP)
- Often subclinical +++
- Recurrent UTIs, low sperm quality, hypo/infertility ++
- Prostate firm, irregular surface; focal/diffuse echogenicity on US; mineralization possible
- Cytology: macrophages, plasma cells, lymphocytes; bacteria variable
- Treatment: same antibiotics as AP but **6–8 weeks**; castration recommended

#### Prostatic Abscess (PA)
- Often sequela to AP; can be primary
- Signs similar to AP + tenesmus/dysuria from compression
- Management: **antibiotics + castration** + drainage
- Drainage: omentalization is treatment of choice surgically; non-surgical US-guided percutaneous drainage (UGPD) followed by **percutaneous ethanol injection (PEI)** (to scar inner tissue, prevent recurrence); PEI effective for small subcapsular single cysts/abscesses not communicating with urethra; cysts less responsive to PEI than abscesses (epithelial lining)

#### Squamous Metaplasia (SM)
- Caused by estrogen excess (estrogen-secreting testicular tumor — Sertoli cell)
- Prostatic columnar cells → squamous cells → occlude glandular ducts → predispose to AP/PA
- Feminization syndrome: nonpruritic symmetric alopecia, skin hyperpigmentation, gynecomastia, penis sheath ptosis and erythema, attracting males +++
- Treatment: castration (removes estrogen source) if testicular tumor; reversible

#### Prostatic Neoplasia
- Most commonly adenocarcinoma and undifferentiated carcinoma; average age ~10 years
- **Higher prevalence in castrated males** (vs intact); not predisposed by BPH
- ~80% metastasized at diagnosis; common sites: vertebral bodies, lungs; may invade urethra, bladder, ureters, colon, pelvic musculature
- No pathognomonic signs; lameness, stiff gait, hind limb paresis, lethargy, weight loss, caudal abdominal pain +++
- Radiography: prostatomegaly, prostatic mineralization, sublumbar lymphadenopathy, ventral lumbar vertebral metastases
- **Prognosis: poor**; survival weeks to months; treatment rarely curative
- Total/subtotal prostatectomy: only for no-metastasis cases
- NSAIDs alone or + chemotherapy: can improve quality of life and significantly prolong survival

#### Prostatic Cysts
- Retention cysts: present in ~75% dogs with BPH; can communicate with urethra, contain urine, become infected
- Paraprostatic cysts (PPCs): remnants of uterus masculinus; single structures attached to prostatic capsule; can invade abdominal/pelvic cavity
- Classification: large ≥3 cm, medium >1 to <3 cm, small ≤1 cm, micro ≤1 mm
- Treatment: castration ± medical; surgical excision/debridement/omentalization/marsupialization for large cysts

### Ch 315 — Reproductive Disorders in Neutered Male and Female Dogs and Cats
*Davidson A. PDF pp. 2597–2604*

> ClinIQ: ✓ Pyometra (DIS-REPRO-PYO) partially covered; other entities ✗ MISSING

#### Urinary Incontinence in the Ovariectomized Bitch
- Common and predictable complication of OHE/OVE; not seen in queens
- Risk ↑ in large dogs >20 kg; timing of surgery ≤12 weeks does not additionally increase risk for UIC
- Important rule-outs: overflow incontinence (isosthenuria/hyposthenuria), UTI, undiagnosed ectopia, behavioral urination, genitourinary inflammatory/neoplastic disorders

#### Chronic Vestibulovaginitis
- Etiology multifactorial (Box 315.1): perivulvar dermatitis (redundant folds), granulomatous uterine stump, foreign bodies, chronic UTI, neoplasia, os clitoris/clitoral hyperplasia
- Antimicrobial: only when culture shows pathogen displacing normal flora (e.g., Pseudomonas)
- NSAIDs preferred over corticosteroids (latter ↑ UTI risk)
- **Tramadol: 4–10 mg/kg PO q8h** or **gabapentin: 10–20 mg/kg PO q8–12h** for analgesia
- Estrogen-responsive (idiopathic): **estriol 2 mg/bitch PO q24h × 14 days**, then taper to 1 mg daily × 7 days, 0.5 mg daily × 7 days, then 0.5 mg q48–72h as needed; **DES 0.1–1 mg/bitch PO q24h × 3–5 days**, then taper to lowest effective dose (typically 1–3 doses/week)

#### Exogenous Estrogen Toxicosis (Neutered Bitch or Queen)
- Sources: human transdermal HRT (most common), veterinary estrogen overdose for UIC
- Signs: attraction of males, vulvar swelling, mucoid/sanguineous discharge, flagging/copulation behavior
- Chronic: symmetric alopecia, myelosuppression, gynecomastia, lactation (bitch); estrus behavior (queen)
- Resolution in 2–4 weeks after discontinuing exogenous source

#### Ovarian Remnant Syndrome (ORS)
- ~17% of post-OVE/OHE complications
- Cyclical signs ~every 5–8 months in bitches; queens show estrus every 18 days if not ovulated
- Diagnosis: **AMH (anti-müllerian hormone)** — MOFA test; most reliable screening test; positive AMH (post-pubertal, >6 months) = ovarian tissue present; exogenous estrogen does NOT affect AMH (unlike LH tests)
- Serum progesterone >2 ng/mL (1–3 weeks after behavioral estrus) = functional luteal tissue
- US: remnant visible during follicular (anechoic cystic) or luteal phase (hypoechoic/isoechoic)
- Treatment: surgical exploratory + removal of all visible ovarian tissue; schedule during clinical signs when active follicles/CL present
- Post-op: cabergoline (Galastop) **2.5 mcg/kg PO q24h × 4–6 days** if profound pseudopregnancy signs

#### Squamous Prostatic Metaplasia (Neutered Dog)
- Due to hyperestrogenism (endogenous: retained cryptorchid testis with Sertoli/Leydig tumor; exogenous: owner transdermal HRT)
- Signs: enlarged firm prostate in neutered dog + compression signs (dysuria, tenesmus) + feminization signs (alopecia, gynecomastia, hyperpigmentation, pendulous prepuce, bone marrow toxicosis)
- Diagnosis: positive AMH → testicular tissue present; US abdominal testis; preputial swab cytology (squamous cells with >30% keratinization)
- Treatment: castration (cryptorchid) or remove exogenous source; treat concurrent infection/abscess (Ch 314)

#### Priapism (Neutered Male Dogs, Predominantly)
- Persistent penile erection without sexual stimulation; most common in neutered dogs; not testosterone-mediated
- Differentiate from paraphimosis (erect penis trapped outside prepuce)
- Ischemic (veno-occlusive, low-flow): → gangrene; prompt aspiration of corpora cavernosa ± irrigation under sedation/anesthesia; intracavernosal phenylephrine: **1–3 mcg/kg** (low starting dose, cardiovascular monitoring)
- Nonischemic (arterial, high-flow): can resolve spontaneously; conservative therapy: analgesia, lubrication, Elizabethan collar, phenothiazine sedation
- Systemic options: **gabapentin 10–20 mg/kg PO q8–12h**; **terbutaline 1.25–5 mg/dog PO q8–12h**; **ephedrine 1–2.5 mg/kg PO q12h** (if available)
- Penile amputation + perineal urethrostomy: if drainage/injections fail or significant tissue damage
